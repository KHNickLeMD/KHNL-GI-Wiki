Prophylactic antibiotics in [[cirrhosis|cirrhotic]] patients with [[upper-gi-bleeding|upper GI bleeding]] aim to prevent bacterial translocation-driven infections ([[spontaneous-bacterial-peritonitis|spontaneous bacterial peritonitis]], bacteremia) that independently worsen short-term mortality. In the AASLD framework this is a form of **SBP primary prophylaxis** ([[aasld-2021-ascites-sbp-hrs]]).

## Indications (AASLD 2021)

- **Acute GI hemorrhage** in cirrhosis → IV ceftriaxone until the hemorrhage has resolved (5–7 days) [[aasld-2021-ascites-sbp-hrs]]
- (Other SBP primary-prophylaxis indication, non-bleeding: low ascitic protein <1.5 g/dL **plus** advanced hepatic/renal dysfunction — see [[spontaneous-bacterial-peritonitis]])

## Standard Agents

- **Ceftriaxone** 1 g IV daily — preferred during acute GI hemorrhage, in Child-Pugh C / hospitalized patients, and in settings with high quinolone resistance; continue 5–7 days (until hemorrhage resolved) [[aasld-2021-ascites-sbp-hrs]]
- **Oral fluoroquinolone** — historically **norfloxacin** 400 mg PO BID, but norfloxacin was **withdrawn from the US market in 2014**; **ciprofloxacin** 500 mg/day is the oral fluoroquinolone used in the US, reserved for lower-risk patients in low-resistance settings [[aasld-2021-ascites-sbp-hrs]]

## Duration Controversy

Current guidelines recommend 5–7 days, but a 2025 systematic review ([[albhaisi-2025-prophylactic-antibiotics-cirrhosis]]) found the evidence supporting this duration is low quality. Shorter courses (2–3 days) may be non-inferior for mortality, though bacterial infection rates are 15.2% higher with shorter durations. An individualized approach is reasonable pending higher-quality RCTs.

See [[variceal-upper-gi-bleeding]] for full clinical context.
