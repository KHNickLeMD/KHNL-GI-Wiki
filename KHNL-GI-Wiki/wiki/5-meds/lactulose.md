## Mechanism

Nonabsorbable disaccharide, used for [[hepatic-encephalopathy|hepatic encephalopathy (HE)]] for decades. Mechanism is described as controversial ([[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]]); four proposed effects:

- **Acidifies the gut lumen** → traps ammonia as ammonium ion, preventing absorption
- **Laxative effect** → increases ammonia elimination in stool
- **Presumed prebiotic effect** → inhibits ammoniagenic bacteria, promotes acidophilic urease-lacking bacteria (lactobacilli) that produce less ammonia
- **Inhibits intestinal glutamine uptake** → decreased ammoniagenesis

[[aasld-easl-2014-hepatic-encephalopathy]] notes culture-independent studies have not borne out the prebiotic/acidifying claims beyond the laxative effect, and that a large meta-analysis did not completely support lactulose for overt HE (it excluded the largest trials for technical reasons); most recent trials are open-label. It remains the least expensive, most cost-effective HE treatment.

## Indications

| Setting | Statement | Grade as printed |
|---|---|---|
| **Overt HE — treatment** | Lactulose to improve patient outcomes and prevent recurrence of overt HE (OHE) episodes (American College of Gastroenterology [ACG] 2026 Rec 7) | Strong; moderate certainty |
| | Lactulose is the first choice for treatment of episodic OHE (2014 Rec 19) | Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) II-1, B, 1 |
| **Secondary prophylaxis after first OHE** | Lactulose titrated to 2–3 soft bowel movements daily as outpatient first-line therapy for prevention of HE recurrence (ACG 2026 Rec 10) | Strong; **high** certainty |
| | Lactulose for prevention of recurrent episodes of HE after the initial episode (2014 Rec 25) | GRADE II-1, A, 1 |
| **Minimal HE (MHE)/covert HE (CHE)** | Suggest treatment with lactulose vs no treatment (ACG 2026 Rec 3) | Conditional; low certainty |
| **MHE/CHE — treatment to prevent OHE** | Insufficient evidence to recommend for or against routine treatment (ACG 2026 Rec 4) | Insufficient evidence — **no recommendation** |
| **HE in [[acute-on-chronic-liver-failure\|acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF)]] / critical illness** | Lactulose orally or rectally, or polyethylene glycol (PEG) if at risk of ileus/abdominal distention (American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases [AASLD] 2024 Guidance Statement [GS] 8) | Guidance statement |
| **[[variceal-upper-gi-bleeding\|Acute variceal bleeding]]** | Lactulose orally or by enemas to prevent **or** treat overt HE by accelerating blood elimination from the gastrointestinal (GI) tract (Baveno VIII 5.22) | Level of evidence (LoE) 2; strength of recommendation (SoR) strong; 91% agreement |
| **Chronic idiopathic constipation (CIC)** | In adults with CIC who **fail or are intolerant to over-the-counter (OTC) therapies**, suggest lactulose over management without lactulose (American Gastroenterological Association [AGA]/ACG 2023 Rec 4) | Conditional; very low certainty |
| **Primary prophylaxis of OHE** | **Not required**, except in cirrhosis with a known high risk to develop HE (2014 Rec 12) | GRADE II-3, C, 2 |
| **Post-[[tips\|transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS)]] prophylaxis** | Routine prophylactic therapy (lactulose or rifaximin) is **not** recommended to prevent post-TIPS HE (2014 Rec 27) | GRADE III, B, 1 |

- The MHE/CHE position moved: the 2014 guideline held that treatment of MHE and CHE is not routinely recommended apart from case-by-case (Rec 29, GRADE II-2, B, 1); ACG 2026 now suggests treating. Follow ACG 2026.
- ACG 2026 makes a peri-TIPS recommendation for [[rifaximin]] only (start 14 days before elective TIPS, continue ≥6 months) — it makes no equivalent recommendation for lactulose.

## Dosing and Titration

### Overt HE — inpatient

| Route | Dose | Endpoint |
|---|---|---|
| **Oral** (grade 2 HE) | **10–20 g (15–30 mL) every 2 h** until the patient has **2 soft bowel movements (BMs)**, then reduce to **2–4 times per day** | Maintain **2–3 BMs daily** |
| **Nasogastric (NG) tube** (grade 3–4 HE, unable to swallow or at risk of aspiration) | Same dosing as oral | Same |
| **Enema** (same patients, with **preserved anal sphincter tone**) | **200 g lactulose or 300 mL lactulose solution in 700 mL water or normal saline**; retain **30–60 min**; repeat **every 4–6 h** | Until mental status improves |

*Doses: [[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]]. [[aasld-easl-2014-hepatic-encephalopathy]] gives a different induction schedule — **25 mL of syrup every 1–2 h** until at least two soft or loose BMs per day, then titrate to 2–3 BMs per day. Same maintenance endpoint; use the ACG 2026 induction dose.*

- Tap-water enemas: rapid access, wide availability — but acidifying agents (lactose, lactitol) were **superior to tap-water enemas** for acute HE ([[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]]); the 2014 guideline calls the only trial showing this underpowered.

### Outpatient secondary prophylaxis

- Dose and frequency titrated to **2–3 soft BMs daily** — the target that balances efficacy against diarrhea-associated hypovolemia ([[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]])
- Use the **[[bristol-stool-form-scale|Bristol Stool Scale]] together with bowel-movement frequency** for outpatient titration, to reduce readmissions (ACG 2026 Rec 11, *conditional, very low certainty*)

### Chronic idiopathic constipation

- **Initial dose 15 g daily**; titrate to symptom response and side effects; **no clear maximum dose** ([[aga-acg-2023-constipation]] Table 4)
- Bloating and flatulence may be limiting with preexisting symptoms or at higher doses
- Positioned **after** failure of or intolerance to OTC agents; PEG carries the strong recommendation in this setting — see [[chronic-idiopathic-constipation]]
- Estimated monthly cost <$50

## Titration Endpoint and Monitoring

*All from [[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]] unless marked.*

- **Inpatient:** ensure **2–3 BMs/day, stool consistency Bristol 3–4**; explicitly **avoid diarrhea and hypernatremia** (inpatient flowchart, Figure 8).
- **Outpatient, >3 loose BMs (Bristol ≥5) on lactulose** → cut lactulose back, or consider [[rifaximin]] monotherapy ± low-dose lactulose (Figure 10).
- **Bristol ≥5 *before* lactulose is started** → consider an alternative, nonlaxative therapy de novo, to protect adherence.
- **Intensive care unit (ICU), intubated/sedated:** titrate on BMs, because mental status cannot be assessed. With a fecal management system (rectal tube), **hold lactulose and other laxatives if >300 mL stool/day**. Monitor for hypernatremia; take active steps to prevent aspiration.
- **Serum ammonia should not be used to guide initiation or titration** (Key concept 14); in one series of 551 patients diagnosed with HE, 40% had normal ammonia levels and lactulose response did not differ between normal and elevated values.
- **MHE/CHE theragnostic trial:** when lactulose is started for covert disease, evaluate adherence and symptom improvement at **4–8 weeks** to decide whether to continue (Key concept 8).
- **Low serum sodium predicts poor response** — as sodium falls, HE incidence rises and the response rate to lactulose decreases ([[aasld-easl-2014-hepatic-encephalopathy]]).

## Nonresponse

- Improvement in mental status with first-line treatment is expected within **24–48 hours** ([[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]]).
- **Persistent delirium at 48 h despite goal BMs and addressed precipitants** = nonresponse → re-evaluate for other causes: exclude alternate neurologic diagnoses → search for undiagnosed precipitants (occult infection, bleeding, unreported sedatives) → cross-sectional imaging for spontaneous portosystemic shunts (ACG 2026 Figure 9). Lack of effect likewise prompts a search for unrecognized precipitants and competing causes of brain impairment in the 2014 guideline. Full pathway on [[hepatic-encephalopathy]].
- **Breakthrough HE in a patient prescribed lactulose is commonly nonadherence or dehydration** ([[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]]).

> [!pitfall] More lactulose does not fix nonresponse
> Lack of effect from smaller amounts of lactulose is not remedied by much larger doses — a named misconception; overuse can itself precipitate HE ([[aasld-easl-2014-hepatic-encephalopathy]]; complications in the table below).

## Adverse Effects and Failure Modes

**Common, generally mild** (secondary-prophylaxis setting): nausea, bloating, flatulence, diarrhea ([[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]]).

**Over-titration is the characteristic failure mode** — and it is self-defeating, because its consequences can become precipitants of HE recurrence ([[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]]):

| Complication | Note |
|---|---|
| Dehydration / diarrhea-associated hypovolemia | Named precipitant of HE recurrence |
| **Hypernatremia** | Monitor actively, especially in the ICU |
| **Hypokalemia** | Also listed with significant diarrhea in constipation ([[aga-acg-2023-constipation]] Table 4) |
| Aspiration | Risk is the reason for NG or enema routes in grade 3–4 HE |
| Perianal skin irritation / infection | 2014 guideline describes it as severe with overuse |
| Ileus, gas distension, abdominal discomfort | Trigger to consider PEG instead |

## Combination with Rifaximin

- **Acute OHE:** suggest **adding [[rifaximin]] to lactulose** vs lactulose alone (ACG 2026 Rec 9, *conditional, low certainty*). A randomized controlled trial (RCT) of the combination showed a higher rate of complete HE reversal, reduced hospital stay, and reduced mortality.
- **Recurrent episodes despite lactulose maintenance:** **add rifaximin** (ACG 2026 Rec 13, *strong, high certainty*). The 2014 guideline placed this add-on after the **second** episode (Rec 26, GRADE I, A, 1).
- Rifaximin is an effective **add-on** to lactulose for prevention of OHE recurrence (2014 Rec 20, GRADE I, A, 1). The pivotal maintenance trial ran on a background of **91% lactulose use**.
- **Rifaximin without lactulose — sources differ:** the 2014 guideline found no solid data for rifaximin alone. ACG 2026 suggests rifaximin in the outpatient setting to prevent HE recurrence after OHE (Rec 12, *conditional, low certainty*), and notes small/retrospective support for rifaximin monotherapy but that the preponderance of evidence favours the combination in patients who tolerate both; for >3 loose BMs see [[#Titration Endpoint and Monitoring]]. Follow ACG 2026.
- **ACLF/ICU:** the role of rifaximin as an add-on to lactulose or PEG warrants further investigation (AASLD 2024 GS 8); its value in the ICU is limited, especially in patients already on broad-spectrum antibiotics ([[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]]).

## Alternatives When Lactulose Fails or Is Not Tolerated

- **High-volume polyethylene glycol (e.g., 4 L)** as an alternative to lactulose for OHE (ACG 2026 Rec 8, *conditional, low certainty*). In an RCT, PEG 3350–electrolyte solution produced **more rapid HE resolution** than standard lactulose therapy. Give orally or by NG tube — specifically consider it in patients with abdominal discomfort, gas distension, or ileus from lactulose.
- **Lactitol** is preferred to lactulose in some centres on the basis of small meta-analyses; simple laxatives alone lack the prebiotic properties of the disaccharides ([[aasld-easl-2014-hepatic-encephalopathy]]).
- **Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs)** alongside standard care: ACG 2025 recommends BCAAs (when available) in addition to standard-of-care treatment in cirrhosis with HE (Rec 8, *strong, moderate quality*; [[acg-2025-liver-nutrition]]); the newer ACG 2026 recommends BCAA supplementation in HE **if protein needs cannot be met by food alone** (Rec 18, *strong, moderate certainty*). See [[nutrition-in-liver-disease]].
- Other second-line agents for HE (zinc, L-ornithine L-aspartate (LOLA), l-carnitine, neomycin, metronidazole) are tabulated on [[hepatic-encephalopathy]].

## Stopping Lactulose

- HE therapy may be **de-escalated and potentially discontinued in a stepwise fashion** in patients who clinically recompensate (ACG 2026 Key concept 16). ACG 2026 describes recompensation outside HE as resolution of ascites off diuretics and no variceal bleeding for 12 months, plus improved liver function (albumin, international normalized ratio, total bilirubin); if such a patient has no HE signs or symptoms on therapy, HE medications may be stopped sequentially with close monitoring for recurrence.
- Prophylactic therapy may be discontinued where precipitating factors have been well controlled (infections, variceal bleeding) **or** liver function or nutritional status has improved (2014 Rec 28, GRADE III, C, 2).
- A **positive MHE/CHE test before stopping** HE drug therapy identifies patients at risk of recurrent HE ([[aasld-easl-2014-hepatic-encephalopathy]]).
- [[baveno-viii-2026-portal-hypertension|Baveno VIII]] adds: absence of HE *while on* lactulose/rifaximin/LOLA is **not** evidence of recompensation — no HE *off* these drugs is one recompensation criterion (7.4b); withdrawal should be considered once aetiologic cure/control is achieved, HE has resolved, and synthetic function has improved to Child-Turcotte-Pugh A laboratory criteria (7.7, LoE 5, SoR strong). See [[cirrhosis]].

## Special Situations

| Situation | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **[[acute-liver-failure\|Acute liver failure (ALF)]]** | **No conclusive evidence for or against** lactulose or rifaximin for encephalopathy in ALF (ACG 2023 key concept). Oral lactulose is used in patients alert enough to maintain a safe airway (grade 1–2 encephalopathy); mind the timing of transplant, because bowel gaseous distention can be problematic intraoperatively. **Grade 3–4 → intubate and discontinue lactulose** ([[acg-2023-alf]]). Older AASLD 2011 Rec 26 (level III): in early encephalopathy, oral or rectal lactulose for a bowel purge, but **not to the point of diarrhea** ([[aasld-2011-alf]]) |
| **Perioperative [[cirrhosis]]** | Avoiding constipation is a priority to minimize postoperative HE flares; daily PEG may be used for regularity. Absent comparative trials, **rifaximin may be preferable in patients with HE who undergo primary bowel surgery**, because it generally causes **less bowel distension than lactulose** ([[aga-2018-surgical-risk-perioperative-cirrhosis]] Best Practice Advice [BPA] 10) |
| **Pregnancy** | Lactulose is among the listed treatment options for constipation in pregnancy (BPA 5); osmotic laxatives (PEG, lactulose) can be given safely, though excessive fiber and osmotic laxatives like lactulose can cause maternal bloating ([[aga-2024-pregnancy-gi-liver]]); [[aga-acg-2023-constipation]] calls it the only osmotic agent studied in pregnancy. See [[liver-disease-in-pregnancy]] |
| **Post-TIPS** | Routine prophylaxis not recommended (see Indications); lactulose or lactitol prevention was unsuccessful vs placebo in smaller studies. Established post-TIPS HE is treated like non-TIPS HE, including lactulose and rifaximin ([[acg-2026-hepatic-encephalopathy]]) — see [[tips]] |

## Diagnostic Use — Breath Testing Substrate

- **10 g lactulose** with ~250 mL water is the standardized lactulose dose for hydrogen/methane breath testing ([[acg-2020-sibo]]); glucose 75 g is the alternative substrate.
- Performance for [[small-intestinal-bacterial-overgrowth|small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)]]: **sensitivity 31–68%, specificity 44–100%**; criticized for high false-positive values from accelerated transit and colonic fermentation.
- Also the substrate option for diagnosing [[intestinal-methanogen-overgrowth|intestinal methanogen overgrowth (IMO)]] in symptomatic patients with constipation. Protocol and thresholds live on [[small-intestinal-bacterial-overgrowth]].
