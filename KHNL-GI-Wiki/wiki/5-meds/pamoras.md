## Class Overview

**Peripherally acting μ-opioid receptor antagonists (PAMORAs)** — drugs that **do not enter the central nervous system** but **block the μ-opioid receptors in the gut**, thereby effectively restoring the function of the enteric nervous system [[aga-2019-opioid-induced-constipation]].

- The therapeutic logic: [[opioid-induced-constipation|opioid-induced constipation]] arises primarily from **enteric μ-receptor activation**, so blocking those receptors reverses the constipating effect **without reversing central analgesia**.
- Distinguished in [[aga-2019-opioid-induced-constipation]] from traditional laxatives, intestinal secretagogues ([[lubiprostone|lubiprostone]]), and selective 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) agonists ([[prucalopride|prucalopride]]).

## Agents

Three PAMORAs carry American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) recommendations in laxative-refractory opioid-induced constipation (OIC); all three are US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved for that indication. A fourth, **alvimopan**, is approved for a different problem entirely — see below.

| Agent | Chemistry / pharmacology | FDA approval | Formulations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Naloxegol** | **Pegylated derivative of naloxone.** Pegylation allows increased **oral bioavailability** and enhanced **peripheral selectivity**. Also a **substrate for the P-glycoprotein transporter**, which limits entry of the medication into the central nervous system (CNS) | **2014** — the **first** PAMORA approved for management of OIC in adult patients with **chronic non-cancer pain** | Oral |
| **Methylnaltrexone** | **Quaternary ammonium cation** that **cannot cross the blood–brain barrier**. Has opioid antagonist effects throughout the body — **reverses itching as well as constipation** | — | **Oral and subcutaneous injection** |
| **Naldemedine** | **Structurally related to naltrexone** | **March 2017** — the latest PAMORA approved for management of OIC | Oral |

### Dosing

[[aga-2019-opioid-induced-constipation]] prints no doses. [[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology|AGA 2017]] supplies them for two of the three:

| Agent | Approved dose for OIC in chronic non-cancer pain | Monitoring |
|---|---|---|
| **Methylnaltrexone** | **12 mg subcutaneous injection** (trial regimen: 12 mg once daily **or** every other day) | — |
| **Naloxegol** | **12.5 mg or 25 mg orally** | **Monitor for adverse cardiovascular events** — an explicit FDA condition of approval |
| **Naldemedine** | dose not printed in either source | — |

- **Naloxegol dosing frequency is left unstated deliberately.** AGA 2017 prints "4 times a day," but the phase II/III trials it tabulates (Webster 2013; Chey 2014) gave naloxegol **daily** and reported spontaneous bowel movements (SBMs) per week from a daily regimen. The strengths are consistent; the printed frequency is not supported by the document's own cited trials.
- **Methylnaltrexone efficacy at 12 mg** (4-week randomized controlled trial [RCT] vs placebo): shortened time to first rescue-free bowel movement, more weekly rescue-free bowel movements, less straining, less sense of incomplete evacuation. **34.2% had a rescue-free bowel movement within 4 h of the first dose vs 9.9%** placebo; **46% within 24 h vs 25.3%**.
- **Naloxegol efficacy:** improved SBMs from the **first week** of treatment, including in patients with an inadequate response to laxatives; also improved stool consistency, complete SBMs, percentage of days with straining, and constipation quality-of-life scores.
- **An improvement in SBM frequency by ≥3 per week** is the change associated with consistent improvement in patient-reported outcomes — a practical response target for any agent in this class.

### Alvimopan — Postoperative Ileus, Not OIC

- **Approved in the United States for postoperative ileus in patients after bowel resection** — not for OIC [[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]].
- Accelerates recovery of gastrointestinal (GI) function (especially the **lower** GI tract), shortens length of hospital stay, and reduces postoperative ileus-related morbidity **without compromising opioid analgesia** in an enhanced recovery setting.
- **Meta-analysis of 9 RCTs, 4,075 patients:** significantly decreased time to first passage of stool and lowered the chance of serious side effects.
- Pharmacologically a **δ (delta)-opioid receptor antagonist as well as a μ antagonist**.

## Safety

- **Avoid PAMORAs in conditions that compromise the blood–brain barrier**, due to the potential for **serious withdrawal or reversal of anesthesia** [[aga-2019-opioid-induced-constipation]].
- **Naldemedine** — adverse events leading to discontinuation were more common than placebo (**relative risk [RR] 1.44; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.03–2.03**): infection, abdominal pain, diarrhea, flatulence, nausea, back pain; the absolute increase was low (**2 more per 100 treated patients**). It is the **only prescription OIC agent in the guideline with long-term (52-week) safety data**.
- **Naloxegol** — generalized or upper abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, headache, flatulence (**RR 2.33; 95% CI 1.62–3.35**). Two deaths in the safety extension study, **one in each study arm**; neither was considered related to study medication.
- **Methylnaltrexone** — no statistically significant increase in adverse events leading to discontinuation. Most common events reported: abdominal pain and nausea; also diarrhea, hyperhidrosis, vomiting.
  - ⚠ **Perforation signal — 7 cases of gastric or intestinal perforation** reported to the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System during the **first 18 months after approval** [[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]. **Causation has not been established**: the affected patients had either a coincidental gastric ulcer or severe constipation, itself a risk factor for perforation.
- **Naloxegol over 52 weeks** in OIC with non-cancer pain — most common side effects abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, headache, flatulence; **no QT/corrected QT (QTc) interval prolongation and no serious adverse events** occurred [[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]. It antagonized morphine-induced delay in orocecal transit with **no effect on miosis or opioid withdrawal** — evidence of exclusively peripheral action.
- **Cost** is a practical limit on the whole class: it may limit patient and provider use of naldemedine, naloxegol use "should be judicious," and methylnaltrexone is high-cost relative to other agents — though its subcutaneous form may be an advantage in some situations.

## Clinical Use

- **Indication:** **laxative-refractory** OIC — i.e. after scheduled laxatives from at least 2 classes have failed. PAMORAs are **not** first-line; laxatives are.
- **Recommendations and efficacy data live on [[opioid-induced-constipation]]** — naldemedine (strong, high-quality evidence), naloxegol (strong, moderate), methylnaltrexone (conditional, low) — alongside the laxative-refractory criteria and the Bowel Function Index cutoff that triggers escalation.
