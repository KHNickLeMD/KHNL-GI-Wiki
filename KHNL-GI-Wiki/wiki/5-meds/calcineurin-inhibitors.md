## Overview

Calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) — [[tacrolimus]] (TAC) and cyclosporine (CSA) — are the backbone of maintenance immunosuppression after [[liver-transplantation|liver transplantation (LT)]], and the rescue drugs in [[ulcerative-colitis|ulcerative colitis (UC)]] that fails intravenous (IV) corticosteroids.

- **Nephrotoxicity is a class effect and the main indication for discontinuing a CNI**; it is the primary driver of chronic kidney disease (CKD) after LT ([[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-graft-complications]]).
- Neither guideline names a preferred CNI. Choice of agent depends on the indication for LT, comorbidities, drug side effects, likelihood of pregnancy, and the rejection, cancer and infection history — **no one regimen can be recommended for every patient** ([[aasld-2012-liver-transplant-long-term]], Rec 6, grade 2, level A).
- Dosing, trough targets, monitoring intervals and the transplant-pregnancy regimen for TAC live on **[[tacrolimus]]**; the full post-transplant context on **[[liver-transplantation]]**.

## Agents

### Tacrolimus (FK506)

- Once-daily extended-release should be used **similarly to** twice-daily immediate-release — equally effective for preventing rejection and maintaining allograft survival ([[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-graft-complications]], Rec 31, Strong, Level 3).
- Rejection switches *toward* TAC: cyclosporine recipients are changed to TAC for T-cell–mediated rejection and for chronic ductopenic rejection (2025 Recs 37, 38, 44).
- **Dosing and trough targets by time after LT live on [[tacrolimus]]** — see that page rather than duplicating them here.

### Cyclosporine (CSA)

- Dose: **2 mg/kg/day orally (PO), given in two divided doses**, adjusted to trough ([[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-graft-complications]], Table 4).
- Trough targets: **250–300 ng/mL for the first 3 months; 100–250 ng/mL from month 3 onward** (Table 4).
- **What changed:** [[aasld-2012-liver-transplant-long-term]] gave a single whole-blood target of **100–150 ng/mL after 3 months**; the 2025 targets replace it.
- Dose and target trough "vary at the discretion of the transplant professional" (2025 Table 4 footnote).

## Side Effects — Which CNI Owns Which

[[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-graft-complications]] Table 4 gives one shared adverse-effect list for the class and marks only the agent-specific effects; [[aasld-2012-liver-transplant-long-term]] Table 7 scores the metabolic-syndrome contributors from **+** to **+++**. Both are reproduced as stated.

| Side effect | Attribution as stated in the sources |
|---|---|
| Nephrotoxicity | **Class effect** — primary driver of post-LT CKD and the main reason a CNI is stopped (2025 Table 4) |
| Electrolyte disorders, neurotoxicity, anemia | **Class effects** (2025 Table 4) |
| Diabetes | **More with TAC** — 2025 Table 4 and text ("TAC increases the risk of de novo" diabetes mellitus [DM]); insulin-resistant DM scored **++** TAC vs **+** cyclosporine (American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases [AASLD] 2012 Table 7) |
| Systemic hypertension | 2025 text: **CSA** increases the risk of hypertension, hyperlipidemia and metabolic syndrome after LT; 2012 Table 7 scores both agents **++** |
| Dyslipidemia | 2012 Table 7: [[tacrolimus\|TAC]] **+**, cyclosporine **+**, sirolimus **+++**; Table 5 hypercholesterolemia: CNIs **+** vs mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors **+++**. CNIs, mTOR inhibitors and glucocorticoids are "the major influences" on post-LT dyslipidemia |
| Gingival hyperplasia | **Cyclosporine only** (2025 Table 4) |
| Hirsutism | **Cyclosporine only** (2025 Table 4) |

> Beyond the metabolic scores in AASLD 2012 Table 7, the guidelines do not grade the relative severity of CNI adverse effects between tacrolimus and cyclosporine.

## Drug Interactions

Levels track cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A): inhibitors raise them, inducers lower them ([[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-graft-complications]] Table 4; [[aasld-2012-liver-transplant-long-term]] Table 4).

**Levels rise** — grapefruit juice and CYP3A inhibitors: macrolide antibiotics (erythromycin > clarithromycin > azithromycin — *markedly* increased), azole antifungals (ketoconazole/voriconazole/posaconazole > itraconazole/fluconazole), calcium channel blockers (diltiazem, verapamil), protease inhibitors, **nirmatrelvir–ritonavir**, antimalarial agents, benzodiazepines (diazepam, alprazolam), serotonin uptake inhibitors, allopurinol, amiodarone, danazol, [[proton-pump-inhibitors|proton pump inhibitors]], and fluoroquinolones (ofloxacin > ciprofloxacin).

**Levels fall** — CYP3A inducers: antimycobacterial agents (rifamycins, rifampin > rifabutin — *markedly* decreased), anticonvulsants, and St. John's wort.

**CSA-specific:** [[direct-acting-antivirals|direct-acting antiviral]] therapy raises CSA levels — sofosbuvir, glecaprevir, voxilaprevir (2025 Table 4).

Voriconazole is marked **contraindicated** with mTOR inhibitors only, not with CNIs (2012 Table 4).

> [!pitfall] Rifampin needs a 2- to 5-fold CNI dose rise
> Rifampin markedly lowers CNI and mTOR inhibitor levels — **raise the CNI dose 2- to 5-fold when it is started**. Rifabutin can be substituted, or a non-rifampin regimen used, but treatment then has to run longer ([[aasld-2012-liver-transplant-long-term]]).

## In Ulcerative Colitis

*[[ulcerative-colitis]] owns the acute severe UC (ASUC) pathway — when to escalate, the [[anti-tnf-agents|infliximab]]-vs-cyclosporine choice, bridging, colectomy. This page owns the drug parameters ([[acg-2025-uc]]).*

### Cyclosporine Rescue in Acute Severe UC

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Indication | Medical **rescue** in ASUC **failing to respond adequately to IV [[corticosteroids-ibd\|corticosteroids]] (IVCS) by day 3** — infliximab **or** cyclosporine (Rec 51, **Strong**, moderate quality) |
| Target dose | **2 mg/kg** — preferred over 4 mg/kg: comparable clinical response and colectomy rates with **fewer adverse events** |
| Route / duration | **CySIF regimen: 2 mg/kg for 1 week, then oral cyclosporine**; responders started azathioprine from day 7. The American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) prints only "2 mg/kg for 1 week, followed by oral cyclosporine" and does not state the initial route |
| Drug level | **200–400** for efficacy (the guideline gives the range without units) |
| When *not* to use | Restricted to IVCS failure, **except** where corticosteroids are contraindicated or not tolerated — efficacy is similar to IVCS, not superior |
| Maintenance after CSA-induced remission | **[[thiopurines]]** (Rec 53, Conditional, low quality) or [[vedolizumab]] (Rec 54, Conditional, very low quality) |
| Surgical safety | Cyclosporine (like infliximab) does **not** increase postoperative complications of colectomy — **do not defer surgery** on account of exposure (Key Concept 55) |

- **Counsel on the long game:** in the landmark controlled trial, 9 of 11 patients given cyclosporine 4 mg/kg responded at a mean of 7 days vs none on placebo — but on long-term follow-up **up to 80% eventually require colectomy**.
- **Thiopurine-naive patients started on thiopurine maintenance after cyclosporine have a lower colectomy risk** than those never started on, or previously failing, a thiopurine — which is why prior thiopurine exposure feeds the rescue-agent choice.
- Use should be restricted to providers familiar with dosing, trough monitoring, and managing its adverse effects.

> [!pitfall] Check cholesterol and magnesium before cyclosporine
> Patients with lower serum cholesterol or magnesium are at **greater risk of neurological adverse events** from cyclosporine and should be considered for infliximab instead ([[acg-2025-uc]]).

### Tacrolimus in Steroid-Refractory UC

Not a guideline recommendation — [[acg-2025-uc]] reviews the evidence without issuing a recommendation for it, so cyclosporine remains the named rescue CNI (Rec 51):

- Double-blind placebo-controlled trial, **62 patients**: week-2 clinical response **50% vs 13%** placebo (*P* = 0.003); mucosal healing **44% vs 13%**; **side effects few**.
- **Target serum trough 10–15 ng/mL** — note this is *higher* than the post-transplant targets on [[tacrolimus]].
- Efficacy appears **similar in children**.
- **Limited long-term outcome and colectomy-rate data** — the reason it is not recommended.

## In Pregnancy

*Reproductive care in [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]] — preconception optimization, trimester plan, delivery, the full drug grid: [[ibd-in-pregnancy]].*

- **Cyclosporine and tacrolimus may be initiated or continued in pregnancy with careful monitoring — but only if there are no viable alternate treatment options** ([[global-consensus-2025-pregnancy-ibd]], Consensus Statement 15, ungraded). In IBD that makes the CNI a last-resort salvage agent in the pregnant patient, not an equal alternative: overall safety data favor anti-tumor necrosis factor (anti-TNF) agents, given their effectiveness and safety.
- Pregnancy data are drawn mainly from transplant rather than IBD populations: **no increase in congenital malformations**, but spontaneous abortion, prematurity, pre-eclampsia and low birth weight have been reported, and disease activity could not be separated from drug effect. Tacrolimus data in pregnant IBD patients amount to a single case report.
- Otherwise, active disease in pregnancy is started or optimized on **the same therapies as in a nonpregnant patient**, except that [[thiopurines]], [[methotrexate]], [[jak-inhibitors|Janus kinase inhibitors]] and sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor modulators are not *started* in pregnancy (Consensus Statement 12).
- The consensus's preconception-through-lactation drug grid does not include the calcineurin inhibitors, and it gives **no lactation guidance** for them.

## After Liver Transplantation

### Induction — Delaying CNI Start

Per [[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-graft-complications]]:

- **Basiliximab** (interleukin-2 receptor antagonist [IL2-RA]), **20 mg IV at postoperative day (POD) 0 and POD 4**, can be used to delay the CNI start and improve renal recovery in deceased- and living-donor recipients **with renal dysfunction** (Rec 25, Weak, Level 2). It is the only IL2-RA still commercially available.
- Supporting registry analysis is for **IL2-RA as a class**: glomerular filtration rate (GFR) better by **3.8 mL/min/1.73 m² at 6 months** after LT in patients not on dialysis, and a **12% reduction in biopsy-proven acute rejection (BPAR) over the first year** (adjusted odds ratio 0.88, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.81–0.96) — the two endpoints are measured over **different windows**.
- Trials in living donor LT (LDLT) also showed **lower de novo DM and hypertension**, attributed to the steroid avoidance or reduction that induction allows (e.g. DM 10.6% → 4.7%, hypertension 12.7% → 7.1%; 2025 Table 3).
- **Anti-thymocyte globulin should NOT be used as induction** — higher mortality and graft failure risk vs corticosteroids (Rec 26, Strong, Level 2). Alemtuzumab has been associated with higher infection and malignancy risk.
- **Simultaneous liver–kidney or ABO-incompatible LT:** routine basiliximab induction is **not** recommended, for insufficient data (Rec 27, Strong, Level 5).

### CNI Minimization Strategies

When CKD develops or is threatened:

| Step | What it does | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduce the CNI dose** | Lower TAC or CSA to the minimum effective dose | Reduction or withdrawal of CNI-based immunosuppression (IS) is the appropriate response to CKD; renal function is best preserved if withdrawal starts while estimated GFR is **40–50 mL/min/1.73 m²** (2012 Rec 29, grade 1, level A) |
| **Add an antimetabolite or an mTOR inhibitor** | Allows reduced TAC exposure without more rejection | 2025 Rec 30, Strong, Level 3 — [[mycophenolate-mofetil\|mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)]] + reduced-dose TAC improves renal function without increasing graft rejection |
| **Convert to or add an mTOR inhibitor** (everolimus or sirolimus) | Prevents progression of kidney disease | 2025 Rec 33, Strong, Level 2 — best implemented early, **~3 months** post-LT |
| **Lower CNI exposure after LT for metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH)** | CNIs contribute to de novo diabetes and metabolic syndrome | 2025 Rec 74, Weak, Level 5 — may be considered, with or without concurrent MMF |
| **Kidney transplantation** | Optimal therapy for end-stage renal disease (ESRD) after LT | **44–60% reduction in long-term mortality** vs dialysis (2012 Rec 30, grade 1, level A) |

- **Everolimus (EVL) has the strongest renal evidence:** meta-analysis of 4 studies (n=893) found GFR improvement of **7.4 mL/min (95% CI 2.75–17.8)**, greater at **10.2 mL/min** when EVL was introduced **within 4 weeks** of LT. Introducing it late, once GFR is already **<60 mL/min/1.73 m²**, gives only minor renal improvement. (The guideline prints plain mL/min for this meta-analysis, not body-surface-normalized.)
- **Sirolimus (SRL):** meta-analysis of 3 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) found GFR improvement of **10.38 mL/min (95% CI 3.98–16.77)** after CNI replacement or reduction with SRL added at a median of **36–50 months** post-LT; the benefit was no longer significant once 8 observational studies were pooled in.
- MMF is **the only immunosuppressant not associated with metabolic syndrome**, which is what makes it an attractive CNI adjunct in MASH recipients (2025).

### Non-CNI Agents Used to Reduce or Replace the CNI

Not calcineurin inhibitors, but the agents CNI minimization converts to or adds ([[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-graft-complications]], Table 4). *Dose and target trough "vary at the discretion of the transplant professional" (table footnote).*

| Class | Agent | Dose | Target trough |
|---|---|---|---|
| mTOR inhibitor | Everolimus | **1 mg PO twice daily, after month 1** | **3–8 ng/mL** |
| mTOR inhibitor | Sirolimus (off-label in LT) | **2 mg PO daily, after month 1** | **4–10 ng/mL** |
| Antimetabolite | MMF / mycophenolic acid (MPA) (off-label) | **MMF 1,000–3,000 mg PO** / **MPA 360–720 mg PO**, in divided doses **twice daily** | Not monitored |
| Antimetabolite | [[thiopurines\|Azathioprine]] | **3–5 mg/kg/day PO once daily** | Not monitored |

- **What changed:** 2012 gave a single whole-blood sirolimus target of **5 ng/mL**; 2025 Table 4 gives **4–10 ng/mL**.
- **mTOR class adverse effects:** gastrointestinal (GI) intolerance, leukopenia, hyperlipidemia, proteinuria, peripheral edema, impaired wound healing, aphthous stomatitis, interstitial pneumonitis, and hepatic artery thrombosis (sirolimus carries a black box warning for it; a meta-analysis showed no increased risk with everolimus).
- **Antimetabolite adverse effects:** cytopenias, GI, infections, and malignancy (azathioprine adds hepatic and hypersensitivity reactions; mycophenolate is **teratogenic**).
- **Antimetabolite interactions:** mycophenolate's are on [[mycophenolate-mofetil]]. For azathioprine, [[mesalamine-5-asa|aminosalicylates]] increase levels; cytopenias may increase with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and ribavirin; azathioprine may inhibit the anticoagulant effect of warfarin.

### Steroid Minimization vs. CNI Minimization

Per [[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-graft-complications]] Rec 28:

- Steroid-free IS **cannot be routinely recommended**: higher BPAR (relative risk [RR] 1.33), steroid-resistant rejection (RR 2.14), and decreased GFR — despite similar graft and patient survival (Strong, Level 2).
- Corticosteroid avoidance or withdrawal reduces hypertension (RR 0.76) and DM (RR 0.81), but these benefits are offset by the higher rejection rates and increased serum creatinine (mean difference 0.11 mg/dL).
- Long-term steroid use should still be minimized where possible — but abrupt steroid elimination increases rejection risk.

### Complete IS Withdrawal

Complete IS withdrawal ([[operational-tolerance|operational tolerance]]) is **not routinely recommended** — no reliable predictors of success and limited data in a very select group of long-term recipients ([[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-graft-complications]], Rec 36, Strong, Level 4). See that page for full discussion.
