## The Modalities at a Glance

| Modality | Class | Primary role | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dilute epinephrine injection | Injection | Temporizing, improves visualization | **Never as monotherapy** — high rebleeding risk |
| Absolute ethanol injection | Injection | Definitive injection option (ACG **strong**) | Volume must be capped (tissue necrosis) |
| Heater probe | Contact thermal | Definitive; ACG **strong** | Fixed-duration energy; consecutive thermal therapy raises perforation risk |
| Bipolar / multipolar probe | Contact thermal | Definitive; ACG **strong** | As above |
| Monopolar hemostatic forceps (MHF) | Contact thermal | Difficult locations, rigid fibrotic base, [[endoscopic-submucosal-dissection\|ESD]]/[[endoscopic-mucosal-resection\|EMR]] bleeding | Dissipated by a large overlying clot; costly |
| [[argon-plasma-coagulation\|APC]] | Noncontact thermal | Flat vascular lesions, diffuse oozing | Superficial; ACG **conditional** for ulcers |
| Through-the-scope clips | Mechanical | Definitive; first-line for postpolypectomy bleeding | Hard to place on fibrotic bases and awkward angles |
| Over-the-scope clips (OTSC) | Mechanical | Recurrent bleeding; large fibrotic ulcer with big visible vessel | Requires scope withdrawal to load; no comparative data between devices |
| Endoscopic band ligation (EBL) | Mechanical | Diverticular hemorrhage; varices | Not for the right colon without care (thin wall) |
| TC-325 hemostatic powder | Topical | Rescue; massive/diffuse bleeding; malignant bleeding | **Present <24 h** — not durable monotherapy for high-risk stigmata |

**There is no definitively superior modality.** Meta-analysis subgroup analyses show **no difference between hemoclips and thermal treatment** in peptic-ulcer rebleeding rates; data showing superiority of one over the other are largely lacking. Choice rests on lesion location, ulcer characteristics, and operator familiarity ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).

## Injection Therapy

### Dilute Epinephrine

- **Concentration: 1:10,000 — equivalent to 100 µg/mL** ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).
- Injected at or near the bleeding site; achieves initial hemostasis by **inducing vasospasm**.
- **This is a temporizing measure only**, used to improve visualization so definitive therapy can be targeted.
- ⚠ **Epinephrine monotherapy must be avoided** — high rebleeding risk. If epinephrine is used, a **second hemostatic modality must follow** ([[acg-2021-ugib]], [[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).
- **But dual therapy is not itself superior:** epinephrine plus another therapy has **no advantage over properly applied thermal or mechanical monotherapy** ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]). The rule is *never alone*, not *always add it*.
- No volume, injection-site count, or maximum dose is stated in any ingested source — **decision gap, flagged not filled.**

### Absolute Ethanol

- **0.1–0.2 mL aliquots, maximum 1–2 mL total** ([[acg-2021-ugib]] — **strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence**).
- The volume cap is the whole safety margin; ethanol sclerosis is dose-dependent tissue injury.

## Contact Thermal Therapy

**General technique** ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]):

- Use a **10F probe when possible**.
- Apply **forceful contact for at least 8 seconds**, with the scope tip as close as possible to the treatment site, to **completely obliterate the culprit vessel**.
- **Failure to obliterate the vessel** causes bleeding during treatment or increases rebleeding risk — the commonest technical failure.
- Excellent visualization is paramount; use an endoscope with a **dedicated water jet**.

### Heater Probe

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Energy per pulse | **25–30 J** | [[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]] |
| Pulses | **4–5** | [[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]] |
| Total energy | **100–150 J** | [[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]] |
| Alternative statement | **30 J applications, 3.2-mm probe with firm pressure** | [[acg-2021-ugib]] (strong, moderate) |

### Bipolar / Multipolar Electrocoagulation

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Power | **15–20 W** | [[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]] |
| ACG technique statement | **15 W, 8–10 second applications, 3.2-mm probe with firm pressure** | [[acg-2021-ugib]] (strong, moderate) |
| LGIB — diverticular hemorrhage | Bipolar/multipolar coagulation is an accepted option | [[acg-2023-lgib]] |

### Soft Monopolar Coagulation and Hemostatic Forceps

**Monopolar hemostatic forceps (MHF)** — small, flat, rotatable jaws (e.g. Coagrasper); originally developed to control bleeding during [[endoscopic-submucosal-dissection|ESD]].

- **Setting: low-voltage soft coagulation, 80 W, effect 4** (ERBE VIO series). Low voltage limits peak voltage, which **minimizes depth of penetration and tissue effect** ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).
- **Technique in ulcer bleeding differs from ESD:** apply the forceps to the bleeding point **closed** — do *not* first open the atraumatic jaws to grasp the vessel — then apply **soft coagulation 80 W, effect 4, for 1–2 seconds**, retract, and retreat as needed.
- [[acg-2021-ugib]] states soft monopolar electrocoagulation as **soft coagulation mode, 50–80 W, 1–2 second applications** (**conditional recommendation**, lower-quality evidence).
- **Indication (BPA 4):** an effective alternative to other mechanical and thermal treatments, **particularly for ulcers in difficult locations or with a rigid and fibrotic base**.
- **Evidence:** RCT of 112 patients with Forrest Ia/Ib/IIa gastric or duodenal ulcers — initial hemostasis **98% with MHF soft coagulation vs 80% with hemoclips (P = .004)**.
- **Advantages:** ease and flexibility of targeting; the smaller forceps reach where a clip cannot; current duration is controlled by the endoscopist rather than fixed, making **retreatment more controlled with less tissue injury**.
- **How it fails:**
  - A **large overlying blood clot dissipates the coagulation effect** and prevents hemostasis.
  - A **large area needing treatment** requires repeated applications — the limited tissue effect becomes a disadvantage.
- ⚠ **Cheaper improvisations are unstudied.** Snare-tip soft coagulation, [[argon-plasma-coagulation|APC]] applied to conduct heat through already-placed clips, and **closed hot biopsy forceps** are all used to avoid MHF cost, but *"these uses of thermal energy delivery to treat peptic ulcer bleeding have not been formally studied"* — reserved for endoscopists very familiar with them ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).
- ⚠ **Switching to forced coagulation** can overcome pools of liquid or blood between forceps and bleeding point, **but** risks treating a larger area with less control over depth. Monitor the coagulum for **deep tissue injury, perforation, or delayed bleeding** whenever electrosurgical energy is applied. See [[electrosurgery]].

## Mechanical Therapy

### Through-the-Scope Clips

- **Placement over and on both sides of the bleeding site** ([[acg-2021-ugib]] — **conditional recommendation**, lower-quality evidence).
- **First-line for postpolypectomy bleeding**; for **diverticular hemorrhage**, direct clipping onto the vessel is preferred over indirect/zipper closure ([[acg-2023-lgib]]).
- **Locations where clips are harder and less effective than thermal therapy:** posterior wall of the duodenum, lesser curvature of the stomach; and lesions with fibrosis, large ulceration, or a large visible vessel ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).
- A **low-profile distal attachment cap** on a forward-viewing endoscope facilitates visualization and therapy; a **duodenoscope may be required** for the posterior duodenal bulb and second portion of the duodenum.

### Over-the-Scope Clips

- **Two devices with different designs and deployment mechanisms: OTSC (Ovesco) and Padlock clip (Steris).**
- ⚠ **No comparative evidence at all:** there have been **no head-to-head comparisons of the two clips**, and **no head-to-head trials of over-the-scope clips against through-the-scope clips or thermal therapy** ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]). The wiki should not imply a preference between devices.
- **Established indication — recurrent bleeding.** An RCT showed OTSC more effective than standard therapy in **recurrent** peptic ulcer bleeding; [[acg-2021-ugib]] reserves OTSC for recurrent ulcer bleeding after previous successful endoscopic hemostasis, reporting **further bleeding 15.2% vs 57.6%**.
- **Consider as first-line (BPA 5 setting):** large, fibrotic ulcer beds with a **large visible vessel** not amenable to through-the-scope clip fixation, or where **thermal therapy is predicted to be ineffective**.
- **Rescue:** small case series support OTSC as an effective rescue modality when conventional hemostasis fails.
- **Not yet a paradigm shift:** non-randomized data suggest possible superiority for *initial* treatment versus historical rebleeding rates, but *"large RCTs are needed that demonstrate considerable benefit"* before changing first-line practice.

### Endoscopic Band Ligation

- **Diverticular hemorrhage:** EBL gives **lower early rebleeding (8% vs 19%)** and **lower late rebleeding (9% vs 29%)** versus clipping, and **may be preferred for durable outcomes** ([[acg-2023-lgib]]).
- Initial hemostasis rates for diverticular bleeding are **99–100% across all modalities** — the modalities separate on **rebleeding**, not on initial success. Even after hemostasis, early rebleeding is **17.4%** and late rebleeding **32.0%**.
- Variceal band ligation is covered on [[variceal-upper-gi-bleeding]].

## Topical Hemostatic Powder (TC-325)

**TC-325 (Hemospray)** — the commercially available agent in North America.

- **Mechanism:** propelled through a delivery catheter by a **CO₂ canister**; **on contact with moisture the powder aggregates, creating a mechanical barrier on the mucosa** ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).
- **Practical advantage:** noncontact — **does not require en-face positioning** and **does not require advanced endoscopic training or skills**. Useful where advanced expertise is not always available and temporizing is needed before definitive therapy or transfer.

**Indication (BPA 6 and 7)** — a rescue agent, with three named exceptions:

> Hemostatic powder should be **preferentially used as a rescue therapy and not for primary hemostasis**, except in cases of **malignant bleeding** or **massive bleeding with inability to perform thermal therapy or hemoclip placement**.

- Also considered for **massive bleeding with poor visualization** and **diffuse bleeding from malignancy** (a pilot RCT supports use in diffuse tumor bleeding, **optimally as a bridge to radiotherapy or surgery**).
- **Postpolypectomy bleeding:** hemostatic powders as monotherapy or rescue give **97–98% initial hemostasis** ([[acg-2023-lgib]]).
- [[acg-2021-ugib]] (**conditional**) restricts TC-325 for ulcers to **actively bleeding** lesions — **it does not adhere to non-bleeding lesions** — and notes high cost (~$2,500 list price) limits first-line US use.

⚠ **The decisive limitation is duration.** Retrospective studies show **the powder remains present for less than 24 hours**. Therefore **TC-325 monotherapy is not recommended in ulcers with high-risk stigmata for rebleeding** — use it to stop bleeding temporarily, then apply another modality **either at the index procedure or at second-look endoscopy** ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).

**Deployment technique** ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]], [[acg-2021-ugib]]):

- **7F and 10F catheters** available for different accessory-channel sizes (diagnostic vs therapeutic scopes).
- Position **1–2 cm** from the bleeding site; deploy in **short controlled bursts (1–2 seconds)**.
- **Keep the catheter dry** — moisture prematurely activates the powder *inside* the catheter and prevents deployment:
  - Keep the catheter tip out of contact with pools of fluid or blood
  - Preflush the accessory channel with air (60-mL syringe)
  - Temporarily detach suction tubing before inserting the catheter, to avoid inadvertent suctioning
- **Avoid kinking** the catheter at the duodenoscope elevator or in retroflexion.
- **Avoid spraying the lens.** **Do not wash the lesion site afterward** — washing removes the powder.
- **Overapplication** produces a diffuse spray effect that limits subsequent mucosal assessment. If multiple modalities are planned, **plan their timing and sequence** around the loss of visibility.

**Safety.** A study in **variceal** bleeding demonstrated clinical effectiveness without adverse events; adverse events occurring in powder studies were judged unlikely to be attributable to the sprayed powder.

⚠ **Open question flagged by the source itself:** 2 pilot RCTs and a non-controlled registry suggest a **potential role for TC-325 as monotherapy** in upper GI bleeding, which the CPU says *"[raises] the need for an appropriately powered clinical trial."* BPA 7 (rescue-only) remains the operative advice until then.

## Confirming Hemostasis — Doppler Probe

- A **through-the-scope Doppler probe can be considered after hemostasis is achieved**.
- **A persistent Doppler signal after hemostasis is a risk factor for rebleeding.**
- A single-blind RCT showed **significantly decreased rebleeding within 30 days** in patients randomized to post-treatment Doppler assessment versus controls ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).

## Bleeding Caused by Endoscopy Itself

**BPA 8** — endoscopists performing higher-risk interventions must be able to treat the bleeding they cause. Higher-risk procedures: **[[polypectomy]], [[endoscopic-mucosal-resection|EMR]], [[endoscopic-submucosal-dissection|ESD]], and endoscopic sphincterotomy** ([[ercp]]). Bleeding may be **immediate/intraprocedural** or **delayed/postprocedural**.

**During EMR or ESD:**

- **Thermal therapy — particularly hemostatic forceps — may be preferable to hemoclips**, because clips may **interfere with completing the resection** ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).

**Post-sphincterotomy bleeding:**

- **Biliary stents may be used to tamponade** the bleeding site.
- Clips and thermal therapy can be used, but note two hazards: **clip placement through a duodenoscope is difficult**, and **thermal therapy or errant clip placement can cause pancreatitis**.
- ⚠ **When clips or thermal coagulation are used for post-sphincterotomy bleeding, keep a low threshold for placing a prophylactic pancreatic duct stent.**
- Be prepared to perform **[[ercp]] for all delayed post-sphincterotomy bleeds** — biliary and/or pancreatic duct stenting may be required.

**Prophylaxis of postprocedural bleeding:**

- **Right-sided colonic EMR:** clip closure of the defect may decrease post-polypectomy bleeding.
- ⚠ **There is no similar high-level evidence for upper GI interventions.** For upper GI EMR/ESD sites the options are MHF to visible vessels, clip closure, or endosuturing — **no comparative trials exist**. Consider closing the site, when amenable, in patients who need **immediate resumption of antithrombotics**.
- **No data at all** exist on prophylaxis of post-sphincterotomy bleeding; in patients requiring immediate antithrombotic resumption, a **fully covered metallic biliary stent** may be used.

## When Endoscopic Hemostasis Fails

**First: try again endoscopically.**

- After rebleeding, **a second attempt at endoscopic hemostasis is typically advised** — ~75% success in preventing further bleeding ([[acg-2021-ugib]]).
- **Switch modality** where possible: after initial thermal therapy, use additional thermal *or* mechanical therapy with a clip. Consecutive thermal contact therapy increases perforation risk. **TC-325 should also be considered as rescue.**
- **Consider OTSC** for recurrent bleeding after initial hemostasis.
- Seek a **second opinion or a more experienced endoscopist** in difficult situations.
- **Second-look endoscopy should not be performed routinely**, but has a role where rebleeding risk is high despite apparent initial success, or where the index procedure did not adequately control bleeding. *(What makes rebleeding risk "high" is the ulcer stigma — the Forrest classes and their class-specific rebleeding rates are on [[upper-gi-bleeding]].)*

**Why the first attempt was suboptimal — the checklist to run before repeating:** ulcer size, location, or fibrosis; **epinephrine-injection monotherapy**; **single use of hemostatic powder for a high-risk lesion**; inadequate coagulation; insufficient tissue grasped by a hemostatic clip ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).

**Then: TAE or surgery — case by case (BPA 9).** Weigh the etiology of bleeding (peptic ulcer disease, unknown source, post-surgical), patient factors (hemodynamic instability, coagulopathy, multi-organ failure, surgical history), rebleeding risk, and potential adverse events, alongside **local expertise and resource availability**.

- Comparative data ([[acg-2021-ugib]]): **TAE** has fewer complications (OR 0.45) and shorter stay (median 8 vs 16 days) with **no mortality difference**, but **higher further bleeding** (OR 2.44) than surgery.
- ⚠ **Prophylactic TAE of high-risk ulcers after successful endoscopic therapy is not encouraged** (BPA 10) — concerns include recanalization and subsequent ischemic complications.
- **A multidisciplinary approach is required** in refractory bleeding: endoscopists, intensivists, surgeons, interventional radiologists, and in some cases hematologists and oncologists.

## Preparation and Equipment

- **Airway:** endotracheal intubation for suspected ongoing or massive bleeding, active hematemesis, or suspected retained gastric contents, to reduce aspiration risk ([[aga-2020-nvugib-endoscopic-therapies]]).
- **Scope choice:** therapeutic upper endoscopes have a **larger working channel accommodating 10F accessories**, with improved suction of retained contents and clots — preferred in severe bleeding. A **dedicated water jet** is required for treating NVUGIB. In the colon, a **clear cap is recommended** — it improves detection and facilitates hemostasis ([[acg-2023-lgib]]).
- **Improving visualization when clot obscures the field:** reposition the patient (reverse Trendelenburg, or supine / right lateral decubitus); pre-procedural prokinetics; "clot-busting" tubing or 6-mm-channel endoscopes for increased suction; extraction/foreign-body retrieval nets. **Do not spend time clearing a large gastric clot unless no source is found distally.** If clot prevents full gastric examination and no source is found, **repeat endoscopy after allowing time for gastric emptying**, timed to patient stability and degree of bleeding.
- **Coagulation status:** endoscopic hemostasis is considered safe at **INR ≤2.5** ([[acg-2023-lgib]]).
- **Adherent clot over an ulcer:** remove it endoscopically to expose and evaluate the underlying stigma, which may require endotherapy. (The Forrest IIb evidence and the competing high-dose-PPI-alone option are on [[upper-gi-bleeding]].)
