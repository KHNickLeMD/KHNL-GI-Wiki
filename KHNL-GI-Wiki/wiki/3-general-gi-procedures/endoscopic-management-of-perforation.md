*What to do at the moment an endoscopist makes a hole — prevention, intraprocedural recognition, and closure, organ by organ. Single home for the size-keyed closure rule; the procedure pages ([[colonoscopy]], [[upper-endoscopy]], [[ercp]], [[polypectomy]], [[poem]]) link here rather than repeating it.*

> ⚠ **Evidence quality is low throughout.** All 16 statements in [[aga-2021-gi-perforations-endoscopic-management|AGA 2021]] are **Best Practice Advice from expert opinion — no GRADE ratings**. The authors state that prospective outcome data on endoscopic therapy of perforations are limited and the literature is retrospective.

## The Two Absolute Surgical Indications

**Go to surgery with no attempt at endoscopic closure** (BPA 15) if the patient is:

1. **Hemodynamically unstable**, **or**
2. Has a **delayed perforation with peritoneal signs or frank peritonitis**.

**And in every other case:** *urgent surgical consultation should be highly considered in **all** perforations — even when the endoscopic repair is technically successful* (BPA 5).

## First-Response Sequence

1. **Inform the staff** — trainees, colleagues, nurses, technicians, anesthesiologists.
2. **Confirm CO₂ insufflation and adjust to low flow** (BPA 3 — CO₂ is encouraged for all endoscopic procedures, and should be used for all of them if available).
3. **Keep the perforation clean** (BPA 2) — aspirate liquids to prevent spillage of GI contents into the defect; if needed **reposition the patient to bring the defect into a non-dependent location**; **minimize insufflation to avoid compartment syndrome**.
4. **Give IV antibiotics covering Gram-negative and anaerobic organisms.**
5. Determine cause and morphology → the **vast majority can be addressed endoscopically if the tools and expertise are available**; a few types are better served by surgery with no endoscopic attempt (above).

- **Prepare before the case:** review surgical history, cross-sectional imaging, and prior endoscopies, and rehearse the planned procedure with the team. **Know your limits** and which cases belong at a high-volume center.

## Closure Device by Defect Size

**Size is the organizing decision of the whole document.** BPA thresholds are the *decision rule*; the body-text ranges below them are *device performance data* — related, but not interchangeable.

| Organ | <1 cm | 1–2 cm | 2–3 cm | >3 cm | Rescue |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Esophagus** (BPA 8) | TTSC or OTSC | TTSC or OTSC | Endoscopic suturing (**>2 cm**) | Endoscopic suturing | Fully covered SEMS **only when primary closure is not possible**; endoscopic vacuum therapy for large/persistent defects and fistulas |
| **Stomach** (BPA 9) | **TTSC optimal** | OTSC preferable (**1–3 cm**) | OTSC preferable | **Endoloop + TTSC** combination, or endoscopic suturing | Omental fat pulled into the lumen and clipped to the defect edges (selected cases) |
| **Duodenum** (BPA 10, 11) | TTSC (**88%–100%** closure for defects <13 mm) | TTSC, OTSC, band ligation, endoloop | Suturing devices | **Type 1 >3 cm → urgent surgical consult** while feasibility of endoscopic closure is assessed | Covered SEMS across the ampulla for **type 2**; enteral SEMS is a last resort |
| **Colon** (BPA 13, 14) | TTSC | TTSC or OTSC | OTSC or suturing | Suturing — **not** in the cecum/right colon | **Right colon and cecum: TTSC only** |

- **TTSC** = through-the-scope clip; **OTSC** = over-the-scope clip; **SEMS** = self-expanding metal stent. Device vocabulary is shared with [[endoscopic-hemostasis]].

## Procedures That Carry Increased Perforation Risk

Know them before the case, so the consent conversation and team preparation happen up front (BPA 4). BPA 1 requires a **thorough discussion with the patient — preferably with family present — of the procedural techniques and risks**, especially for these:

- Any **dilation**; **foreign body removal** ([[ingested-foreign-body]]); any **per-oral endoscopic myotomy** (Zenker's, esophageal, pyloric — [[poem]]); **stricture incision**
- **Thermal coagulation** for hemostasis or tumor ablation; **percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy** ([[enteral-access]]); **ampullectomy**
- **[[endoscopic-mucosal-resection|EMR]]**, **[[endoscopic-submucosal-dissection|ESD]]**, **[[endoscopic-full-thickness-resection|full-thickness endoscopic resection]]**
- **Endoluminal stenting with SEMS** ([[colonic-stenting]])
- **[[ercp|ERCP]] in surgically altered anatomy**; **[[endoscopic-ultrasound|EUS]]-guided biliary and pancreatic access**; **EUS-guided cystogastrostomy**; **endoscopic gastroenterostomy using a LAMS**

## Esophagus

- **Non-iatrogenic causes:** caustic ingestion, sharp penetrating foreign bodies (e.g. fishbone), Boerhaave syndrome.
- **Perforation rates:** esophageal dilation for simple rings or peptic strictures **0.09%–2.2%** (higher for caustic or radiation-induced strictures); **[[pneumatic-dilation|pneumatic dilation]] in [[achalasia]] 0.4%–14%**; EMR in [[barretts-esophagus|Barrett's esophagus]] **0%–3%**.
- **In [[eosinophilic-esophagitis]]:** 10 perforations (**2.0%**) among 511 patients over 14 years; risk factors were a **history of food impaction (OR 14.9, 95% CI 1.7–129.2)** and **a focal stricture (OR 4.6, 95% CI 1.1–19.7)**. In a systematic review of 76 perforations in 70 EoE patients most were **non-iatrogenic**; pushing impacted food into the stomach caused 5 and dilation caused 8; 25/76 required surgery, 10 got a stent, 2 got clips.
- **[[poem|POEM]]:** small intraprocedural mucosal-flap perforations are common and usually inconsequential → TTSC. The dangerous complication is **delayed barrier failure** → leak, mediastinitis, peritonitis; rate **0.2%–1.1%**. Prevention: control energy delivery from the knife tip at all times; maintain orientation in the tunnel (avoid spiraling); avoid excessive forward force tearing the mucosotomy; avoid "counter-coup" flipping of the knife during myotomy. **Prefer posterior orientation where feasible** — 3 of 4 RCTs and a meta-analysis of all 4 showed significantly more mucosal injuries with anterior POEM. ⚠ *Check this preference against the newer POEM and achalasia sources before treating it as the wiki's standing position.* **Stenting after POEM is discouraged** — stent erosion through the myotomy, high migration rate, inadequate seal at the patulous post-POEM EGJ.
- **Clip technique:** begin placement **at the end of the defect distal to the endoscope**, to preserve the view of the remainder.
- **OTSC performance:** effective for **1–2 cm** perforations; systematic-review clinical success **85.3% (399/468)**, with failure driven by **defects >2 cm** and **closure attempted >72 hours** after perforation. Mounting an OTSC increases scope tip diameter to **16.5–21 mm** and the cap edges are not smooth — introduce **gently** through the hypopharynx, especially after head-and-neck surgery or cervical radiation.
- **SEMS:** primary closure is the goal; a fully covered stent diverting flow to allow healing by secondary intention is next best, and gives a longer, more drawn-out course. Diameter **slightly wider than the native esophagus** — seal the defect without expanding it. **Secure the proximal end** by endosuturing or a stent-fixing device to prevent migration — essential in fistula, where there is no stricture to hold the stent.
- **Esophagectomy leaks** (within days of operation): attempt primary closure with TTSC, OTSC, or endosuturing, **plus** a fully covered stent. **Esophageal fistula:** TTSC or endosuturing alone is usually ineffective → fully covered stent with a snug fit.
- **Endoscopic vacuum therapy** — polyurethane sponge on a nasogastric tube inserted into the defect. Review of 11 studies, 210 patients: **90% healing for anastomotic leaks, 96% closure for esophageal perforations**; median follow-up 235 days, median therapy duration **17 days**.

## Stomach

- **Non-iatrogenic causes:** peptic ulcer disease, caustic ingestion. **High-risk procedures:** EMR and ESD.
- **The proximal gastric wall is thinner than the antrum** — extra caution for proximal resections.
- **Gastric ESD perforation rate 3.02% (512/16,941)**, of which **98% (506/512) recovered without surgery.** Most EMR/ESD perforations are intraprocedural; **delayed perforation 0.4%–0.7%**.
- **ESD perforation risk factors:** lesion location (**upper third, middle third, greater curvature**); **lesion size >20 mm**; depth of invasion; **submucosal fibrosis**; endoscopist's prior workload; **procedure length >60–120 min**; octogenarian patients; **piecemeal resection**. Perform lengthy procedures under **general anesthesia**.
- **Abdominal compartment rescue:** if the gastric lumen will not insufflate and the abdomen is tense, place a **16–18 gauge angiocath percutaneously into the peritoneum** to decompress and recover luminal insufflation. Percuss the left upper and mid-to-low abdomen for a tympanitic area; for a **duodenal bulb** perforation the entry point is roughly **3–5 cm below the umbilicus**. Clean with alcohol; introduce the needle slowly, monitoring air flow.
- **TTSC technique:** apply **suction before placing each clip** for tighter approximation.
- **OTSC caveats — you get one attempt, so aim precisely.** The **Twin Grasper** (two independently opening jaws) grabs one edge, pulls it across, then grasps the opposing edge; pull both into the suction cap, apply suction, then fire. OTSC may inadvertently capture adjacent organs or vessels beyond the GI wall. The **remOVE** cutting device can remove a deployed OTSC but has a **10–15 minute warm-up**, so re-deploying after a misfire costs significant time.
- Combined **OTSC + TTSC** closed post-ESD mucosal defects in **91.3% (11/12)**, facilitating early discharge.
- **>3 cm:** endoloop/TTSC — TTSCs placed at equal distance to fix a detachable snare around the defect, then tighten the snare (double-channel scope). Alternatively **endoscopic suturing**, which obtains **full-thickness purchases** and is arguably superior for large defects — succeeded in **31/31 patients** (58.1% gastric, 41.9% rectal ESD) with no delayed bleeding or perforation.

## Duodenum and Periampullary

**Why the duodenum is the highest-risk site:** thin wall; the anatomic challenge of the C-loop; paradoxical endoscope motion; and **luminal exposure to pancreatic proteolytic enzymes**, which pool in the duodenum and break down exposed muscle at an EMR/ESD base — the mechanism of **delayed** perforation.

- **Iatrogenic causes:** duodenal stricture dilation, EMR (including ampullectomy), ESD, EUS, [[ercp|ERCP]].
- **ERCP perforation:** overall risk **<1%**, with **mortality 7.8%–9.9%**. Mechanisms: duodenoscope-related trauma, sphincterotomy, intraductal guidewire manipulation. **Risk factors:** older age, longer procedure duration, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, **non-dilated bile duct**, performance of a sphincterotomy/precut sphincterotomy, access papillotomy, and **altered anatomy** (e.g. Billroth II partial gastrectomy).
- **Surgically altered anatomy tip:** start with a standard **forward-viewing upper endoscope** to identify and mark the entrance of the pancreatobiliary limb before advancing the side-viewing duodenoscope.
- **Large duodenal polyps** (BPA 12): **EMR only by experienced endoscopists, ESD only by experts** — both require proficiency in resection *and* mucosal-defect closure to manage immediate and delayed perforation.

**Type 1 — lateral duodenal wall tear**

- Excessive shearing force or angle-related trauma from the **shaft or tip of the duodenoscope**; intraperitoneal or retroperitoneal leakage. Most often during scope advancement where the shaft presses on the duodenal wall or a periampullary diverticulum, or on forceful retraction during difficult stone extraction. Often **>3 cm**.
- **Presentation:** sudden bleeding, lumen deflation, difficulty maintaining insufflation.
- **Usually requires surgery** unless early endoscopic closure succeeds. **>3 cm → urgent surgical consultation** while feasibility of endoscopic closure is assessed (BPA 10).
- Small perforations (**<13 mm**) close with clips in **88%–100%**; band ligation and endoloops also reported. Where endoscopic closure succeeds, **>90%** recover clinically without surgery.
- Enteral SEMS is the last endoscopic resort — ⚠ **covered duodenal stents were not available in the US** as of 2021 (time-limited statement); fully covered through-the-scope esophageal stents have been used with proximal suturing to prevent migration. **Uncovered SEMS should be avoided.**

**Type 2 — periampullary (retroperitoneal)**

- Caused by **overextension of a sphincterotomy beyond the intraduodenal portion of the ampulla**.
- **Prevention:** minimize the length of cutting wire in contact with the ampulla; use **stepwise incisions**; direct the force and direction of the cut with the **right hand on the scope shaft creating counter-clockwise torque**, rather than lifting the elevator or bowing the sphincterotome; **terminate the sphincterotomy just before the flat transverse duodenal mucosa cephalad of the papillary mound**; use newer generators with controlled, pulsed-blended current to reduce "zipper cuts."
- **Detection is the problem — these are subtle and easily missed** (BPA 11): carefully assess the **fluoroscopic gas pattern under the liver or around the right kidney**, and **request a CT** if concerned.
- ⚠ **Asymptomatic retroperitoneal air after ERCP may need no intervention** — **14% (7/50)** of post-ERCP patients had CT retroperitoneal air with uneventful recovery. But type 2 perforations can still lead to retroperitoneal abscess, peritonitis, and death.
- **Closure:** TTSCs if feasible, and/or a **fully covered SEMS into the bile duct across the ampulla** — likely to seal the defect with high clinical success.
- **Course:** patients declare within **24–48 h** whether they need surgery (worsening abdominal pain or systemic inflammatory response). If improving, at **2–4 days** obtain a water-soluble upper GI series or CT with oral contrast to confirm no extravasation before starting clear liquids.
- **Delayed recognition >6 hours after ERCP** is associated with increased length of stay, increased mortality, and more complicated surgery.
- ⚠ **Types 3 and 4 of this classification are not printed by the source** and are not on the wiki — do not add them from memory.

**Duodenal resection defects**

- Closure modalities: TTSC, OTSC, endosuturing, polymer gels or sheets, TTSC + endoloop cinching, and covering the defect with **Vicryl mesh** affixed by TTSCs.
- If a large post-EMR duodenal defect **cannot** be closed → **admit with close observation for delayed perforation**.
- **Nasopancreatic tube** placement may prevent delayed perforation by diverting pancreatic secretions away from the defect — weigh against the risks of duodenoscope passage, pancreatitis, and drain dislodgement.

## Colon

- **Non-iatrogenic causes:** [[diverticulitis]], [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease]], rarely colonic obstruction. **Iatrogenic:** endoscopic resection, hot forceps biopsy, stricture dilation (Crohn's, radiation), colonic stenting, and **forceful scope advancement** across angulations, into diverticula, or without scope straightening.
- **Rates:** stricture dilation in [[crohns-disease|Crohn's disease]] **3%**; colonoscopy with [[polypectomy|polypectomy]] **0.8 per 1000**; EMR of large colon polyps **1.5% (96/6595)**.
- **Site distribution** (systematic review, 19 studies, 744 colon perforations) — **the sigmoid dominates because of direct trauma from forceful scope advancement** (BPA 13):

| Site | Share |
|---|---|
| **Sigmoid** | **63.8%** (310) |
| Cecum | 10% (49) |
| Transverse | 9% (44) |
| Rectum | 7% (34) |
| Ascending | 5.6% (27) |
| Descending | 4.4% (21) |
| Hepatic flexure | 0.2% (1) |

- **[[colonic-stenting|Colorectal stenting]] perforation by scenario** (meta-analysis, 4086 patients): overall **7.4%**; **post-stent dilation 20.4%**; **bevacizumab-based therapy 12.5%**; chemotherapy without bevacizumab **7.0%**. A tertiary cancer-center series of 199 SEMS placements reported **2.0%**. **Age >70 y and sigmoid location** were associated with early perforation after SEMS (n=474).
- **EMR technique to avoid colon perforation — the five-step sequence:**
  1. Use a submucosal injection solution **more viscous than normal saline** — succinylated gelatin, hydroxyethyl starch, or glycerol.
  2. Consider **partially reopening and closing the snare before resection** to let the muscularis propria fall back down.
  3. **Inspect the resection base** for muscularis propria injury — look for the **target sign**.
  4. **Recognize and close muscularis propria injury or perforation early.**
  5. When submucosal injection fails to lift the polyp, consider **full-thickness resection** with a full-thickness resection device.
- **Management:** intraprocedural perforation in a **stable** patient with a **clean** site and **no spillage** → attempt endoscopic closure. A **rectal** perforation gives the most time and space for TTSC, OTSC, or suturing. In the systematic review, endoscopic clipping was used in **114 cases (15.4%)** and conservative management in **71 (9.1%)**; the remainder were surgical.
- **Cecum / tortuous or unclean colon → TTSC only** (BPA 14). Withdrawing the colonoscope to fit an OTSC may be difficult and dangerous, and a suturing device may not reach. *(A newer suture-based device allowing deep submucosal and intramuscular fixation through a standard gastroscope or colonoscope is noted by the source.)*
- **Large sigmoid tears from blunt scope trauma**, hemodynamic instability, or delayed perforation with peritoneal signs → **surgery** (BPA 15).

## Aftercare and Documentation

**All upper GI perforations** (BPA 6) — consider admission for observation, plus:

- **IV fluids**, **NPO**, **broad-spectrum antibiotics** (Gram-negative and anaerobic coverage), **nasogastric tube** (with some exceptions), and **surgical consultation**.
- **Before starting clear liquids**, consider a **water-soluble upper GI series** to confirm the absence of a continuing leak at the perforation site (BPA 7).

**Documentation (BPA 16)** — in any adverse event including perforation it is paramount to ensure **accurate documentation**, **prompt discussion with the patient and family**, and **swift reporting to the institution's quality officer (or equivalent) and risk management team** (in major adverse events).
