## Diet by Clinical State

| State | Approach |
|---|---|
| Remission / mild symptoms | Mediterranean diet; reintroduce fiber and produce (soluble fiber preferred); no fiber restriction unless stricture |
| Symptomatic flare | Short-term reduced-fiber, low-residue ± low-FODMAP (fermentable oligo-, di-, and monosaccharides and polyols); return to Mediterranean once symptoms resolve |
| Symptomatic stricture | Texture modification — careful chewing, cooking/blending produce to a soft, less fibrous consistency (applesauce vs unpeeled raw apple) |
| Active CD — induction | Exclusive enteral nutrition ([[#EEN]]) as a steroid-sparing bridge, or Crohn's disease exclusion diet ([[#CDED (Partial Enteral Nutrition)]]) |
| Malnourished CD before elective surgery | [[#Preoperative EEN]] ("prehabilitation") to optimize nutrition and reduce postoperative complications |
| Intra-abdominal abscess / phlegmon limiting intake via the gut | Short-term parenteral nutrition (PN) for preoperative bowel rest — decreases infection and inflammation as a bridge to definitive surgery |
| Intestinal failure, [[short-bowel-syndrome\|short bowel syndrome (SBS)]], high-output fistula | [[#Parenteral Nutrition & Short Bowel Syndrome]] |

## Background / Maintenance Diet
- **Mediterranean diet** for all patients unless contraindicated: variety of fresh fruits and vegetables; fat mainly from fish, olive oil, nuts, seeds; complex carbohydrates; lean protein (low-fat dairy, poultry, fish, shellfish, beans, legumes); **low in ultraprocessed foods, added sugar, salt**; red meat infrequent and in small quantity.
- Benefits beyond IBD — reduced cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, cancer; may improve gut microbiome and metabolome diversity. Which components drive the IBD benefit is unknown.
- **No diet consistently lowers the flare rate in adults.** Low red/processed meat may reduce [[ulcerative-colitis|ulcerative colitis (UC)]] flares, but has not been shown to reduce CD relapse.
- Ultraprocessed foods and sugar-sweetened beverages → higher risk of developing IBD and a more severe multiyear course (**CD strongest association**); low dietary fiber intake also linked to incident CD.
- **No** gluten avoidance absent [[celiac-disease|celiac disease]] or suspected gluten sensitivity.
- **Low-FODMAP:** improves [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)]]-like symptoms but depletes butyrate-producing organisms — the same organisms whose abundance tracks with endoscopic and clinical remission → **short-term flare use only**.
- **Breastfeeding** associated with lower risk of childhood-diagnosed IBD; protection strongest with ≥12 mo.

> [!pearl] Fiber restriction is for strictures and flares, not for life
> Patients in remission without strictures need no fiber limit. With a symptomatic stricture, careful chewing plus cooking or blending produce to a soft consistency (applesauce vs an unpeeled apple) puts plant foods and fiber back on the plate ([[aga-2024-diet-nutrition-ibd]]).

### Diets Studied in IBD

| Diet | Description / rationale | Comments |
|---|---|---|
| Mediterranean | Plant-focused — whole grains, fruits, vegetables; fat from fish, olive oil, nuts, seeds; lean protein; red meat at much lower frequency and quantity | Similar efficacy to the specific carbohydrate diet for symptomatic remission and calprotectin response in mild–moderate CD (DINE-CD); aligns with a moderate- to high-fiber diet in remission |
| Specific carbohydrate | Nutritionally complete, grain-free, low in sugar and lactose; restricts hard-to-digest carbohydrates (no grains, high-fructose corn syrup, high-lactose dairy, starchy vegetables, sugars other than honey) | Challenging to follow; limited large-scale evidence of benefit |
| Low-FODMAP | Elimination–rechallenge: FODMAPs omitted for up to 8 wk, then reintroduced one at a time | Worth trying with concomitant IBS-like symptoms; not long-term |
| CDED | Whole-foods diet limiting foods thought to harm the microbiome or intestinal barrier; 3 phases of 6 wk each, each including partial enteral nutrition (PEN) — liquid formula by mouth or enterically infused | Mild–moderate CD with short flare duration; allows some solid food (vs 100% liquid EEN) → may improve compliance |

## Enteral Nutrition Therapies (Crohn's)

### EEN
- Entire caloric intake from commercially available oral liquid meal replacements, **all other food excluded**, typically **6–8 wk**; usually taken by mouth.
- Effective induction of **clinical remission and endoscopic response** in CD — **stronger evidence in children than adults**. Routinely offered as **first-line steroid-sparing therapy in pediatric CD**, remission **60–80%** (similar to corticosteroids).
- Adults: may be considered a **steroid-sparing bridge**; effective when tolerated. Limited uptake reflects product fatigue and poor adherence, plus difficulty recruiting to trials.
- Standard **polymeric** formulas, generally well tolerated; **no evidence favors any one product** (eg, Ensure Plus, Kate Farms, Jevity). Choose for nutritional balance and individualize calories.

### CDED (Partial Enteral Nutrition)
- Whole-foods diet plus PEN; effective induction for **mild–moderate CD of relatively short duration**. Required foods are low in fiber, taurine, and saturated fat.
- **Phase 1 (wk 1–6) — 50% of calories from formula:** mandatory fish, chicken breast, eggs; allows rice, cooled potatoes, tomatoes, onion, garlic, ginger, olive and canola oil; limited quantities of cucumber, carrots, spinach, lettuce, banana, apple, avocado, strawberry, melon, citrus juice.
- **Phase 2 (wk 7–12) — 25% of calories from PEN:** adds tuna, whole-grain bread, oats, yams, red peppers; certain vegetables, beans, peas, turnips, parsnips after wk 10.
- **Phase 3 (wk 13 onward) — maintenance, 25% from PEN:** adds more seafood, eggs, cocoa, coffee, grains, some dairy, alcohol if tolerated.
- Initial studies used the Modulen IBD formula; alternative oral nutrition supplements are sometimes substituted.
- In children: **better tolerated than EEN and equally effective** for inducing clinical remission by wk 6; 1–2 wk of EEN followed by CDED had efficacy comparable to EEN alone. May work as **salvage after biologic loss of response**.
- Monitor for nutritional deficiencies on any of these diets.

### Preoperative EEN
- In **malnourished CD before elective surgery**: improves disease activity, C-reactive protein (CRP), and nutritional status, with postoperative complication rates comparable to well-nourished patients — nutritional "prehabilitation."
- Independent protective factor against **anastomotic leak, intra-abdominal abscess, surgical site infection, ileus, unplanned stoma, reoperation**.
- Oral EEN when feasible; infusion through an [[enteral-access|enteral access device]] for more severe malnutrition, especially with elemental formulas.
- Data on enteral nutrition (EN) in active **UC** are limited — safe and well tolerated in severe acute flares, can improve prealbumin.

## Parenteral Nutrition & Short Bowel Syndrome
- **EN preferred over PN whenever the gut is accessible and safe to use** — maintains gut integrity and function, feeds the microbiota, fewer infectious complications. Can be combined with PN to reach nutritional goals.
- **PN indications:** intestinal failure; malnutrition with a clear contraindication to EN, or after failed EN trials; insufficient bowel function to maintain or restore nutritional status; nil per os (NPO) ≥7 d before surgery; EN not feasible or insufficient to meet total needs for ≥7–10 d; intra-abdominal abscess/phlegmon needing short-term preoperative bowel rest.
- **Thresholds that trigger PN:** high-output intestinal fistula **>500 mL/24 h**; [[ostomy-management|high ostomy output]] **>2000 mL/24 h**; inability to maintain **>60% of energy and protein goals** by mouth or EN for **7–10 d**.
- **Long-term PN risks:** central line–associated bloodstream infection, thrombotic vascular complications, PN-associated liver disease, hypertriglyceridemia, cholestasis.
- **SBS:** intestinal adaptation takes **1–2 yr** to reach maximum effect (better absorption, slower transit); ~**50% of adults and 73% of children** wean off PN. Aid weaning with "dry meals" (separate the bulk of liquids from solids), avoiding high-osmotic sugar-sweetened beverages, timing antidiarrheals with enteral feeding, and **[[glp-2-agonists|glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) agonists]]** — increase villous height and crypt depth and slow gastrointestinal (GI) motility.
- Persistent dehydration risk on long-term management → customized **intravenous (IV) electrolyte solutions and/or oral rehydration solutions** (water, sugar, salt) in place of calorie-containing PN, with oral intake and enteric stimulation continued whenever possible.
- Interdisciplinary team — physicians, midlevel providers, pharmacists, and **registered dietitians (RDs)** with intestinal-failure and PN expertise.

## Malnutrition Screening
- **Screen every patient with IBD** at diagnosis and routinely during long-term management: unintended weight loss, edema and fluid retention, fat and muscle mass loss → full evaluation by an RD when any is observed. Recommended by the European Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ESPEN), the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, and the American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN).
- **Drivers:** decreased oral intake, increased energy/protein requirements, increased GI losses from inflammation, malabsorption, disease activity, SBS, medications.
- Malnutrition odds **5.57× higher** in IBD vs non-IBD inpatients; **16%** prevalence in IBD outpatient clinics, **56.8%** of them CD. Associated with more emergency department visits, more and longer hospitalizations, nonelective surgery, higher mortality, reduced response to medical therapy, poor quality of life.
- **RD co-management** for all complicated IBD — malnutrition, SBS, enterocutaneous fistula, complex nutrition therapy (PN, EN, EEN), or on CDED; **all newly diagnosed patients should have access to an RD**. RDs also prevent extraintestinal complications (eg, diet modification to prevent enteric hyperoxaluria and kidney stones).

> [!pitfall] Serum proteins do not diagnose malnutrition
> [[albumin|Albumin]] and prealbumin lack specificity for nutritional status and are highly sensitive to inflammation — no longer recommended for identifying or diagnosing malnutrition. Hypoalbuminemia is still useful as a risk marker: higher risk of surgical complications and diminished response to [[anti-tnf-agents|anti–tumor necrosis factor (anti-TNF)]] therapy ([[aga-2024-diet-nutrition-ibd]]).

### Diagnostic Criteria

| Criteria set | Diagnosis requires |
|---|---|
| Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics / ASPEN consensus | **≥2 of 6:** clinically significant weight loss · reduced energy intake · loss of lean mass · loss of subcutaneous fat mass · fluid accumulation · diminished functional status on formal grip-strength testing |
| Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) | **≥1 phenotypic criterion** (clinically significant weight loss, low body mass index [BMI], reduced muscle mass) **plus ≥1 etiologic criterion** (reduced food consumption, impaired assimilation of nutrients, inflammation) |
| ESPEN | **BMI <18.5 kg/m²** |
| Abridged patient-generated subjective global assessment (PG-SGA) | Validated IBD-specific malnutrition **screening** tool (patient-completed) |

Generic inpatient screening tools (Nutritional Risk Screening 2002, Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool) and their cut-points are on [[nutrition-in-hospitalized-patients]].

## Micronutrient Monitoring

| Nutrient | Monitor in | Labs and interval | Repletion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vitamin D | All patients with IBD; deficiency more often in CD | — | — |
| [[iron-deficiency-anemia\|Iron]] | All patients with IBD, regardless of age | Complete blood count + ferritin + transferrin saturation + CRP; **q6–12 mo** in remission or mild disease, **q3 mo** if active | IV iron more effective, faster response, often better tolerated; oral reasonable in mild anemia or clinically inactive disease; dose by baseline hemoglobin and body weight |
| Vitamin B12 | Extensive ileal disease, terminal ileal resection **>30 cm**, ileoanal pouch (bacterial overgrowth) | **Yearly** if high risk or with clinical features — fatigue, anemia, loss of appetite, weight loss, glossitis, neuropathy; serum levels may overestimate body stores | **1000 µg intramuscular (IM) or subcutaneous (SC) q1–4 wk for life** — IM gives more rapid uptake, SC is more comfortable and self-administered; both preferred over sublingual for long-term repletion |

- [[aga-2024-diet-nutrition-ibd|AGA 2024]] gives no vitamin D testing interval or repletion dose. Untreated deficiency causes loss of bone mineral density and metabolic bone disease; bone-densitometry indications for the steroid-exposed patient with IBD are on [[ibd-preventive-care]].
- Also consider **zinc, copper, folate** (especially on [[methotrexate]] or [[mesalamine-5-asa|sulfasalazine]]) and **fat-soluble vitamins** by risk factor. Many micronutrients are acute-phase reactants — ferritin and copper rise with inflammation, zinc, folate, and selenium fall — so check levels in quiescent disease and recheck after repleting.
