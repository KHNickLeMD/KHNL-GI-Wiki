Extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs) of [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]] may occur in **every system of the body** ([[ecco-2024-eim-ibd|European Crohn's and Colitis Organisation (ECCO) 2024]]). **The category an EIM falls into predicts whether controlling the bowel disease will control it** — that is the decision this page turns on.

Entities with their own pages are linked out; this page carries the framework, the monitoring schedules, and the management that has no other owner.

## Classification

| Category | Definition | Named examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Classical** | Inflammatory process occurring at a **distant site** | Erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum, episcleritis, uveitis, peripheral and axial spondyloarthropathy, [[primary-sclerosing-cholangitis\|primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)]], [[autoimmune-hepatitis\|autoimmune hepatitis (AIH)]] |
| **Associations** | Association with **other immune-mediated disorders** | Multiple sclerosis, interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, ischaemic heart disease, myocarditis, pericarditis, cerebrovascular accident |
| **Complications** | Complications of **systemic inflammation** | Venous thromboembolism (VTE), anaemia, osteoporosis, fatigue |
| **Treatment** | **Side effects of IBD therapy** | Pancreatitis, drug-induced skin lesions, drug-induced liver injury |

> [!pearl] The category tells you whether treating the gut is the treatment
> Reactive and classical EIMs such as erythema nodosum track intestinal disease activity and resolve when it is controlled. Associations (multiple sclerosis, bronchiectasis) and treatment-related manifestations do not — chasing the bowel will not fix them ([[ecco-2024-eim-ibd]]).

## Venous Thromboembolism

**Thrombotic events are more than twice as frequent in IBD than in the general population**, with similar prevalence in [[ulcerative-colitis|ulcerative colitis (UC)]] and [[crohns-disease|Crohn's disease (CD)]] *[EL2]*. The most important risk factors are **active disease, hospitalization, and surgery** *[EL2]* ([[ecco-2024-eim-ibd]] Statement 1).

**Prophylaxis** (Statement 2.1 *[EL1/EL3/EL4]*):

| Setting | Prophylaxis |
|---|---|
| **Hospitalized for acute medical illness or major surgery** | **Prophylactic-dose low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) or fondaparinux** *[EL1]* — LMWH preferred over unfractionated heparin in acutely and critically ill patients |
| **After a major surgical procedure** | Prophylactic-dose LMWH **until at least 3 weeks post-discharge** *[EL3]* |
| **Post-hospitalization, or ambulatory with a severe IBD flare** | Thromboprophylaxis **can be considered** *[EL4]* |

- In-hospital pharmacological thromboprophylaxis is associated with a **54% decreased risk of VTE** after discharge — the reason **all** IBD patients hospitalized for any cause, flare or surgery, should receive it.

**Treatment of an acute event** (Statement 2.2 *[EL1]*):

- **Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) at therapeutic dose** are recommended; LMWH is an alternative in the acute phase.
- **Investigate risk factors for VTE at the time of the event to guide the duration of anticoagulation** — recurrence risk depends on whether a modifiable risk factor is present.
- DOACs may be of particular benefit in **portal or cerebral venous thrombosis**: a **fourfold greater chance of complete radiological resolution**, better recanalization, and a favourable safety profile versus warfarin. See [[portal-vein-thrombosis]].

## Cardiovascular Risk

- **Marginally increased risk**, independent of IBD therapy, of **cerebrovascular accident** *[EL2]*, **ischaemic heart disease** *[EL2]*, **mesenteric ischaemia** *[EL4]*, **atrial fibrillation** *[EL3]* and **heart failure** *[EL3]*. **No difference in cardiovascular mortality** *[EL2]*. Risk is more pronounced in **CD, in women, and at younger age**; peripheral arterial disease data are conflicting (Statement 3).
- **Small increased risk of myocarditis and pericarditis**, with low absolute incidence — **8.3 per 100 000 patient-years in CD** and **2.6 per 100 000 patient-years in UC** (Statement 4 *[EL4]*).
- ⚠ **Myocarditis has been associated with mesalazine and [[anti-tnf-agents|tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) antagonists]]**; on the available data it is not possible to say whether the risk is driven by the drugs or by IBD itself. **Pericarditis, by contrast, is almost universally therapy-driven**, including by TNFα antagonists, and **resolves completely after treatment withdrawal**.

## Anaemia

**One of the most common EIMs of IBD.** World Health Organization (WHO) definitions apply: haemoglobin **<130 g/L for adult men**, **<120 g/L for adult non-pregnant women**, **<110 g/L for children aged 6–59 months**. Iron deficiency and anaemia of chronic disease are the most frequent types (Statement 5.1 *[EL4]*).

Diagnostic criteria, monitoring intervals, retreatment thresholds and the route-of-iron decision live on **[[iron-deficiency-anemia]]**.

- **Vitamin B12 and folate:** patients at risk — especially **small-bowel CD or resection**, and anyone with **macrocytosis** — should have serum levels **screened at least annually** (Statement 5.3 *[EL4]*).

---

## Pulmonary

**Increased risk of bronchiectasis, interstitial lung disease, and granulomatous lung disease** (Statement 7 *[EL4]*). Data for other pulmonary conditions such as asthma are conflicting; ECCO 2024 makes no treatment recommendation for pulmonary EIMs.

## Hepatobiliary

### Liver Test Monitoring

**Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), γ-glutamyltransferase (GGT) and total serum bilirubin (TSB) should be measured in the treatment-naive patient with suspected IBD, then at 6-month intervals throughout follow-up** (Statement 8 *[EL4]*).

**How far the elevation is from normal sets when the aetiology screen happens** ([[ecco-2024-eim-ibd]] Table 3):

| Grading | ALT | ALP | GGT | TSB | Timing of aetiology screen |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mild** | >1–3× ULN | >1–2.5× ULN | >1–2.5× ULN | >1–1.5× ULN | **Delay 1 month** |
| **Moderate** | 3–5× ULN | 2.5–5× ULN | 2.5–5× ULN | 1.5–3× ULN | **Immediately** |
| **Marked** | >5× ULN | >5× ULN | >5× ULN | >3× ULN | **Immediately** |

*ULN = upper limit of normal*

- Adults with IBD carry an estimated **5% risk of an immune-related liver disease** (PSC, AIH, or both), more common in children.
- **One-third** of IBD patients have [[nafld-masld|nonalcoholic fatty liver disease]] — **twice** the prevalence of healthy controls.
- Most drugs used to treat IBD can cause liver injury; see [[drug-induced-liver-injury]].

### PSC and AIH in IBD

| | PSC | AIH |
|---|---|---|
| **Prevalence in IBD** | **2–8%**, especially in UC (Statement 9) | **<0.5%** in adults, higher in children and adolescents (Statement 12) |
| **When to investigate** | **Persistent elevation of cholestatic liver enzymes** plus **symptoms of cholestasis such as pruritus**, or **prominent perihilar lymphadenopathy** → **high-quality magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)** *[EL2]* | Where AIH and IBD coexist, investigate **additional sclerosing cholangitis with high-quality MRCP** *[EL3]* |
| **Key management point** | **Refer to a hepatologist with expertise in PSC at diagnosis; joint care continues thereafter** *[EL5]* (Statement 11) | **Thiopurines should be considered first-line for maintenance of remission** in IBD-AIH *[EL2]* (Statement 12) |

- Full disease-script detail: [[primary-sclerosing-cholangitis]], [[autoimmune-hepatitis]].
- **In adolescents with IBD the odds ratio for AIH was 8 in UC and 4 in CD**; **2–7%** of AIH patients carry a concurrent PSC diagnosis, and **up to 10%** of adult AIH patients show concurrent features of PSC.

## Pancreatitis

**Acute pancreatitis in IBD is commonly associated with gallstone disease, alcohol intake, medications ([[thiopurines]] and [[mesalamine-5-asa|5-aminosalicylates]]), and duodenal CD. [[autoimmune-pancreatitis|Autoimmune pancreatitis]] may also occur** (Statement 13 *[EL3]*).

- Pooled annual incidence of [[acute-pancreatitis]] in IBD: **210 per 100 000 patient-years**.
- Autoimmune pancreatitis prevalence is **three times higher** in IBD (odds ratio 3.11, 95% confidence interval 2.93–3.30), and higher in CD than UC (odds ratio **4.12 vs 2.61**).
- **Drug-induced pancreatitis from thiopurines** develops in **4–7.5%** of azathioprine-treated patients, is **dose-independent**, and typically occurs **within the first 3–4 weeks**; smoking and female sex are risk factors. 5-ASA–induced pancreatitis is also not dose-related and can present at any time.
- ⚠ **Do not re-challenge** — thiopurines and 5-ASAs should be **immediately stopped** in drug-induced pancreatitis and re-challenge should not be attempted.

> [!pitfall] A raised lipase in IBD is usually not pancreatitis
> **Asymptomatic increases in serum amylase, lipase or both occur in 8–21% of IBD patients**, and recurrent abdominal pain is itself common in IBD. ECCO states these tests **should not be requested in the absence of symptoms of pancreatitis** ([[ecco-2024-eim-ibd]]).

## Metabolic Bone Disease

**Patients with IBD have an increased risk of developing osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures, particularly of the spine** *[EL2]*. **In patients with IBD at high risk for osteoporosis, bone mineral density measurement by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) is recommended** *[EL2]* (Statement 14).

- **WHO thresholds:** **T-score below −2.5 standard deviations (SD)** is diagnostic of **osteoporosis**; **T-score between −1 and −2.5 SD** is **osteopenia**.
- **Magnitude:** **32%** increased risk of osteoporosis or osteoporotic fracture (odds ratio 1.32, 95% confidence interval 1.20–1.45); incidence odds ratio **2.9 in CD** and **2.8 in UC**; **two-fold** increased spinal and **three-fold** increased hip fracture risk.
- **Risk factors for low bone mineral density:** systemic inflammation · frequent [[corticosteroids-ibd|corticosteroid]] use · low body mass index · CD diagnosis · smoking · malabsorption of vitamins D and K and calcium · malnutrition · reduced physical activity · genetic factors.

**Management** (Statement 15):

| Intervention | Who |
|---|---|
| **Daily dietary calcium and vitamin D, smoking cessation, physical activity** | **All patients**, to prevent osteopenia and osteoporosis *[EL5]* |
| **Calcium and vitamin D supplementation** | Patients **treated with corticosteroids**, and/or with **known osteopenia or risk factors for low bone mineral density** *[EL2]* |
| **Bisphosphonates** | Patients with **corticosteroid-induced or postmenopausal osteoporosis** *[EL1]* |

- Bisphosphonate tolerability is generally good and adverse effects usually temporary (abdominal pain, diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, rash, muscle pain); **osteonecrosis of the jaw is rare (<1% of recipients)**. A meta-analysis suggests **zoledronate is the most effective agent for increasing bone mineral density**, while **risedronate has the most favourable safety profile**.

## Joint Disease

**Clinical symptoms of joint disease in IBD are common**, including peripheral and axial spondyloarthropathy; **articular symptoms may occur before as well as after the IBD diagnosis** (Statement 16 *[EL2]*). The Assessment of SpondyloArthritis Society (ASAS) criteria distinguish **axial** (back pain, morning spinal stiffness) from **peripheral** (upper and lower limb) spondyloarthritis.

- **Prevalence in IBD:** peripheral arthritis **13%** (95% confidence interval 12–15%), sacroiliitis **10%** (8–12%), ankylosing spondylitis **3%** (2–4%). Radiological sacroiliitis pooled prevalence **21%** (17–26%).
- **Lifetime IBD risk in spondyloarthritis is 4–14%**; ASAS includes IBD as a classification criterion.

**Agent choice by pattern** ([[ecco-2024-eim-ibd]] Table 7; green = can be used, yellow = may be used, red = should not be used):

| Agent | Axial spondyloarthropathy | Non-axial (peripheral) spondyloarthropathy |
|---|---|---|
| Sulfasalazine | **Should not be used** | May be used |
| Methotrexate | **Should not be used** | **Can be used** |
| [[anti-tnf-agents\|TNFα antagonist]]¹ | **Can be used** | **Can be used** |
| [[jak-inhibitors\|JAK inhibitor]] | May be used | May be used |
| [[vedolizumab\|Vedolizumab]] (anti-integrin) | **Should not be used** | **Should not be used** |
| Ustekinumab ([[il-23-and-il-12-23-inhibitors\|anti-IL-12/23]]) | **Should not be used** | May be used |
| Ozanimod (S1P-receptor modulator) | **Should not be used** | **Should not be used** |

*¹ Does not apply to etanercept*

- **Statement 18 *[EL2]*:** TNFα antagonists are recommended for **axial** spondyloarthropathy associated with IBD; **vedolizumab and ustekinumab are not recommended** in axial spondyloarthropathy. No randomized trial has evaluated any biologic, JAK inhibitor or NSAID in this setting.
- **Statement 19 *[EL2]/[EL3]*:** TNFα antagonists are recommended for **non-axial** spondyloarthropathy; there are also data supporting **methotrexate, sulfasalazine, and ustekinumab**. This guidance treats peripheral arthritis as an **enteroarthropathy**, not as coexisting rheumatoid or psoriatic arthropathy.
- ⚠ **Etanercept causes paradoxical gastrointestinal inflammation in a subset of patients and should be avoided in the IBD population.**
- **Management should involve both a gastroenterologist and a rheumatologist**, since choice of immunosuppression varies with whether intestinal or peripheral arthritic symptoms predominate.

**NSAIDs** (Statement 17):

- **There is no evidence of an association between NSAID use and UC flare** *[EL1]*, **although there is potentially an association with CD flare** *[EL2]*.
- **The decision to use an NSAID for arthropathy is made case by case** *[EL3]*; **selective cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitors may be used for short periods** *[EL2]*.
- A meta-analysis of 13 studies and 6276 participants found no consistent association (CD relative risk 1.42, 95% confidence interval 0.65–3.09; UC relative risk 1.52, 0.87–2.63).

## Skin and Orofacial

**Approximately 15–20% of patients with IBD will develop cutaneous EIMs.** Their relationship with the bowel disease may be **specific**, **reactive**, **associated**, or **treatment-related** — **early involvement of a dermatologist is recommended when appropriate** (Statement 20 *[EL3]*).

| Entity | Category | Management ([[ecco-2024-eim-ibd]]) |
|---|---|---|
| **Erythema nodosum** | Reactive | **Control the underlying intestinal disease** — the skin lesions usually resolve when bowel activity is controlled; exclude and manage other causes *[EL3]* (Statement 21) |
| **Pyoderma gangrenosum** | Reactive | **TNFα antagonists, particularly infliximab**, with **early use** for severe disease *[EL2]*. Also: systemic steroids, ciclosporin *[EL3]*, ustekinumab *[EL4]*, dapsone, metronidazole *[EL3]*, tetracyclines *[EL4]*. **Milder disease:** topical steroids and topical calcineurin inhibitors *[EL3]* (Statement 22) |
| **Sweet syndrome** | Reactive | **Systemic corticosteroids first-line** *[EL4]*; **TNFα antagonists for steroid-dependent or refractory cases** *[EL4]* (Statement 23) |
| **Hidradenitis suppurativa** | Associated | Mild-to-moderate: topical or systemic treatment (antibiotics, dapsone). **Severe: adalimumab first-line, with early dose intensification frequently required.** Also infliximab, ustekinumab, or surgery *[EL3]* (Statement 24) |
| **Metastatic CD** | Specific | Systemic and intralesional corticosteroids, azathioprine, metronidazole, TNFα antagonists *[EL3]* (Statement 25) |
| **Orofacial** (aphthous ulcers, periodontitis, pyostomatitis vegetans, orofacial granulomatosis) | Reactive / specific | **Manage the underlying IBD plus supportive mouth care, including topical steroids** *[EL5]* (Statement 26) |

- **Erythema nodosum** is the most common skin disorder in IBD, prevalence up to 15% (**CD 4–15%; UC 2.8–10%**): painful erythematous-violaceous subcutaneous plaques or nodules **1–5 cm**, symmetrically on the **extensor surfaces of the lower extremities (anterior tibia)**, also thighs and forearms. **Skin biopsy is needed only if the presentation is atypical.**
- **Pyoderma gangrenosum** is the second most common reactive cutaneous EIM and the most debilitating: prevalence in IBD **0.4–2.6%**, more common in **women, black Africans, and those with a family history of UC**, and lesions often arise **at a site of trauma**.
- **Sweet syndrome** requires the combination of sudden tender erythematous-to-violaceous papules and plaques **with histological neutrophilic dermal infiltration without vasculitis**; IBD is the **third most common** underlying condition after haematological malignancy and infection. **Azathioprine may induce a Sweet-syndrome-like dermatosis.**
- ⚠ **Hidradenitis suppurativa's sinus tracts and scarring can be mistaken for [[perianal-fistulizing-crohns-disease|perianal CD]].**
- **Paradoxical skin inflammation on TNFα antagonists** (psoriasiform reactions, psoriasiform eczema, paradoxical hidradenitis suppurativa) occurs in **5–10%** of treated IBD patients and does not appear related to age or treatment duration.

## Eye

**Ocular manifestations are relatively frequent; the most frequent is anterior uveitis.** Less frequent: episcleritis, conjunctivitis, scleritis, optic neuritis, ischaemic optic neuropathy, other uveitis subtypes, central retinal vein occlusion, and orbital inflammation or myositis (Statement 28 *[EL3]*).

| Condition | Management |
|---|---|
| **Anterior uveitis** | **Topical corticosteroid drops first-line** |
| **Intermediate, posterior, panuveitis** | **Intravitreal or systemic corticosteroids**, ± second-line steroid-sparing agents |
| **Sight-threatening ocular inflammation persisting, and/or active underlying IBD** | **Adalimumab or infliximab as third-line** *[EL4]* |
| **Scleritis** | **Early oral NSAIDs**; consider **systemic corticosteroids**. Steroid-sparing agents including TNFα antagonists for refractory cases or incomplete corticosteroid response *[EL4]* |
| **Episcleritis** | **Topical lubricants and cool compresses** *[EL4]*; topical NSAIDs and corticosteroids if symptoms persist despite controlling the IBD |

- **Uveitis should be jointly managed by a gastroenterologist and a specialized ophthalmologist on an urgent basis** (Statement 29). **There is currently no trial evidence for non-TNFα-antagonist biologics or small molecules in IBD-associated uveitis.**
- Uveitis/iritis prevalence **11.1%** (205/1840) in the prospective Swiss IBD Cohort Study.
- Scleritis occurs in **<1%** of IBD cases (incidence ~2.8 per 100 000 person-years); CD carries an over three-fold (odds ratio 3.6, *p* < 0.001) and UC an over two-fold (odds ratio 2.2, *p* < 0.001) increased risk.

> [!pitfall] Eye pain that prevents sleep is an urgent referral
> It suggests scleritis rather than episcleritis. **Refer urgently to an ophthalmologist** — correct classification and management change severity and prognosis ([[ecco-2024-eim-ibd]] Statement 30).

## Ear, Nose and Throat

**Aural and nasal manifestations in IBD are very rare and there is not sufficient evidence to recommend any specific treatment** (Statement 27 *[EL5]*). All information rests on case reports, case series and expert opinion.

- **Cogan's syndrome** — a very rare autoimmune disorder affecting the inner ear and eye. In an ECCO CONFER project of 22 cases with concomitant IBD, **immunomodulatory maintenance and even TNFα antagonists did not prevent disease onset**, and IBD activity did not appear to trigger it. Early corticosteroids at inception may hinder audiovestibular deterioration.

## Neurological

**Central nervous system manifestations may be more common in IBD than in the general population** *[EL4]*, including **venous sinus thrombosis, stroke, and central demyelination** (Statement 31).

- ⚠ **In patients with IBD and central demyelination, TNFα antagonists are contraindicated** *[EL4]*.
- **IBD is rarely associated with peripheral neuropathies** *[EL4]*; **treatable causes should be identified**. Central neuropathies are described only in a case series *[EL5]*, and **drug adverse effects should be considered**.
- Reported prevalence of neurological manifestations varies widely, **0.2% to 36%**. There is a known **bidirectional association with multiple sclerosis** reflecting shared genetic susceptibility.

---

## Fatigue

**Fatigue is more frequent and more severe in patients with IBD than in the general population** (Statement 32 *[EL4]*).

- Prevalence **40–60% in inactive to mild disease**, **over 80% in active disease** (up to 86%, with hospitalization a specific risk factor).
- **Fatigue is an independent entity** — a prevalence of **40%** has been reported in patients in **remission**.
- **It negatively impacts health-related quality of life independently of disease activity or anaemia**, is more severe in CD than UC, and is the most common reason for work absence in IBD.
