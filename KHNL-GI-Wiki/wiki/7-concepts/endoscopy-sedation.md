## Sedation Continuum
Sedation is a drug-induced depression of consciousness on a continuum; patients can drift deeper than intended, so providers must be able to **rescue** from a deeper level than targeted.

| Level | Responsiveness | Airway | Ventilation | CV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minimal (anxiolysis) | Normal to verbal | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected |
| Moderate ("conscious") | Purposeful to verbal/tactile | No intervention | Adequate | Usually maintained |
| Deep | Purposeful only after repeated/painful stimulus | Intervention may be needed | May be inadequate | Usually maintained |
| General anesthesia | Unarousable even to pain | Intervention often needed | Frequently inadequate | May be impaired |

- US expectation: at least minimal sedation for [[upper-endoscopy|EGD]] and [[colonoscopy]] (patient wishes/medical condition may favor unsedated).
- Endoscopist-delivered minimal/moderate sedation appropriate for **ASA I–III**; also for patients with prior successful moderate-sedation procedures, an expectation of moderate sedation, and a procedure expected to be routine/uncomplicated.

## Pre-Procedural Assessment
- Informed consent (benefits, risks, alternatives); target depth matched to patient expectation and procedure needs.
- History: snoring/stridor/**OSA**, drug allergies/interactions, prior adverse sedation reaction, time **and contents** of last oral intake, tobacco/alcohol/substance use.
- Focused exam: vitals, heart/lung auscultation, baseline consciousness, **airway anatomy**; pregnancy test in women of childbearing age (some sedatives teratogenic).
- Documented assessment + procedural time-out are required quality metrics (Joint Commission). The time-out verifies patient identity and the planned procedure **including the sedation plan**, before sedation is started.
- Laboratory work-up before the procedure is **selective, not routine** — see [[preprocedure-testing]].

### ASA Physical-Status Class

| Class | Description |
|---|---|
| **I** | Normal, healthy patient |
| **II** | Mild systemic disease that does **not** limit activities (controlled HTN, controlled DM without systemic sequelae) |
| **III** | Moderate–severe systemic disease that does **not** limit activities (stable angina, DM with systemic sequelae) |
| **IV** | Severe systemic disease that is a **constant threat to life** (severe CHF, end-stage renal failure) |
| **V** | Moribund — substantial risk of death within **24 h**, with or without the procedure |
| **E** | Emergency suffix appended to the underlying class (I–V) |

- Higher ASA class → higher risk of unplanned cardiopulmonary events during endoscopy (>1 million-patient retrospective series; CORI database); useful for risk stratification.

### Airway Assessment
**Mallampati class** — structures seen with maximal mouth opening and tongue protrusion, sitting. Identifies possible OSA and predicts difficult endotracheal intubation.

| Class | Structures visible |
|---|---|
| **I** | Soft palate, fauces, uvula, pillars |
| **II** | Soft palate, fauces, **portion** of uvula |
| **III** | Soft palate, **base** of uvula only |
| **IV** | **Hard palate only** |

Airway management may also be difficult with:
- Previous problems with anesthesia/sedation; history of stridor, snoring, or sleep apnea
- Dysmorphic facial features (Pierre-Robin, trisomy 21)
- Oral: mouth opening **<3 cm** in an adult, edentulous, protruding incisors, loose/capped teeth, high arched palate, macroglossia, tonsillar hypertrophy, nonvisible uvula
- Neck: [[obesity]] involving neck/facial structures, short neck, limited extension, hyoid-mental distance **<3 cm** in an adult, neck mass, cervical spine disease/trauma, tracheal deviation, advanced rheumatoid arthritis
- Jaw: micrognathia, retrognathia, trismus, significant malocclusion

**One or more sedation-related risk factors + potential for deep sedation → higher likelihood of a sedation-related adverse event** (ASA). If the practitioner cannot rescue from general anesthesia in that situation, consult an anesthesia professional.

### NPO / Aspiration Risk
- Fasting limits aspiration with blunted airway reflexes; no universally accepted standard, no direct fasting-duration ↔ aspiration data.
- ASA defaults cited by ASGE: **≥2 h after clear liquids, ≥6 h after a light meal**.
- *Note the discrepancy:* [[aga-2024-glp1-endoscopy|AGA 2024]] describes the standard perioperative fast as **8 h solids / 2 h liquids** — solids differ (6 h vs 8 h), clear liquids agree at 2 h.
- [[gastroparesis|Impaired gastric emptying]] or emergency → weigh aspiration risk when choosing (1) the target depth of sedation, (2) whether to delay the procedure, and (3) whether to protect the airway with endotracheal intubation; use a team approach.

### GLP-1 Receptor Agonists Before Endoscopy
[[glp-1-receptor-agonists|GLP-1 receptor agonists]] ([[semaglutide]], tirzepatide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide) slow gastric emptying → concern for **retained gastric contents and aspiration**, especially at [[upper-endoscopy|EGD]]. GLP-1 RAs retain **solids more than liquids** (liquid emptying T½ ≈ 23 min; abnormal solid emptying = >60% retention at 2 h, >10% at 4 h on scintigraphy).

| Society | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **ASA** (consensus, expert opinion) | **Daily-dosed** → hold **day of** procedure. **Weekly-dosed** → hold **1 week prior**. Irrespective of indication/dose/procedure. If not held but no GI symptoms → **"full stomach" precautions**. |
| **AGA** (2024 Rapid CPU) | **Individualized approach**, not routine cessation. Standard fast (**8 h solids, 2 h liquids**) **+ no symptoms** (nausea, vomiting, [[dyspepsia]], distention) → **proceed** with upper and/or lower endoscopy. |

- Weigh the **indication** — stopping in a patient relying on a GLP-1 RA for **diabetes** may pose more risk than benefit; withholding one dose is reasonable for **weight-loss-only** use but is not mandatory/evidence-based, and may not reliably restore motility.
- **Symptoms** suggesting retained contents → transabdominal gastric ultrasound if expertise/equipment available (unproven in routine practice); if delay is harmful → consider rapid-sequence intubation (often infeasible in ambulatory settings).
- Preferred alternative to stopping the drug: **clear/liquid diet the day before** sedated procedures.
- AGA and a multisociety statement conclude there are **no data to support routinely stopping GLP-1 agonists** prior to elective endoscopy. [[aga-2024-glp1-endoscopy]]

## Agents

### Benzodiazepine + Opioid (Moderate Sedation)
- Standard regimen: **benzodiazepine (anxiolysis/amnesia) + opioid (analgesia)** — safe and effective for EGD/colonoscopy in patients without sedation-risk factors.
- Benzodiazepines: midazolam (favored — fast onset, short duration, less thrombophlebitis, amnestic) or diazepam (comparable efficacy).
- Opioids: fentanyl (faster onset/clearance, less nausea) or meperidine.
- Adjuncts in select cases (they deepen sedation and may prolong recovery): **diphenhydramine** (improves sedation and decreases pain at colonoscopy with an opioid + benzodiazepine), **promethazine**, **droperidol** (efficacy shown in therapeutic endoscopy and difficult-to-sedate patients).
- **Droperidol — the QT numbers that decide use:**
  - FDA **black-box warning**: use only when first-line agents have not given adequate sedation.
  - **Contraindicated** with prolonged QTc — **>440 ms in men, >450 ms in women**.
  - **Extreme caution** (risk of ventricular tachyarrhythmia): other QT-prolonging drugs, CHF, bradycardia, diuretic use, cardiac hypertrophy, alcohol abuse, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, **age >65 y**.
- Reversal agents may wear off **before** the benzodiazepine/opioid does → extended recovery-room monitoring may be needed to avoid post-discharge re-sedation.

### Propofol-Based Sedation
- Forms: non-anesthesiologist–administered propofol (NAAP), of which **NAPS** = propofol as a single agent to *deep* sedation under endoscopist direction, and **balanced propofol sedation (BPS)** = single loading dose of opioid + benzodiazepine then intermittent propofol boluses (**5–15 mg**) to *moderate* sedation; or anesthesia-provider/**MAC**.
- Shorter sedation and recovery times than opioid + benzodiazepine; propofol has **no specific reversal agent** → deep-sedation rescue skills and personnel required. BPS mitigates this: analgesia/amnesia at sub-hypnotic doses, finer titration, and partial reversibility retained via naloxone/flumazenil.
- Propofol dosing and depth are **individualized** — no fixed schedule.
- Safety in trained hands: clinically important events (apnea/airway compromise needing assisted ventilation) **<0.2%** in >36,000 NAPS cases; major adverse events **0.016%** and minor **0.46%** in >24,000 patients.
- Use when it improves safety, comfort, efficiency, or procedure completion.
- Routine anesthesia-administered propofol for low-risk EGD/colonoscopy shows no safety benefit (higher aspiration/cardiopulmonary events and perforation rates in population studies of ASA I–III patients), raises cost (**$150–$1500 per case**, billed separately), and does not change adenoma detection rate.

**Requirements whenever propofol is used** (ASGE Table 5) — all of the following:
- A sedation team with appropriate education and training; **a physician present throughout** and immediately available until discharge criteria are met.
- ≥1 person qualified in **advanced life support** (airway management, defibrillation, resuscitative medications).
- Trained personnel dedicated to **uninterrupted** monitoring of clinical and physiologic parameters.
- Physiologic monitoring must include **pulse oximetry, ECG, and intermittent BP**; oximetry is **not** a substitute for monitoring ventilation. Capnography should be considered.
- Ability to **rescue** a patient who becomes unresponsive, cannot protect the airway, or loses spontaneous respiration/circulation; age-appropriate airway and resuscitation equipment immediately available.

### Reversal Agents
- **Naloxone** — reverses opioids. **Flumazenil** — reverses benzodiazepines. Both must be immediately available in every endoscopy unit.
- **Propofol has no reversal agent.**

## Monitoring
- Required for all sedated procedures: **BP, SpO₂, HR** + visual assessment of ventilatory activity, level of consciousness, and discomfort.
- **Reassess consciousness and vitals at 5 defined points:** (1) before the procedure, (2) after sedative/analgesic administration, (3) **at least every 5 minutes** during the procedure, (4) during initial recovery, (5) just before discharge. Set device alarms if recording is automated.
- **Who watches:** for *moderate* sedation the monitoring person may perform brief, interruptible tasks (e.g. mucosal biopsy) provided the patient has not reached deep sedation; for *deep* sedation monitoring must be **continuous and uninterrupted**.
- **ECG monitoring:** continuous during moderate sedation in patients with significant cardiovascular disease or dysrhythmia; also consider with significant pulmonary disease, elderly patients, and anticipated prolonged procedures.
- Supplemental O₂: consider for moderate sedation, **required** for all procedures with intended deep sedation, and give if hypoxemia is anticipated/develops. Risk factors for hypoxemia: **baseline SpO₂ <95%**, emergent indication, long procedure duration, difficult esophageal intubation, comorbid illness (and, in one deep-sedation RCT, age, high BMI, sleep apnea, higher sedative doses).
- **Capnography**: consider for procedures targeting **deep sedation** (detects depressed respiratory activity before transient hypoxemia; lower transient hypoxemia in a >500-patient RCT). In moderate sedation with benzodiazepine + opioid an RCT found **no** reduction in hypoxemia, and no link between transient hypoxemia and serious cardiopulmonary events has been established.
- Post-procedure: monitor for adverse effects of instrumentation and sedation; discharge on **standardized criteria**.

## Who Needs an Anesthesia Provider
Consider anesthesia-provider–administered sedation for:
- Prolonged or therapeutic procedures needing deep sedation (eg, [[ercp]] — prone position alters cardiopulmonary physiology and limits airway access; [[endoscopic-ultrasound|EUS]]-guided FNA and [[device-assisted-enteroscopy|overtube-assisted enteroscopy]] have improved outcomes)
- Anticipated intolerance to standard sedatives
- Severe comorbidity (**ASA IV or V**) — extremes of age; severe pulmonary, cardiac, renal, or hepatic disease; pregnancy; drug or alcohol abuse; uncooperative patient
- Increased airway-obstruction risk from an anatomic variant, difficult positive-pressure ventilation, or anatomy predicting difficult intubation
- Jurisdictions where regulation forbids endoscopist-administered propofol but a propofol regimen is judged worth its risks and cost

## Unsedated Endoscopy
- An option in selected patients; provider education increases willingness to offer it.
- **Small-diameter endoscopes (<6 mm)** improve tolerability of unsedated upper endoscopy; topical pharyngeal anesthesia is generally used (it adds nothing when propofol is used).
- **Water-assisted or CO₂ insufflation** reduces pain during and after colonoscopy, sedated or not.
- More willing: older patients, men, non-anxious patients, no history of abdominal pain.
- Still do the **standard pre-sedation preparation and monitoring, including IV access** — sedation may become necessary mid-procedure.

## Graded Recommendations (ASGE 2018)

GRADE evidence quality: ⊕⊕⊕⊕ high · ⊕⊕⊕⊖ moderate · ⊕⊕⊖⊖ low. "Recommend" = strong; "suggest" = weak.

| # | Recommendation | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Recommend** evaluating all patients for sedation risk from pre-existing medical conditions | ⊕⊕⊕⊕ |
| 2 | **Recommend** opioid + benzodiazepine as safe and effective for minimal–moderate sedation for EGD/colonoscopy in patients without risk factors for sedation-related adverse events | ⊕⊕⊕⊕ |
| 3 | **Suggest** an adjunctive agent (diphenhydramine, promethazine, droperidol) with conventional sedatives in select circumstances | ⊕⊕⊖⊖ |
| 4 | **Recommend** specific provider training in endoscopic sedation, including **rescue from deeper-than-intended sedation** | ⊕⊕⊕⊕ |
| 5 | **Recommend** routine BP, SpO₂, and HR monitoring plus clinical observation; supplemental O₂ considered for moderate sedation and **administered during deep sedation**, and given if hypoxemia is anticipated or develops | ⊕⊕⊕⊕ |
| 6 | **Suggest** capnography for endoscopy targeting **deep sedation** | ⊕⊕⊖⊖ |
| 7 | **Recommend** anesthesia-provider–administered sedation for complex procedures, multiple comorbidities, or risk of airway compromise | ⊕⊕⊕⊖ |
| 8 | **Suggest** propofol-based sedation (endoscopist-directed or anesthesia-provider administered) when it is expected to improve safety, comfort, procedural efficiency, and/or successful completion | ⊕⊕⊖⊖ |
