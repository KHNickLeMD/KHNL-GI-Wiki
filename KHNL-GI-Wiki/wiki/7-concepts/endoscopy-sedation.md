## Sedation Continuum
Sedation is a drug-induced depression of consciousness on a continuum; patients can drift deeper than intended, so providers must be able to **rescue** from a deeper level than targeted.

| Level | Responsiveness | Airway | Ventilation | Cardiovascular (CV) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minimal (anxiolysis) | Normal to verbal | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected |
| Moderate ("conscious") | Purposeful to verbal/tactile | No intervention | Adequate | Usually maintained |
| Deep | Purposeful only after repeated/painful stimulus | Intervention may be needed | May be inadequate | Usually maintained |
| General anesthesia | Unarousable even to pain | Intervention often needed | Frequently inadequate | May be impaired |

- US expectation: at least minimal sedation for [[upper-endoscopy|esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD)]] and [[colonoscopy]] (patient wishes/medical condition may favor unsedated).
- Endoscopist-delivered minimal/moderate sedation appropriate for **ASA I–III**; also for patients with prior successful moderate-sedation procedures, an expectation of moderate sedation, and a procedure expected to be routine/uncomplicated.

### Depth Scales — Scoring the Continuum
The four-level continuum above describes depth but does not score it. Two interchangeable bedside scales do; the multisociety curriculum names both as acceptable titration tools and **requires that the scale used be identified and documented**, with documentation at regular intervals throughout the procedure. Ungraded expert consensus. [[aga-acg-asge-aasld-2012-sedation-curriculum]]

| Score | Ramsay Sedation Scale (response to verbal stimulation) | Modified Observer's Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) |
|---|---|---|
| **6** | Agitated | — |
| **5** | Responds readily to name spoken in normal tone | Responds readily to name spoken in normal tone |
| **4** | Lethargic response to name spoken in normal tone | Lethargic response to name spoken in normal tone |
| **3** | Responds only after name called loudly and/or repeatedly | Responds only after name called loudly and/or repeatedly |
| **2** | Responds only after mild prodding or shaking | Responds only after mild prodding or shaking |
| **1** | Does not respond after mild prodding or shaking | Responds only after painful trapezius squeeze (**purposeful** response, not withdrawal) |
| **0** | Does not respond to test stimulus | No response after painful trapezius squeeze |

- Levels 5–2 are worded identically in both scales; they diverge only at the extremes — Ramsay adds *agitated* (6) at the top, MOAA/S defines its two lowest levels by trapezius squeeze.
- **Bispectral index (BIS) monitoring has no supporting data** for procedural sedation in GI endoscopy.

## Pre-Procedural Assessment
- Informed consent (benefits, risks, alternatives); target depth matched to patient expectation and procedure needs.
- History: snoring/stridor/**obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)**, drug allergies/interactions, prior adverse sedation reaction, time **and contents** of last oral intake, tobacco/alcohol/substance use.
- Focused exam: vitals, heart/lung auscultation, baseline consciousness, **airway anatomy**; pregnancy test in women of childbearing age (some sedatives teratogenic).
- Documented assessment + procedural time-out are required quality metrics (Joint Commission). The time-out verifies patient identity and the planned procedure **including the sedation plan**, before sedation is started.
- Laboratory work-up before the procedure is **selective, not routine** — see [[preprocedure-testing]].

### ASA Physical-Status Class

| Class | Description |
|---|---|
| **I** | Normal, healthy patient |
| **II** | Mild systemic disease that does **not** limit activities (controlled hypertension [HTN], controlled diabetes mellitus [DM] without systemic sequelae) |
| **III** | Moderate–severe systemic disease that does **not** limit activities (stable angina, DM with systemic sequelae) |
| **IV** | Severe systemic disease that is a **constant threat to life** (severe congestive heart failure [CHF], end-stage renal failure) |
| **V** | Moribund — substantial risk of death within **24 h**, with or without the procedure |
| **E** | Emergency suffix appended to the underlying class (I–V) |

- Higher ASA class → higher risk of unplanned cardiopulmonary events during endoscopy (>1 million-patient retrospective series; CORI database); useful for risk stratification.

### Airway Assessment
**Mallampati class** — structures seen with maximal mouth opening and tongue protrusion, sitting. Identifies possible OSA and predicts difficult endotracheal intubation.

| Class | Structures visible |
|---|---|
| **I** | Soft palate, fauces, uvula, pillars |
| **II** | Soft palate, fauces, **portion** of uvula |
| **III** | Soft palate, **base** of uvula only |
| **IV** | **Hard palate only** |

Airway management may also be difficult with:
- Previous problems with anesthesia/sedation; history of stridor, snoring, or sleep apnea
- Dysmorphic facial features (Pierre-Robin, trisomy 21)
- Oral: mouth opening **<3 cm** in an adult, edentulous, protruding incisors, loose/capped teeth, high arched palate, macroglossia, tonsillar hypertrophy, nonvisible uvula
- Neck: [[obesity]] involving neck/facial structures, short neck, limited extension, hyoid-mental distance **<3 cm** in an adult, neck mass, cervical spine disease/trauma, tracheal deviation, advanced rheumatoid arthritis
- Jaw: micrognathia, retrognathia, trismus, significant malocclusion

**One or more sedation-related risk factors + potential for deep sedation → higher likelihood of a sedation-related adverse event** (ASA). If the practitioner cannot rescue from general anesthesia in that situation, consult an anesthesia professional.

### NPO / Aspiration Risk
- Fasting limits aspiration with blunted airway reflexes; no universally accepted standard, no direct fasting-duration ↔ aspiration data.
- ASA defaults cited by the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE): **≥2 h after clear liquids, ≥6 h after a light meal**.
- *Note the discrepancy:* [[aga-2024-glp1-endoscopy|American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) 2024]] describes the standard perioperative fast as **8 h solids / 2 h liquids** — solids differ (6 h vs 8 h), clear liquids agree at 2 h.
- [[gastroparesis|Impaired gastric emptying]] or emergency → weigh aspiration risk when choosing (1) the target depth of sedation, (2) whether to delay the procedure, and (3) whether to protect the airway with endotracheal intubation; use a team approach.

### GLP-1 Receptor Agonists Before Endoscopy
[[glp-1-receptor-agonists|glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs)]] ([[semaglutide]], tirzepatide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, exenatide) slow gastric emptying → concern for **retained gastric contents and aspiration**, especially at [[upper-endoscopy|EGD]]. GLP-1 RAs retain **solids more than liquids** (liquid emptying T½ ≈ 23 min; abnormal solid emptying = >60% retention at 2 h, >10% at 4 h on scintigraphy).

| Society | Guidance |
|---|---|
| **ASA** (consensus, expert opinion) | **Daily-dosed** → hold **day of** procedure. **Weekly-dosed** → hold **1 week prior**. Irrespective of indication/dose/procedure. If not held but no GI symptoms → **"full stomach" precautions**. |
| **AGA** (2024 Rapid Clinical Practice Update [CPU]) | **Individualized approach**, not routine cessation. Standard fast (**8 h solids, 2 h liquids**) **+ no symptoms** (nausea, vomiting, [[dyspepsia]], distention) → **proceed** with upper and/or lower endoscopy. |

- Weigh the **indication** — stopping in a patient relying on a GLP-1 RA for **diabetes** may pose more risk than benefit; withholding one dose is reasonable for **weight-loss-only** use but is not mandatory/evidence-based, and may not reliably restore motility.
- **Symptoms** suggesting retained contents → transabdominal gastric ultrasound if expertise/equipment available (unproven in routine practice); if delay is harmful → consider rapid-sequence intubation (often infeasible in ambulatory settings).
- Preferred alternative to stopping the drug: **clear/liquid diet the day before** sedated procedures.
- AGA and a multisociety statement conclude there are **no data to support routinely stopping GLP-1 agonists** prior to elective endoscopy. [[aga-2024-glp1-endoscopy]]

## Agents

### Agent Profiles and Initial Doses
**Doses below are for a healthy individual <60 years of age** — that is the table's own stated qualifier, and it is the qualifier that makes the numbers usable. Titrate; propofol depth is individualized with no fixed schedule. Ungraded expert consensus. [[aga-acg-asge-aasld-2012-sedation-curriculum]]

| Drug | Onset (min) | Peak (min) | Duration (min) | Initial dose | Antagonist | Side effects |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dexmedetomidine | <5 | 15 | Unknown | 1 µg/kg | None | Hypotension, bradycardia |
| Diazepam | 2–3 | 3–5 | 360 | 5–10 mg | Flumazenil | Respiratory depression, chemical phlebitis |
| Diphenhydramine | 2–3 | 60–90 | >240 | 25–50 mg | None | Dizziness, prolonged sedation |
| Droperidol | 3–10 | 30 | 120–240 | 1.25–2.5 mg | None | QT prolongation, ventricular arrhythmia, extrapyramidal effects |
| Fentanyl | 1–2 | 3–5 | 30–60 | 50–100 µg | Naloxone | Respiratory depression, vomiting |
| Flumazenil | 1–2 | 3 | 60 | 0.1–0.3 mg | *(is the antagonist)* | Agitation, withdrawal symptoms |
| Ketamine | <1 | 1 | 10–15 | 0.5 mg/kg | None | Emergence reaction, apnea, laryngospasm |
| Meperidine | 3–6 | 5–7 | 60–180 | 25–50 mg | Naloxone | Respiratory depression, pruritus, vomiting, monoamine oxidase inhibitor (MAOI) interaction |
| Midazolam | 1–2 | 3–4 | 15–80 | 1–2 mg | Flumazenil | Respiratory depression, disinhibition |
| Naloxone | 1–2 | 5 | 30–45 | 0.2–0.4 mg | *(is the antagonist)* | Narcotic withdrawal |
| Nitrous oxide | 2–3 | Dose dependent | 15–30 | Titrate to effect | None | Respiratory depression, headache |
| Promethazine | 2–5 | Unknown | >120 | 12.5–25 mg | None | Respiratory depression, hypotension, extrapyramidal effects |
| Propofol | <1 | 1–2 | 4–8 | 10–40 mg | None | Respiratory depression, cardiovascular instability |

*The table prints midazolam's peak as "3–3"; the document's pharmacology appendix gives **within 3–4 min**, used here. **Fospropofol** is an agent trainees must know but has no row in the table — a water-soluble propofol prodrug, moderate sedation in 84.6% at **6.5 mg/kg** in phase II dose-ranging, with no trial comparing it with propofol for endoscopic sedation.*

**Titration and dose-reduction rules** (same source):

| Agent | Titration / dose reduction |
|---|---|
| Midazolam | Healthy adult <60 y: **1–2 mg (no more than 0.03 mg/kg) intravenously (IV) over 1–2 min**, then **1 mg** at 2-min intervals. **Age >60 y or ASA III+ → reduce by ≥20%.** **Total >6 mg usually not required** for routine endoscopy. Clearance reduced in elderly, obese, hepatic or renal impairment |
| Diazepam | 5–10 mg over 1 min, further doses at 5-min intervals; reduce in debilitated/elderly. 10 mg suffices for most procedures; up to 20 mg if no opioid coadministered |
| Fentanyl | 50–100 µg, then **25 µg q2–5 min**. **Reduce by ≥50% in the elderly.** Accumulates in skeletal muscle and fat with repeated dosing |
| Meperidine | 25–50 mg slowly over 1–2 min, then **25 mg q2–5 min**. **Avoid in significant renal insufficiency** — normeporidine accumulates (myoclonus, convulsions); use fentanyl instead |
| Propofol | Onset 30–45 s. Cirrhosis and renal failure do **not** significantly alter its pharmacokinetics. Formulated with soybean oil and purified egg phosphatide → **avoid with egg, soy or sulfite allergy**. Injection pain in up to 30% (small veins); lidocaine minimizes it |
| Ketamine | Emergence reaction in 10–30% of adults (midazolam reported to minimize). Does not depress airway or cardiovascular reflexes even at 5–100× the intended dose |
| Dexmedetomidine | **1 µg/kg, then infusion 0.2 µg/kg/h**. Reversed by the α₂ antagonist atipamezole |
| Promethazine | **12.5–25 mg IV infused slowly (≤25 mg/min)** to limit hypotension; total 25–50 mg as adjuvant |

- **Topical pharyngeal anesthesia** (benzocaine, lidocaine, tetracaine) → more patients rate discomfort none/minimal (odds ratio [OR] 1.88; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.13–3.12) and more endoscopists rate the procedure not difficult (OR 2.60; 95% CI 1.63–4.17), in a meta-analysis of 5 RCTs.
- **Methemoglobinemia** from topical anesthetics: **not detectable by standard pulse oximetry or blood gases** (needs multiple-wavelength co-oximetry) — suspect it with cyanosis despite adequate supplemental O₂. Treat with **methylene blue 1–2 mg/kg IV over 3–5 min followed by a 15–30 mL fluid flush**, repeating **1 mg/kg at 30–60 min** if there is no improvement. Failure to improve suggests coexistent glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) or nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) methemoglobin reductase deficiency.

### Benzodiazepine + Opioid (Moderate Sedation)
- Standard regimen: **benzodiazepine (anxiolysis/amnesia) + opioid (analgesia)** — safe and effective for EGD/colonoscopy in patients without sedation-risk factors.
- Benzodiazepines: midazolam (favored — fast onset, short duration, less thrombophlebitis, amnestic) or diazepam (comparable efficacy).
- Opioids: fentanyl (faster onset/clearance, less nausea) or meperidine.
- Adjuncts in select cases (they deepen sedation and may prolong recovery): **diphenhydramine** (improves sedation and decreases pain at colonoscopy with an opioid + benzodiazepine), **promethazine**, **droperidol** (efficacy shown in therapeutic endoscopy and difficult-to-sedate patients).
- **Droperidol — the QT numbers that decide use:**
  - US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) **black-box warning**: use only when first-line agents have not given adequate sedation.
  - **Contraindicated** with prolonged corrected QT interval (QTc) — **>440 ms in men, >450 ms in women**.
  - **Extreme caution** (risk of ventricular tachyarrhythmia): other QT-prolonging drugs, CHF, bradycardia, diuretic use, cardiac hypertrophy, alcohol abuse, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, **age >65 y**.
- Reversal agents may wear off **before** the benzodiazepine/opioid does → extended recovery-room monitoring may be needed to avoid post-discharge re-sedation.

### Propofol-Based Sedation
- Forms: non-anesthesiologist–administered propofol (NAAP), of which **nurse-administered propofol sedation (NAPS)** = propofol as a single agent to *deep* sedation under endoscopist direction, and **balanced propofol sedation (BPS)** = single loading dose of opioid + benzodiazepine then intermittent propofol boluses (**5–15 mg**) to *moderate* sedation; or anesthesia-provider/**monitored anesthesia care (MAC)**.
- Shorter sedation and recovery times than opioid + benzodiazepine; propofol has **no specific reversal agent** → deep-sedation rescue skills and personnel required. BPS mitigates this: analgesia/amnesia at sub-hypnotic doses, finer titration, and partial reversibility retained via naloxone/flumazenil.
- Propofol dosing and depth are **individualized** — no fixed schedule.
- Safety in trained hands: clinically important events (apnea/airway compromise needing assisted ventilation) **<0.2%** in >36,000 NAPS cases; major adverse events **0.016%** and minor **0.46%** in >24,000 patients.
- Use when it improves safety, comfort, efficiency, or procedure completion.
- Routine anesthesia-administered propofol for low-risk EGD/colonoscopy shows no safety benefit (higher aspiration/cardiopulmonary events and perforation rates in population studies of ASA I–III patients), raises cost (**$150–$1500 per case**, billed separately), and does not change adenoma detection rate.

**Requirements whenever propofol is used** (ASGE Table 5) — all of the following:
- A sedation team with appropriate education and training; **a physician present throughout** and immediately available until discharge criteria are met.
- ≥1 person qualified in **advanced life support** (airway management, defibrillation, resuscitative medications).
- Trained personnel dedicated to **uninterrupted** monitoring of clinical and physiologic parameters.
- Physiologic monitoring must include **pulse oximetry, electrocardiogram (ECG), and intermittent blood pressure (BP)**; oximetry is **not** a substitute for monitoring ventilation. Capnography should be considered.
- Ability to **rescue** a patient who becomes unresponsive, cannot protect the airway, or loses spontaneous respiration/circulation; age-appropriate airway and resuscitation equipment immediately available.

### Reversal Agents
- **Naloxone** — reverses opioids. **Flumazenil** — reverses benzodiazepines. Both must be immediately available in every endoscopy unit.
- **Propofol has no reversal agent.**

**Naloxone — the rescue protocol** ([[aga-acg-asge-aasld-2012-sedation-curriculum]], ungraded):
- Supplied at **0.2, 0.4 and 1 mg/mL**. Give **0.2–0.4 mg (0.5–1.0 µg/kg) IV every 2–3 min until the desired response**.
- Onset 1–2 min, half-life 30–45 min → **supplemental doses may be needed after 20–30 min**, and more again with longer-half-life opioids.
- **Observe for a minimum of 2 hours after naloxone to ensure resedation does not occur.**
- **Ineffective against non-opioids** — benzodiazepines, barbiturates. Adverse effects: nausea, vomiting, sweating, restlessness, seizures.
- Evidence base stated plainly: naloxone rescue in endoscopy is extrapolated from opiate-overdose experience; **no large prospective trial has evaluated it in the endoscopy suite**.

**Flumazenil:**
- Half-life 0.7–1.3 h and **average duration of antagonism only 1 hour**, while midazolam's effect may persist **80 min or longer** → **sedation may recur**.
- Reverses benzodiazepine sedation and amnesia **more effectively than respiratory depression**.
- **May induce seizures or withdrawal** in patients on chloral hydrate, carbamazepine, high-dose tricyclic antidepressants, or chronic benzodiazepines.
- Elective use after endoscopy shortens recovery time, but the practical benefit to patient or unit is unproven.
- The multisociety document gives **no titration regimen** — only the 0.1–0.3 mg initial dose in the agent table above.

### Airway Management and Rescue
Providers must be able to rescue from one level deeper than targeted, so these are competencies rather than optional skills. **Advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) certification with hands-on airway training is required**, and all airway maneuvers are practiced first on models. Ungraded expert consensus. [[aga-acg-asge-aasld-2012-sedation-curriculum]]

| Problem | Response |
|---|---|
| Airway obstruction | **Head-tilt/chin-lift or jaw-thrust**; nasopharyngeal or oropharyngeal airway |
| Inadequate ventilation | **Bag-mask ventilation**; laryngeal mask airway |
| Hypoxemia | Supplemental oxygen, to treat and to prevent |
| Opioid or benzodiazepine excess | **Naloxone / flumazenil** as above |
| Bradycardia | **Atropine, glycopyrrolate** or another vagolytic |
| Hypotension | **Position change + fluid bolus**; if severe, IV agents **including ephedrine** |
| Severe hypertension | IV agents **including β-blockers** |
| Arrhythmia / arrest | Recognize common atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, interpret significance, manage, perform cardiopulmonary resuscitation |

**Aspiration is the most serious sedation complication** — its consequences "may be impossible to correct or prevent once substantial aspiration has occurred," so the work is all prevention.
- **Highest-risk conditions:** active upper GI hemorrhage, [[achalasia]], bowel obstruction with gastric distention, [[gastroparesis|delayed gastric emptying]].
- Reduce risk by positioning, e.g. **elevating the head of the bed**.
- **Signs demanding protective measures mid-procedure:** frank emesis, drooling during colonoscopy, excessive retained fluid in the esophagus or stomach, hiccoughing, protracted coughing.

*An increased Mallampati score predicts the need for anesthesia-directed airway manipulation in patients receiving anesthesia-assisted sedation; there are **no comparable data for sedation targeting moderate depth**.*

## Monitoring
- Required for all sedated procedures: **BP, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂), heart rate (HR)** + visual assessment of ventilatory activity, level of consciousness, and discomfort.
- **Reassess consciousness and vitals at 5 defined points:** (1) before the procedure, (2) after sedative/analgesic administration, (3) **at least every 5 minutes** during the procedure, (4) during initial recovery, (5) just before discharge. Set device alarms if recording is automated.
- **Who watches:** for *moderate* sedation the monitoring person may perform brief, interruptible tasks (e.g. mucosal biopsy) provided the patient has not reached deep sedation; for *deep* sedation monitoring must be **continuous and uninterrupted**.
- **ECG monitoring:** continuous during moderate sedation in patients with significant cardiovascular disease or dysrhythmia; also consider with significant pulmonary disease, elderly patients, and anticipated prolonged procedures.
- Supplemental O₂: consider for moderate sedation, **required** for all procedures with intended deep sedation, and give if hypoxemia is anticipated/develops. Risk factors for hypoxemia: **baseline SpO₂ <95%**, emergent indication, long procedure duration, difficult esophageal intubation, comorbid illness (and, in one deep-sedation randomized controlled trial [RCT], age, high body mass index [BMI], sleep apnea, higher sedative doses).
- **Capnography**: consider for procedures targeting **deep sedation** (detects depressed respiratory activity before transient hypoxemia; lower transient hypoxemia in a >500-patient RCT). In moderate sedation with benzodiazepine + opioid an RCT found **no** reduction in hypoxemia, and no link between transient hypoxemia and serious cardiopulmonary events has been established.
- Post-procedure: monitor for adverse effects of instrumentation and sedation; discharge on **standardized criteria** — the two scored instruments below.

## Recovery and Discharge Criteria
Two sequential scored gates, each out of 10. Ungraded expert consensus. [[aga-acg-asge-aasld-2012-sedation-curriculum]]

**Aldrete score — fitness to leave the procedure room for phase II recovery. Threshold: ≥8** (and/or return to a similar preprocedure status).

| Domain | 2 | 1 | 0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Respiration | Deep breath and cough | Dyspnea/shallow breathing | Apnea |
| Oxygen saturation | Maintains >92% on room air | Needs O₂ to maintain >90% | <90% even with supplemental O₂ |
| Consciousness | Fully awake | Arousable on calling | Not responding |
| Circulation | BP ±20 mm Hg of preprocedure | BP ±20–50 mm Hg | BP ±50 mm Hg |
| Activity | Moves 4 extremities | Moves 2 extremities | Moves 0 extremities |

**Postanesthetic Discharge Scoring System — fitness to leave the unit. Threshold: ≥9.**

| Domain | 2 | 1 | 0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vital signs | Within 20% of preprocedure | 20–40% of preprocedure | >40% of preprocedure |
| Activity and mental status | Oriented ×3 **and** steady gait | Oriented ×3 **or** steady gait | Neither |
| Pain, nausea and/or vomiting | Minimal | Moderate, required treatment | Severe, requiring treatment |
| Bleeding | Minimal | Moderate | Severe |
| Intake and output | Oral fluids **and** voided | Oral fluids **or** voided | Neither |

- Recovery monitoring: periodic blood pressure, pulse and oximetry, **plus electrocardiography in selected situations**.
- **Anyone who received any sedation must have an adult escort and may not drive themselves home.**
- Because reversal agents can wear off before the sedative does, watch for **resedation** — a minimum **2-hour observation after naloxone** (see *Reversal Agents*).

## Who Needs an Anesthesia Provider
Consider anesthesia-provider–administered sedation for:
- Prolonged or therapeutic procedures needing deep sedation (eg, [[ercp|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]] — prone position alters cardiopulmonary physiology and limits airway access; [[endoscopic-ultrasound|endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)]]-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA) and [[device-assisted-enteroscopy|overtube-assisted enteroscopy]] have improved outcomes)
- Anticipated intolerance to standard sedatives
- Severe comorbidity (**ASA IV or V**) — extremes of age; severe pulmonary, cardiac, renal, or hepatic disease; pregnancy; drug or alcohol abuse; uncooperative patient
- Increased airway-obstruction risk from an anatomic variant, difficult positive-pressure ventilation, or anatomy predicting difficult intubation
- Jurisdictions where regulation forbids endoscopist-administered propofol but a propofol regimen is judged worth its risks and cost

## Unsedated Endoscopy
- An option in selected patients; provider education increases willingness to offer it.
- **Small-diameter endoscopes (<6 mm)** improve tolerability of unsedated upper endoscopy; topical pharyngeal anesthesia is generally used (it adds nothing when propofol is used).
- **Water-assisted or CO₂ insufflation** reduces pain during and after colonoscopy, sedated or not.
- More willing: older patients, men, non-anxious patients, no history of abdominal pain.
- Still do the **standard pre-sedation preparation and monitoring, including IV access** — sedation may become necessary mid-procedure.

## Graded Recommendations (ASGE 2018)

Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) evidence quality: ⊕⊕⊕⊕ high · ⊕⊕⊕⊖ moderate · ⊕⊕⊖⊖ low. "Recommend" = strong; "suggest" = weak.

| # | Recommendation | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Recommend** evaluating all patients for sedation risk from pre-existing medical conditions | ⊕⊕⊕⊕ |
| 2 | **Recommend** opioid + benzodiazepine as safe and effective for minimal–moderate sedation for EGD/colonoscopy in patients without risk factors for sedation-related adverse events | ⊕⊕⊕⊕ |
| 3 | **Suggest** an adjunctive agent (diphenhydramine, promethazine, droperidol) with conventional sedatives in select circumstances | ⊕⊕⊖⊖ |
| 4 | **Recommend** specific provider training in endoscopic sedation, including **rescue from deeper-than-intended sedation** | ⊕⊕⊕⊕ |
| 5 | **Recommend** routine BP, SpO₂, and HR monitoring plus clinical observation; supplemental O₂ considered for moderate sedation and **administered during deep sedation**, and given if hypoxemia is anticipated or develops | ⊕⊕⊕⊕ |
| 6 | **Suggest** capnography for endoscopy targeting **deep sedation** | ⊕⊕⊖⊖ |
| 7 | **Recommend** anesthesia-provider–administered sedation for complex procedures, multiple comorbidities, or risk of airway compromise | ⊕⊕⊕⊖ |
| 8 | **Suggest** propofol-based sedation (endoscopist-directed or anesthesia-provider administered) when it is expected to improve safety, comfort, procedural efficiency, and/or successful completion | ⊕⊕⊖⊖ |
