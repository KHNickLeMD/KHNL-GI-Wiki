Endoscopy-related injury (ERI) is musculoskeletal injury caused by repetitive microtrauma to connective tissues during endoscopy. ERIs affect the majority of endoscopists. The [[asge-2023-ergonomics|American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) 2023 ergonomics guideline]] frames risk reduction as an occupational-health priority for endoscopists performing [[colonoscopy]], [[upper-endoscopy|esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD)]], [[ercp|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]], and [[endoscopic-ultrasound|endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)]], and addresses it through ergonomics education, work-rest scheduling, neutral-posture room setup, and antifatigue mats.

## Epidemiology
**Finding 1: endoscopists report high rates of ERI.**

- Overall pooled ERI rate **57.7%** (95% confidence interval [CI] 48.8–66.1) — meta-analysis of 17 surveys, 5227 respondents.
- Survey-based prevalence by career stage: **39%–89% in practicing gastroenterologists** vs **20%–47% in gastroenterology trainees**.
- 61% of gastroenterologists spend >40% of their time performing endoscopy.

| Most common site | Pooled rate (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Hands and fingers | 35.8% (18.1–58.6) |
| Back | 35.3% (24.3–48) |
| Upper back and neck | 32.6% (21.3–46.3) |
| Thumb alone | 29.2% (16.3–46.7) |
| Neck alone | 26.1% (16.9–37.9) |

## Biomechanics of Injury
ERI arises from **repetitive, high-force loads applied in non-neutral postures**, repeated across a procedure day. The four exposures named:

| Maneuver | Structure loaded |
|---|---|
| Torque steering | Right wrist extensors |
| Grasping and stabilizing the endoscope controller | Left forearm extensors |
| Manipulating the endoscope dial | Left thumb abductors |
| High-risk pinching | Not specified |

- Consequences run from pain and physical restrictions during procedures to **disability** — with downstream provider dissatisfaction and loss of a highly skilled workforce.

## Risk Factors
Risk factors named by the guideline: higher procedure volume, time spent performing endoscopy, cumulative time in practice, small hand size, age, and female gender. Two are supported by graded findings:

- **Female gender (Finding 2)** — ERI 62.4% in female vs 45.5% in male endoscopists; odds ratio (OR) **1.79** (95% CI 1.35–2.38; P<.01), from 8 studies and 3355 gastroenterology respondents.
- **Procedure exposure (Finding 3)** — more time performing endoscopy and higher procedure volume. Independent predictors across surveys: hours/week performing endoscopy (P=.009), years in practice (P=.022), **>15 years in practice** (P=.03), **>20 endoscopies/week** (P<.001), **>16 endoscopy hours/week** (P<.001).
- **Hand size** — data inconsistent and could not be pooled; detail and the device options are under [[#Hand Size and Assist Devices]].

## Neutral Posture
- **Neutral posture** = the body position where muscles are at resting length and joints are naturally aligned — joints held near the middle of their range of motion, maximizing control and strength while minimizing stress on joints and spine.
- Ergonomic stance during endoscopy: neutral neck and back without hyperextension or flexion; even weight distribution between both legs; no knee hyperextension.
- Recommendations 3 and 4 (monitor position, bed height) are both named for it.

## Preventive Interventions

| # | ASGE 2023 recommendation (all to reduce the risk of ERI) | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ergonomics education | Strong |
| 2 | Gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopists take microbreaks and scheduled macrobreaks | Conditional |
| 3 | A neutral monitor position during endoscopies | Strong |
| 4 | Use of a neutral bed height | Strong |
| 5 | Use of antifatigue mats | Conditional |

- **Grading vocabulary** (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation [GRADE]): *"recommend"* = **strong**; *"suggest"* = **conditional**.
- All 5 recommendations rest on **very low quality evidence**. No gastroenterology studies informed the monitor, bed-height, or antifatigue-mat questions — the panel extrapolated from the laparoscopic surgical literature and down-rated for indirectness and imprecision.
- The 3 strong recommendations were made *despite* that, because the panel placed high emphasis on preventing harm to endoscopists and the interventions are low-cost and easy to implement.

### 1. Ergonomics Education

> [!pearl] Everyone needs at least a didactic session
> The panel's floor for every endoscopist is to pursue some form of ergonomics education, at minimum a didactic session. No standardized approach exists yet, so responsibility for being educated rests with the individual endoscopist ([[asge-2023-ergonomics]]).

- Delivery options: online courses, in-person teaching, physical-therapist consultation, unit posters, short videos (ASGE GI LEAP, ASGE training curriculum, VideoGIE series). Incorporating ergonomic technique into fellowship curricula, education sessions, and teaching conferences also spreads awareness.
- Evidence: randomized trial of 15 fellows — education (didactics + video + supervisor feedback + checklist) improved Rapid Entire Body Assessment scores (P<.001); teaching video in 58 fellows improved ergonomics knowledge; individualized physical-therapist-guided training reduced or resolved pain in **63%** of endoscopists.
- **No study assessed cost-effectiveness.** Posters and video didactics are low-cost; physical therapy assessment and individualized plans add cost.

### 2. Microbreaks and Macrobreaks

| Break | Duration | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Microbreak | 30 s to 2 min | Short, biologically meaningful movement break taken through the day |
| Targeted stretching microbreak (TSMB) | 1.5 min | Every 20–40 min throughout each procedure |
| Macrobreak | 15–45 min | Scheduled into the day's endoscopy schedule |

- TSMBs target neck, shoulders, back, wrists, hands, knees, and ankles.
- Evidence (survey, n=1698): microbreaks → lower ERI odds (**OR 0.69**, 95% CI 0.54–0.87); macrobreaks 15–45 min → lower ERI odds (**OR 0.72**); **macrobreak duration itself was not associated with ERI** (P=.50). Surgical studies: TSMBs improved postprocedure pain, physical performance, and mental focus **without lengthening operative duration**.
- Mayo's OR-Stretch web application guides intraprocedure stretches.

> [!pearl] Microbreaks are free; macrobreaks need buy-in
> Microbreaks pose minimal to no risk, have no significant impact on procedure duration, and can be built into an individual's own schedule; macrobreaks may require administrative support ([[asge-2023-ergonomics]]).

### 3. Neutral Monitor Position
- Place the monitor **directly in front** of the endoscopist, **just below eye level**.
- **Viewing angle 15–25° below the horizon**; **viewing distance 52–182 cm**; monitor height **adjustable 93–162 cm above the floor** (5th-percentile female → 95th-percentile male eye height).
- Supporting surgical data: neck strain lowest with the monitor at eye level directly in front (not to the right or left); optimal monitor–operator distance **90–182 cm**; maximum distance at which the finest image detail is still resolvable **139–303 cm**.
- Unit-level action: make **all** monitors in the unit adjustable (booms/mobile stands) and adjust before starting each procedure.

### 4. Neutral Bed/Table Height
- Adjust table height so the endoscope is held **between elbow height and 10 cm below elbow height** — the height associated with significantly improved discomfort ratings.
- Examination table **adjustable 85–120 cm** (5th-percentile female → 95th-percentile male elbow height).
- Made a strong recommendation because adjustable stretchers/beds are already widespread and add no cost in most settings.

### 5. Antifatigue Mats
- Offsets the effects of prolonged standing: lower-extremity tiredness/discomfort, lower-extremity swelling, venous blood restriction, low-back pain, whole-body tiredness.
- Randomized study (gel mat vs no mat, 100 laparoscopic renal cases, 18 providers): less pain in feet (P=.003), knees (P=.001), and back (P=.001); lower overall discomfort (P=.001); higher overall energy (P=.049) — **benefits still present at 24 h**. A second study in 11 urologists doing cystoscopy showed improved postprocedure discomfort. Both studies were urologic, so results may not be entirely applicable.

> [!pitfall] A mat that cannot be cleaned is a biohazard
> Floor mats contaminated by routine use are a potential biohazard — they must be **easily and regularly cleanable** — and are a tripping hazard unless they have **beveled edges** ([[asge-2023-ergonomics]]).

## Special Considerations
Expert-opinion concept statements, ungraded — **no systematic review was performed** for any of the following. The guideline's abstract also suggests the use of ancillary devices in those with risk factors predisposing them to ERI; this is not one of the 5 numbered recommendations and carries no strength or quality grade.

### Hand Size and Assist Devices
- Hand-size data are **inconsistent and could not be pooled** (4 studies).
  - Large national survey — **no** significant difference: small glove 78.13% vs large glove 75.95% (P=.12); extra-small/small/medium 74.74% vs large/extra-large 77.05% (P=.27). Two international studies similar.
  - One anthropometric study — small-handed endoscopists (small/medium glove) had longer colonoscope insertion times (**9.4 vs 8.2 min**, P=.04) and more injury sites (P=.03), performing fewer colonoscopies.
  - Among gastroenterology fellows, 97.4% of those with small hand size (**glove ≤6.5**) were women.
- **2 reusable rubber dial adaptors** clip onto the standard right/left angulation knob in the control body, so it can be reached and manipulated by smaller hands: **Olympus MAJ-1072 auxiliary angle knob cover** (discontinued by the manufacturer without notice) and **Pentax OE-B1 right/left assistant knob**.
- Pilot study (hand span — thumb to fifth digit — **<19 cm**): trend toward shorter procedure time but **no significant difference in duration or ease**; **retroflexion rated significantly easier** by all endoscopists. Inexpensive, easy to use, safe, non-disposable; processed per manufacturer recommendations.

> [!pearl] Stock a dial adaptor and kill the stigma
> The panel judged it important to keep a dial adaptor available in the unit for endoscopists with smaller hands, and called for dispelling any stigma attached to using assist devices ([[asge-2023-ergonomics]]).

### Lead Aprons
- Used in procedures requiring fluoroscopy (ERCP, EUS-guided biliary interventions, luminal stent placement); they place additional loads on the trunk muscles and intervertebral discs.
- **222/1277 (22%)** of those surveyed reported lead-apron-related ERI; **men more than women — 26.5% vs 14.3%** (P<.001) of those affected.
- Not systematically studied in ERCP. **2-piece aprons** produce less discomfort by redistributing part of the weight across the hips from the upper body.

### Load-Reduction Devices
- The endoscope support stand and the antigravity arm are being evaluated for reducing ERI but are **not currently available in the United States**.

### Endoscope Maintenance
- Usual wear and tear makes a scope less responsive to maneuvers over time, so the endoscopist must expend greater force for the same task.
- **Every endoscopy unit should run a robust endoscope maintenance program** to identify suboptimally performing endoscopes.
