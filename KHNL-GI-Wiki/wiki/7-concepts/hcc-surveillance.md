## Who to Survey

*Basis: [[aasld-2023-hcc]] Table 1 + Guidance Statements 7–9; [[aasld-idsa-2025-chb-treatment]] Table 4 + Recs 6–9 (the [[chronic-hepatitis-b\|HBV]] rows); corroborated by [[aga-2026-hcc-surveillance]] BPA 2–3.*

Surveillance is targeted to those who **would be candidates for HCC treatment** (GS 7, Level 2, Strong). Few data show any difference in surveillance benefit by age, sex, race, or ethnicity.

### Sufficient Risk — Surveillance Advised

| Population | Defining criteria | Annual HCC incidence |
|---|---|---|
| [[cirrhosis\|Cirrhosis]], **Child-Pugh A–B**, any etiology | [[chronic-hepatitis-b\|Hepatitis B]]; [[hepatitis-c\|hepatitis C]] (viremic **or** post-SVR); [[alcohol-associated-liver-disease\|alcohol-associated cirrhosis]]; [[nafld-masld\|nonalcoholic steatohepatitis]]; other etiologies ([[primary-biliary-cholangitis\|PBC]], [[autoimmune-hepatitis\|AIH]], [[hereditary-hemochromatosis\|HH]], [[wilson-disease\|Wilson]]) | ≥1.0%/y |
| Cirrhosis, **Child-Pugh C** | **Only if a [[liver-transplantation\|transplant]] candidate** — early-stage HCC raises waitlist priority. All patients listed for LT undergo semiannual surveillance (GS 7b, Level 3, Strong) | ≥1.0%/y |
| Chronic HBV **without** cirrhosis | Man from an **endemic country**, age **>40 y** | ≥0.2%/y |
| | Woman from an **endemic country**, age **>50 y** | ≥0.2%/y |
| | Person **from Africa**, at an earlier age (see qualifiers) | ≥0.2%/y |
| | **Family history of HCC** | ≥0.2%/y |
| | **PAGE-B score ≥10** | ≥0.2%/y |
| | **HBV/HDV co-infection** — all adults, **independent of cirrhosis status** ([[hepatitis-d\|HDV]]; Rec 7, Conditional/Very Low) | — |
| | **HBV/HIV co-infection** — men **≥18 y**, women **≥40 y** (Rec 8, Conditional/Very Low) | — |
| After **HBsAg loss** | Continue surveillance if: cirrhosis; family history of HCC; **man who lost HBsAg after age 40**; **woman who lost HBsAg after age 50** (Rec 6, Conditional/Very Low) | — |

### Insufficient Risk / Do Not Survey

| Population | Annual HCC incidence | Statement |
|---|---|---|
| [[hepatitis-c\|HCV]] with **stage 3 fibrosis**, no cirrhosis — including post-SVR advanced fibrosis | <0.2%/y | GS 8, Level 3, Weak — *against* routine surveillance |
| **Non-cirrhotic [[nafld-masld\|NAFLD/MASLD]]**, including advanced fibrosis without cirrhosis | <0.2%/y (0.008 per 100 person-years in non-cirrhotic NAFLD) | GS 9, Level 3, Weak — *against* routine surveillance |
| Child-Pugh C cirrhosis **not** eligible for transplant | — | GS 7a, Level 3, Strong — competing risk of liver-related mortality |
| **Life-limiting comorbidity** — life expectancy **<1–2 years** that cannot be remedied by transplant or other directed therapy | — | GS 7c, Level 5, Strong |

- **Non-cirrhotic, non-HBV etiologies are not surveyed** ([[aga-2026-hcc-surveillance]] BPA 3) — low annual incidence.

### Qualifiers That Change the Answer

- **"Endemic country" = ≥2% chronic HBV prevalence** — most of Africa, Asia, the Middle East, and Eastern Europe ([[aasld-idsa-2025-chb-treatment]] Table 4 footnote a). [[aasld-2023-hcc]] only defers to "AASLD HBV guidance" for the definition.
- **"Person from Africa at earlier age"** — surveillance **can start as early as the third decade of life**, given a median age of **46 y** at HCC diagnosis (both guidances, footnote b).
- **PAGE-B ≥10** — validated in Western populations **on antiviral therapy**. Other calculators (**REACH-B**, validated in **untreated** persons; **REAL-B**) can be considered.
- **HCV cirrhosis after SVR** — HCC risk stays elevated for **up to 10 years** after SVR, so surveillance **continues indefinitely** unless future data show sufficiently reduced incidence.
- **Advanced fibrosis, case-by-case exception to GS 8/9** — surveillance "may be considered in select patients with advanced fibrosis on a case-by-case basis, **particularly for those in whom there is clinical suspicion of understaging of fibrosis** by noninvasive markers or biopsy." Non-cirrhotic MASLD is a genuine dilemma: ~¼ of MASLD-related HCC arises without cirrhosis, yet incidence is far below the cost-effectiveness threshold.
- **Triple/quadruple viral co-infection** — follow the surveillance rule of the co-infection with the **highest associated risk** (e.g. HBV+HDV+HCV → follow the HBV/HDV rule).
- **Cleared co-infection** — prior HCV (anti-HCV+, HCV RNA−) or prior HDV (anti-HDV+, HDV RNA−): surveillance is the **same as HBV alone**. HBV/HCV co-infection → treat HCV with [[direct-acting-antivirals|DAAs]], then survey per HBV mono-infection criteria (Rec 9).
- **HBsAg loss at unknown timing** — individualize, weighing cirrhosis and family history.
- **Children/adolescents with HBV/HDV co-infection** — HCC risk is largely unknown; individualize on age, family history, and fibrosis stage (the "all adults" rule does not extend to them).
- **What changed:** the incidence threshold for initiating surveillance in cirrhosis is now modeled at **~1.0%/y**, *lower* than the traditional **1.5%/y** cutoff readers may still carry.
- ⚠ **Decision gap — the PAGE-B / REACH-B / REAL-B component variables and point values are not in any ingested source.** The guidances give only the PAGE-B ≥10 threshold. Use an external calculator or ingest the derivation papers — do not reconstruct the scores from memory.

## Surveillance Modality

- **Semiannual ultrasound + α-fetoprotein (AFP), in combination** (GS 10, Level 2, Strong; [[aga-2026-hcc-surveillance]] BPA 2).
  - US alone for early-stage HCC: sensitivity **53%** (95% CI 35–70%), specificity **91%** (95% CI 86–94%). US + AFP: sensitivity **63%** (95% CI 48–75%) — [[aasld-2023-hcc]]. Table 2 of the same guidance lists the US+AFP pair at sensitivity 61% / specificity 92%. [[aga-2026-hcc-surveillance]] quotes **63% vs 51%**; the figures overlap within the AASLD confidence interval.
  - Small specificity trade-off, but a higher diagnostic odds ratio than US alone, and US+AFP was the **most cost-effective** strategy on formal analysis.
  - **AFP cutoff 20 ng/mL** — sensitivity ~60%, specificity ~90%; the most common surveillance threshold, though the optimal cutoff **may be lower in nonviral cirrhosis**.
  - US is highly **operator dependent** and performs worse in [[obesity]]; visualization limits (LI-RADS visualization score B or C) occur in ~20%, especially with obesity and nonviral cirrhosis.
- **CT- or MRI-based surveillance is not recommended routinely**, nor are tumor biomarkers other than AFP, in at-risk patients with cirrhosis or chronic HBV (GS 11, Level 5, Weak) — radiation/contrast exposure with repeated semiannual CT, radiology service capacity, patient acceptance, and cost.
  - **Exception (GS 11a, Level 3, Weak):** alternative imaging such as **contrast-enhanced MRI may be considered in select patients in whom US-based surveillance is suboptimal** — e.g. truncal obesity or marked parenchymal heterogeneity.
  - ⚠ Countervailing signal in the same guidance: early data suggest MRI-based surveillance may have **poorer** performance in **Child-Pugh B or C** cirrhosis, a group where blood-based biomarkers may matter more. (The "CT/MRI may be preferred in obesity, alcohol/NASH cirrhosis, or Child-Pugh B–C" note is a footnote to the **recall** algorithm, not a surveillance-modality recommendation — see [[hepatocellular-carcinoma]].)
  - Abbreviated MRI protocols reached sensitivity 80–90% / specificity 91–98% in small cohorts; not yet guideline-endorsed.
- A positive surveillance test triggers diagnostic recall imaging → see [[li-rads]] and [[hepatocellular-carcinoma]].

## Interval

- **Semiannual — approximately every 6 months** (GS 10, Level 2, Strong).
  - Rationale started from HCC tumor doubling time; semiannual then beat annual on tumor stage and lead-time-adjusted survival (**40.3 vs 30 months**, p = 0.03).
  - A multicenter RCT found **quarterly surveillance did not improve** early detection or survival over semiannual — 3-monthly is not better.
- Major problem is **underuse**: fewer than 1 in 4 patients with cirrhosis (~24% even under subspecialist care); only 30–40% of HCC is diagnosed at an early stage. **Outreach** (mailed invitations) and **in-reach** (EMR best-practice alerts, provider education) significantly improve participation and are recommended (GS 10a, Level 2, Strong).

## Harms & Limitations

- Consider physical, psychological, and financial harms (BPA 4): false positives/negatives, downstream diagnostic procedures, emotional distress, cost. Shared decision making is key.
- Harms from false-positive and indeterminate tests occur in **~10% of patients with cirrhosis** and are **mostly mild** — AASLD judges benefit to outweigh harm.
- Strongest direct evidence: randomized HBV trial — surveillance reduced HCC mortality (**HR 0.63; 95% CI 0.41–0.98**). No RCTs in cirrhosis (an attempted trial failed to enroll), but cohort meta-analyses show earlier detection, more curative treatment, and improved survival (**pooled HR 0.67; 95% CI 0.61–0.72**, 12 cohort studies), persisting after adjustment for lead- and length-time bias.

## Emerging Biomarkers

- Novel **blood-based** biomarkers (e.g., **GALAD**) and **radiologic** biomarkers are under validation; some commercially available but **evidence insufficient** to replace guideline tests (BPA 5).
- **Multicancer detection (MCD) blood panels should NOT be used** for HCC screening/surveillance (BPA 6).

## Risk-Stratification Scores

- Many cirrhosis risk scores exist but **few are sufficiently validated** for clinical use (BPA 7).
- In **non-cirrhotic chronic HBV**, **PAGE-B** and **REAL-B** can stratify future HCC risk (BPA 8); PAGE-B ≥10 marks sufficient risk to survey.

## Prevention (Upstream of Surveillance)

- The best way to reduce HCC morbidity/mortality is to **prevent cirrhosis** (BPA 1): vaccinate and treat viral hepatitis ([[chronic-hepatitis-b|HBV]] nucleos(t)ide analogs; [[hepatitis-c|HCV]] [[direct-acting-antivirals|direct-acting antivirals]] → cure lowers HCC risk), treat alcohol use disorder, manage metabolic syndrome, and address liver disease early.
- Screen HBV patients for HCV, HDV ([[hepatitis-d]]), and HIV coinfection — all raise HCC risk above HBV monoinfection.
