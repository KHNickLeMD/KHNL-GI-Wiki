## Scope

**Psychogastroenterology** = the field applying scientifically based psychological principles and techniques to the alleviation of digestive symptoms. **Brain–gut behavioral therapies** are its treatment arm: short-term, skills-based, gastrointestinal (GI) symptom–focused psychotherapies ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]).

> [!pearl] Your recommendation decides whether care happens
> Without the gastroenterologist's strong, compelling recommendation and know-how in facilitating referral, many patients do not receive this care at all — or get it too late, after self-management has failed and refractory psychopathology and/or inflammatory disease have developed ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]).

- Optimally delivered by mental health professionals specializing in psychogastroenterology; can **reduce health care utilization and symptom burden, especially when integrated directly into GI practice settings**.
- Where they sit in the treatment sequence for painful [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI)]], and the modality-by-modality evidence table: [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction#Brain–Gut Psychotherapies]].

## Best Practice Advice

Five statements, **ungraded** — the update rests on literature review, existing systematic reviews and expert opinion, with no strength or quality rating ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]):

| # | Best Practice Advice |
|---|---|
| **1** | Routinely assess health-related quality of life (QOL), symptom-specific anxiety, early life adversity and functional impairment as these relate to the patient's digestive symptoms |
| **2** | Master patient-friendly language on: the bi-directional brain–gut pathway and how it can become dysregulated; psychosocial factors that perpetuate or exacerbate GI symptoms regardless of original etiology; and why you are referring to a mental health provider |
| **3** | Know the structure and core features of the most effective brain–gut psychotherapies |
| **4** | Establish a **direct referral and ongoing communication pathway with 1–2 qualified mental health providers**, and assure patients you will remain part of their care team |
| **5** | Familiarize yourself with **1 or 2 neuromodulators** that can complement behavioral therapies when necessary |

## What Makes Them "Brain–Gut" Therapies

Distinguishing features vs traditional psychotherapy ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]):

- Leverage the brain's ability to bring under voluntary control symptom processes that seem, initially, to be driven entirely by the gut
- **Two related pathways:** target abdominal pain, visceral hypersensitivity and GI motility; and/or build coping, resilience and self-regulation skills
- **Typically short-term and GI symptom–focused**
- **Skills-based** — down-regulating unpleasant GI sensations, decreasing avoidance behaviors tied to fear of symptoms, building coping and resilience to stress or lifestyle changes imposed by a chronic condition
- Traditional psychotherapy instead provides support, normalizes psychopathology, and/or facilitates insight into *why* symptoms occur
- **Impact can be durable long after therapy is discontinued** — cost-effective long-term compared with psychotropics
- Best results from a health psychologist or other medically trained mental health provider familiar with the physiologic basis of symptoms and the medical team's goals

## The Two Therapies

| | Cognitive-behavior therapy (CBT) | Gut-directed hypnotherapy |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence** | **>30 randomized controlled trials (RCTs)**, number needed to treat **3** | Well tested in a range of pediatric and adult GI disorders, including [[irritable-bowel-syndrome\|irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)]] (**>11 RCTs**), heartburn and [[inflammatory-bowel-disease\|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]] |
| **Targets** | Maladaptive or unhelpful thoughts, feelings and behaviors **as they relate to symptoms** — not a root cause. In functional GI disorders (incl. in IBD): **catastrophizing, cognitive inflexibility, fear of symptoms, hypervigilance/attentional bias to benign gut sensations** | **Visceral hypersensitivity, motility disturbance, hypervigilance to benign sensations, somatization** |
| **Techniques** | Chosen by GI symptom profile: **diaphragmatic breathing, cognitive restructuring, new problem-solving skills**. **Interoceptive exposure** (building tolerance to unpleasant GI symptoms), with or without mindfulness, for symptom-specific anxiety | Session protocols (next row) |
| **Dose / delivery** | **As few as 4 sessions** in IBS, with efficacy equivalent to longer courses; effective delivered **by nurses, by telephone, and in groups**; promising online for IBS and IBD | **7-session North Carolina Protocol** and **12-session Manchester protocol** — similar benefit in refractory IBS, **up to 80% of patients achieving ≥50% reduction in IBS Severity Scoring**. The fully scripted North Carolina Protocol has been delivered **home-based**, validated for **gastro-esophageal reflux** and **[[ulcerative-colitis\|ulcerative colitis]]**, and lets **less-experienced providers** deliver gut-directed hypnotherapy |
| **Other uses** | Depressed teenagers with IBD; augmenting self-management in [[crohns-disease\|Crohn's disease]]. Diaphragmatic breathing for esophageal conditions incl. [[rumination-syndrome\|rumination disorder]] and supragastric belching | — |

- **IBS alone:** >30 RCTs for CBT and 11 for hypnosis, numbers needed to treat **between 2 and 4** — IBS patients often appreciate hearing this.
- Brain–gut psychotherapies have also been used successfully across [[noncardiac-chest-pain|non-cardiac chest pain]], [[functional-dyspepsia|functional dyspepsia]], [[gerd|gastroesophageal reflux disease]] and IBD.

## Assessment Before Referral

**Assessment can be informal and brief — 1–2 open-ended questions** (e.g. how bowel symptoms interfere with daily life; which areas of life are most affected). The symptom-specific-anxiety screen lives on [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]]. Why it is worth asking:

1. Without an open-ended option you may stay unaware of the areas where targeted medical intervention would most restore functioning
2. Life-impact questions **build rapport**
3. Responses give **comparison data points** for later — a fuller picture of progress than change in digestive symptoms alone
4. They identify who needs a behavioral health professional — **a referral to a GI psychologist proposed specifically to improve QOL is more likely to be well received**

| Instrument | Use |
|---|---|
| Irritable Bowel Syndrome Quality of Life (IBS-QOL) | Disease-specific QOL; quantifies current disease impact and change with treatment |
| Inflammatory Bowel Disease Questionnaire (IBDQ) | Disease-specific QOL, same use |
| 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36) | **General** QOL — typically *not* very clinically useful in GI care; unclear whether poor scores are attributable to the GI disorder, and less likely to reflect treatment response |
| Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) | Free brief depression questionnaire; useful on hand for quick measurement |
| Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) | Free screening tools; useful when screening against the general population (e.g. primary care) |
| Adverse Childhood Experience (ACE) Questionnaire | Commonly used screen for **early life adversity** |

- **Routine depression and anxiety screening is not required for the general GI population**, but may help in high-risk populations **including IBD**.
- **Early life adversity — what to ask about:** personal history of physical, emotional or sexual abuse; witnessed domestic violence growing up; raised by a caregiver with substance abuse or mental illness; an incarcerated family member. Identifies **who would benefit from psychological intervention earlier in care**.

## Explaining It to the Patient

> [!pearl] Explain the brain–gut axis early
> Raised only after everything else has failed, it implies a solely psychological cause. Explained early, it gives a clear rationale for alternative approaches when conventional treatment gives unsatisfactory relief ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]).

**The brain–gut script, in lay language:**

1. Brain and intestines **communicate continually** through nerves and chemical signals
2. The intestines send frequent messages about their condition — fullness after a meal, the need to have a bowel movement
3. The brain usually **dampens these signals** so they are not uncomfortable, keeping them outside conscious awareness
4. The brain sends messages back to **fine-tune gut muscle activity, acid and fluid secretion, and immune activity** — e.g. increasing acid secretion when food is expected, inhibiting digestion during exercise
5. This **can go wrong** when something disturbs the brain or nervous system for an extended time — substantial life stress, strong negative emotions, inadequate sleep; inflammation or infection can do it too. The brain then perceives gut sensations more strongly and may send inappropriate signals back down

Closing point: because the brain is part of the gut's control system, it can often reduce symptoms **regardless of whether it is actively contributing to causing them**.

**The referral script:**

- **Neuromodulation:** explain that medications such as low-dose antidepressants and anti-seizure medications can alter nerve signalling and dampen discomfort. Cons: side effects; limited efficacy, especially vs behavioral therapies; most patients must commit to them long-term.
- Give the **biologic reasoning** for hypnosis or cognitive therapy. The GI tract is always moving, and the brain's normal job is to stop you feeling it — like a watch or glasses, which you feel when you put them on and then stop noticing. This filtering of non-essential sensation is **extremely important but complex and poorly understood**; these therapies help divert attention away from symptoms and make them less bothersome.
- Stay basic about effects and mechanisms — this **dispels the impression that you think nothing is wrong**:
  - CBT gives a skill set to change how symptoms are viewed, or make them feel less threatening
  - **Hypnosis is not a stage entertainment show and is nothing magical** — a real medical therapy studied with great outcomes
  - **No side effects**, and positive effects on symptoms often last for years
- Behavioral and psychosocial contributors (stress, poor sleep, maladaptive eating patterns, inactivity) can exacerbate symptoms and are changeable, but are **generally not the cause** of the GI disease. Introducing routine referral **early in the evaluation** is better received.
- **Failure to explain the referral properly** leads to poor follow-through, will likely send the patient to another provider, can damage the physician–patient relationship, and decreases the likelihood of successful symptom control.
- **You do not need to decide which therapy** — usually best decided by the behavioral health provider.

Useful models for separating what *initiated* a disease from what *maintains* it: **[[postinfectious-ibs|post-infectious IBS]]** and **IBD with functional overlap**.

## Who Delivers Them

- **GI psychologists** — licensed, **doctoral degree**, within the American Psychological Association's recognized subspecialty of **clinical health psychology**. Generalist health psychologists are often glad to see GI patients and will seek specialized training or read the disease literature.
- **Psychiatrists** — medical doctors focused on psychiatric diagnosis and psychotropic medication; the subset specializing in **psychosomatic medicine** is a welcome addition to a multidisciplinary GI team.
- The **Rome Foundation psychogastroenterology section** brings together psychologists, gastroenterologists, psychiatrists, internists/pediatricians with psychosomatic interests, and non-doctorally trained providers (nurses, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, social workers). Future directions may include core competencies or training credentials.

## Building the Referral Pathway

Access is limited and not every gastroenterologist knows a GI health psychologist. Viable routes: the **state psychological society**, or a **national registry** of therapists interested in GI patients and familiar with ≥1 evidence-based brain–gut psychotherapy.

**The toolbox of someone equipped to see GI patients:**

- Experience with medical populations or chronic illness
- Collaborates with physicians
- Trained under a **cognitive-behavioral theoretical orientation**
- Collaborative, active patient interaction style
- Practices ≥1 of the evidence-based brain–gut psychotherapies
- If practising hypnotherapy, trained through a reputable organization focused on training health professionals

**The communication loop** — a clear pathway improves outcomes by building patient buy-in (not left to find a provider alone) and keeps treatment focused on the GI condition:

- The mental health provider should **feel free to call** with questions about symptoms, workup and medical recommendations
- Expect **a copy of the psychological intake report and possibly a final treatment summary**
- **Plan to see the patient yourself during active psychotherapy** — to maintain continuity, reinforce gains, change or reduce medication, and troubleshoot

## Candidacy and Sequencing

> [!pitfall] Distress is not a reason to refer
> Significant psychological distress does not suggest suitability for a GI psychologist — many brain–gut psychotherapies are **less effective with comorbid psychopathology**, and comorbid chronic depression or anxiety typically makes a poor candidate ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]).

| Psychological comorbidity | Sequencing |
|---|---|
| Comorbid Axis I disorder with **very pronounced emotional symptoms** | May be best **addressed first** — a general community psychologist focusing on depression and anxiety, or psychotropic medication. Often improves the odds of subsequently benefitting from GI-focused psychological treatment |
| **More moderate** emotional symptoms | Can be addressed **in parallel** with the GI psychologist's work |

- Keep information on a **wide range of mental health resources, including free or sliding-scale programs**, to ensure access.
- When to raise these therapies in the course of care, and how later guidance differs on candidacy: [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction#Brain–Gut Psychotherapies]].

**Neuromodulators** complement behavioral therapy — sometimes necessary and often effective in chronic digestive diseases. Classes named: **selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI), tricyclic antidepressant (TCA)**. Common reasons to add one:

- A **minor comorbid depression or anxiety disorder**
- **Lack of insight or motivation that precludes participation** in psychological treatment
- **Chronic pain not responding fully** to behavioral intervention

AGA 2018 names no agent, dose or duration; starting doses and titration: [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction#Gut–Brain Neuromodulators]].

## Cost and Coverage

- Short term — **usually less than 3 months** — with a **one-time total cost of $1000 to $2000 out of pocket**.
- **Effects are commonly durable for several years and therapy usually does not need to be re-administered.** Framed as a monthly cost against other, less effective uncovered interventions (probiotics, herbs, special diets, colonic cleanses), the expense often becomes less of a concern.
- Health psychologists can be reimbursed under **health and behavior codes, Current Procedural Terminology 96150/96152** — but there are **restrictions on which other professionals may use them**. Medicare and commercial plans often cover these services; many providers are out of network or do not accept insurance.

## Gaps

Named by AGA 2018 ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]):

- **Research:** no comparative effectiveness trials between the psychotherapies or against psychotropics; insufficient research on **mindfulness meditation and acceptance-based approaches** (later evidence: [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]]); only a few studies of impact on **actual disease course or inflammation** in Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis; limited studies on brain–gut psychotherapies accelerating or enhancing pharmacologic therapy.
- **Clinical:** insurance coverage; too few trained providers; need to expand remote, online or digitally based therapies.
