## Scope

**Psychogastroenterology** = the field applying scientifically based psychological principles and techniques to digestive symptoms. **Brain–gut behavioral therapies** are its treatment arm: short-term, skills-based psychotherapies aimed at gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms rather than at psychiatric diagnosis.

- The **gastroenterologist is the rate-limiting step.** Without a strong, well-informed recommendation from the gastroenterologist, many patients never receive these treatments, or receive them far too late — after self-management has failed and refractory psychopathology and/or inflammatory disease have developed ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]).
- Where they sit in the treatment sequence for painful [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI)]], and the modality-by-modality evidence table: [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]].
- Delivered optimally by mental health professionals specializing in psychogastroenterology, and **capable of reducing health care utilization and symptom burden especially when integrated directly into GI practice settings**.

## Best Practice Advice

Five statements, **ungraded** — no GRADE rating, strength label or quality level anywhere in the document ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]):

| # | Best Practice Advice |
|---|---|
| **1** | Routinely assess health-related quality of life (QOL), symptom-specific anxiety, early life adversity and functional impairment as these relate to the patient's digestive symptoms |
| **2** | Master patient-friendly language on: the bi-directional brain–gut pathway and how it becomes dysregulated; psychosocial factors that perpetuate or exacerbate GI symptoms regardless of original etiology; and why you are referring to a mental health provider |
| **3** | Know the structure and core features of the most effective brain–gut psychotherapies |
| **4** | Establish a **direct referral and ongoing communication pathway with 1–2 qualified mental health providers**, and assure patients you will remain part of their care team |
| **5** | Familiarize yourself with **1 or 2 neuromodulators** that can complement behavioral therapies when necessary |

## What Makes Them "Brain–Gut" Therapies

Distinguishing features vs traditional psychotherapy ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]):

- Leverage the brain's ability to bring under voluntary control symptom processes that appear to be driven entirely by the gut
- **Typically short-term and GI symptom–focused**
- **Skills-based** — down-regulating unpleasant GI sensations, decreasing avoidance behaviors tied to fear of symptoms, building coping and resilience
- Traditional psychotherapy instead provides support, normalizes psychopathology, and/or facilitates insight into *why* symptoms occur
- **Impact can be durable long after therapy is discontinued** — cost-effective long-term compared with psychotropics
- Best results from a health psychologist or medically trained mental health provider familiar with the physiology and the medical team's goals

## The Two Therapies

| | Cognitive-behavior therapy (CBT) | Gut-directed hypnotherapy |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence** | **>30 randomized controlled trials**, number needed to treat **3**; in irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) specifically, numbers needed to treat **2 to 4** | **>11 randomized controlled trials** across pediatric and adult GI disorders |
| **Targets** | Maladaptive thoughts, feelings and behaviors **as they relate to symptoms** — not a root cause. Specifically **catastrophizing, cognitive inflexibility, fear of symptoms, hypervigilance/attentional bias to benign gut sensations** | **Visceral hypersensitivity, motility disturbance, hypervigilance to benign sensations, somatization** |
| **Techniques** | **Diaphragmatic breathing, cognitive restructuring, problem-solving skills**; **interoceptive exposure** (building tolerance to unpleasant GI symptoms), with or without mindfulness, for symptom-specific anxiety | Scripted protocols |
| **Dose / delivery** | **As few as 4 sessions** with efficacy equivalent to longer courses in IBS; effective delivered **by nurses, by telephone, and in groups**; promising online for IBS and [[inflammatory-bowel-disease\|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]]. Diaphragmatic-breathing elements used for esophageal conditions incl. [[rumination-syndrome\|rumination disorder]] and supragastric belching | **7-session North Carolina Protocol** and **12-session Manchester protocol** — similar benefit in refractory IBS, **up to 80% of patients achieving ≥50% reduction in IBS Severity Scoring**. The fully scripted North Carolina Protocol has been delivered **home-based**, validated for **gastro-esophageal reflux** and **[[ulcerative-colitis\|ulcerative colitis]]**, and lets **less-experienced providers** deliver gut-directed hypnotherapy |

## Assessment Before Referral

**Assessment can be informal and brief — 1–2 open-ended questions** are enough (the specific questions and the symptom-specific-anxiety screen live on [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]]). Why it is worth asking:

1. Without an open-ended option you may stay unaware of the areas where targeted medical intervention would most restore functioning
2. Life-impact questions **build rapport**
3. Responses give **comparison data points** for later — a fuller picture of progress than change in digestive symptoms alone
4. They identify who needs a behavioral health professional — and **a referral proposed specifically to improve QOL is better received**

| Instrument | Use |
|---|---|
| Irritable Bowel Syndrome Quality of Life (IBS-QOL) | Disease-specific QOL; quantifies current disease impact |
| Inflammatory Bowel Disease Questionnaire (IBDQ) | Disease-specific QOL, same use |
| 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36) | **General** QOL — *not* usually clinically useful in GI care; unclear whether poor scores are attributable to the GI disorder, and less likely to reflect treatment response |
| Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) | Free brief depression questionnaire; useful on hand for quick measurement |
| Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) | Free screening tools; useful where patients are screened against the general population |
| Adverse Childhood Experience (ACE) Questionnaire | Commonly used screen for **early life adversity** |

- **Routine depression and anxiety screening is not required for the general GI population**, but may help in high-risk populations **including IBD**.
- **Early life adversity — what to ask about:** personal history of physical, emotional or sexual abuse; witnessed domestic violence growing up; raised by a caregiver with substance abuse or mental illness; an incarcerated family member. These identify **who would benefit from psychological intervention earlier in care**.

## Explaining It to the Patient

**Educate early in the relationship.** Raised only after everything else has failed, the explanation implies a solely psychological cause. Presented early — when conventional treatment has given unsatisfactory relief — it gives a clear rationale for an alternative approach.

**The brain–gut script, in lay language:**

1. Brain and intestines **communicate continually** through nerves and chemical signals
2. The intestines send frequent messages about their condition — fullness after a meal, the need to have a bowel movement
3. The brain usually **dampens these signals** so they are not uncomfortable, keeping them outside conscious awareness
4. The brain sends messages back to **fine-tune gut muscle activity, acid and fluid secretion, and immune activity** — e.g. increasing acid secretion when food is expected, inhibiting digestion during exercise
5. This **can go wrong** when something disturbs the brain or nervous system for an extended time — substantial life stress, strong negative emotions, inadequate sleep; inflammation or infection can do it too. The brain then perceives gut sensations more strongly and may send inappropriate signals back down

Closing point: because the brain is part of the gut's control system, it can often reduce symptoms **regardless of whether it is actively contributing to causing them**.

**The referral script:**

- Give the **biologic reasoning** for hypnosis or cognitive therapy. The GI tract is always moving, and the brain's normal job is to stop you feeling it — like a watch or glasses, which you feel when you put them on and then stop noticing. This filtering of non-essential sensation is **important but complex and poorly understood**; these therapies help divert attention away from symptoms and make them less bothersome.
- Stay factual about effects and mechanisms — this **dispels the impression that you think nothing is wrong**.
- **Hypnosis is not a stage entertainment show and is nothing magical** — it is a real medical therapy studied with great outcomes.
- **There are no side effects to these therapies**, and positive effects on symptoms often last for years.
- Emphasize that behavioral and psychosocial contributors, though changeable, are **generally not the cause** of the GI disease.
- **Failure to explain the referral properly** leads to poor follow-through, will likely send the patient to another provider, can damage the physician–patient relationship, and decreases the likelihood of successful symptom control.
- **You do not need to decide which therapy** — that is best decided by the behavioral health provider.

Useful models for separating what *initiated* a disease from what *maintains* it: **[[postinfectious-ibs|post-infectious IBS]]** and **IBD with functional overlap**.

## Who Delivers Them

- **GI psychologists** — licensed, **doctoral degree**, within the American Psychological Association's recognized subspecialty of **clinical health psychology**. Generalist health psychologists are often glad to see GI patients and will seek specialized training or read the disease literature.
- **Psychiatrists** — medical doctors focused on psychiatric diagnosis and psychotropic medication; the subset specializing in **psychosomatic medicine** is a welcome addition to a multidisciplinary GI team.
- The **Rome Foundation psychogastroenterology section** brings together psychologists, gastroenterologists, psychiatrists, internists/pediatricians with psychosomatic interests, and non-doctorally trained providers (nurses, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, social workers). **Future directions may include core competencies or training credentials** for the practice.

## Building the Referral Pathway

Access is limited and not every gastroenterologist knows a GI health psychologist. Viable routes: the **state psychological society**, or a **national registry** of therapists interested in GI patients.

**The toolbox of someone equipped to see GI patients:**

- Experience with medical populations or chronic illness
- Collaborates with physicians
- Trained under a **cognitive-behavioral theoretical orientation**
- Collaborative, active patient interaction style
- Practices ≥1 of the evidence-based brain–gut psychotherapies
- If practising hypnotherapy, trained through a reputable organization focused on health professionals

**The communication loop:**

- The mental health provider should **feel free to call** with questions about symptoms, workup and medical recommendations
- Expect **a copy of the psychological intake report and possibly a final treatment summary**
- **Plan to see the patient yourself during active psychotherapy** — to maintain continuity, reinforce gains, change or reduce medication, and troubleshoot
- Keep information on a **wide range of mental health resources, including free or sliding-scale programs**

## Candidacy and Sequencing

**Counter-intuitive rule: significant psychological distress does not indicate suitability** — brain–gut psychotherapies are **less effective when a patient has comorbid psychopathology**, and patients with comorbid chronic depression or anxiety are **typically not good candidates** ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]).

| Psychological comorbidity | Sequencing |
|---|---|
| Comorbid Axis I disorder with **very pronounced emotional symptoms** | **Address first** — a general community psychologist focusing on depression and anxiety, or psychotropic medication. This improves the odds of subsequently benefitting from GI-focused treatment |
| **More moderate** emotional symptoms | Address **in parallel** with the GI psychologist's work |

> Note the disagreement with [[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]], which advises raising brain–gut psychotherapies **at the outset of care** for painful disorders. Both are ungraded AGA updates; the newer 2021 statement governs when to raise them, and the sequencing rule above governs what to do about comorbid psychopathology, which 2021 does not address.

**Neuromodulators** complement behavioral therapy. Common reasons to add one: a **minor comorbid depression or anxiety disorder**; **lack of insight or motivation** impeding participation; or **chronic pain not responding fully to behavioral intervention**. The classes named are **selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI), and tricyclic antidepressant (TCA)** — no agent, dose, titration or duration is given here; dosing is on [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]].

## Cost and Coverage

- Short term — **usually less than 3 months** — with a **one-time total cost of $1000 to $2000 out of pocket**.
- **Effects are commonly durable for several years and therapy usually does not need to be re-administered.** Framed as a monthly cost against other uncovered interventions (probiotics, herbs, special diets, colonic cleanses), the expense often becomes less of a concern.
- Health psychologists can be reimbursed under **health and behavior codes, Current Procedural Terminology 96150/96152** — but there are **restrictions on which other professionals may use them**. Medicare and commercial plans often cover these services; many providers are out of network or do not accept insurance.

**Gaps the authors name:** no comparative effectiveness trials between the psychotherapies or against psychotropics; insufficient research on **mindfulness meditation and acceptance-based approaches** as of 2018 (see [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]] for the later evidence); only a few studies of impact on **actual disease course or inflammation** in [[crohns-disease|Crohn's disease]] and ulcerative colitis; insurance coverage, too few trained providers, and the need for remote/online delivery.
