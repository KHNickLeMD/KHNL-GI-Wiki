## What It Is — and What It Is Not

- **Palliative care (PC) principles:** early identification and management of physical, psychological, social, and spiritual suffering; excellence in communication for advance care planning (ACP), goals-of-care discussions, and prognostication; screening for caregiver fatigue; providing caregiver support [[aga-2020-palliative-care-cirrhosis]]
- Three misconceptions the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) update names and rebuts:
  - **PC ≠ hospice.** Hospice sits *within* PC and targets end-of-life care for estimated survival **<6 months**; PC applies at **all stages** of a life-limiting illness
  - **PC is not mutually exclusive with curative/life-prolonging therapy** — including [[liver-transplantation|liver transplantation]]. Transplant-listed patients have high symptom burden and caregiver distress
  - **PC is not only for decompensated disease.** Distress and depressive symptoms are identifiable in **Child-Pugh class A** patients, many of whom have quality-of-life and symptom scores well below population norms
- Median survival: **2 years in decompensated disease to 12 years in compensated disease**; quality of life with worsening severity **parallels advanced cancer**

**Best Practice Advice (BPA) 1 (verbatim):** *Care with palliative care principles should be provided to any patient with advanced serious chronic illness or life-limiting illness such as cirrhosis, irrespective of transplant candidacy; this care should be based on needs assessment instead of prognosis alone, delivered concurrently with curative or life-prolonging treatments, and tailored to stage of disease.*

All ten BPA statements are reproduced verbatim on [[aga-2020-palliative-care-cirrhosis]]. **The AGA update attaches no evidence grades to them** — no Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE), strength, or quality label exists in the document.

## Who Delivers It

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Primary palliative care** | Delivery of care by a clinician who is **not** a PC specialist — hepatology/gastroenterology, primary care, advance practice providers, allied health, intensive care unit (ICU)/emergency department (ED) |
| **Specialty palliative care** | Delivery by a PC specialist with dedicated training and/or board certification in hospice and palliative medicine |

- **BPA 2:** care inclusive of PC principles may be delivered by providers **from any specialty within any healthcare setting**
- Specialty PC is a limited workforce resource against a growing cirrhosis population — generalist delivery is the operative model, with specialty PC reserved for complex needs
- Four requirements for the circle of care: **communication, documentation, collaboration, and role clarity** (explicitly, who does what)

## The Primary Palliative Care Toolkit

What a non-PC specialist should actually do, with the example instruments and scripts the update supplies:

| Element | Do this | Examples given |
|---|---|---|
| **Symptoms** (BPA 3) | Screen for presence *and severity* across physical, psychological, social, and spiritual domains | Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) (including pruritus), Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2), Distress Thermometer, a quality-of-life scale |
| **Prognosis, functional status** (BPA 6) | Ask *"Compared to last year, have you noticed any changes in your ability to carry out your day-to-day tasks? Is this change happening over years? months? days?"* | Measure: Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD)-Na, Karnofsky Index, Liver Frailty Index |
| **ACP readiness → ACP/Goals of Care Designation (GCD)** (BPA 6, 7) | Ask *"I want to share with you my understanding of where things are at with your illness… Is this okay with you?"* | Serious Illness Conversation Guide |
| **Caregiver needs** (BPA 5) | Ask *"I know this must be hard on you. How are you doing?"* | Measure: Caregiver Strain Index, Zarit Burden Interview |
| **Communication skills** (BPA 4, 7) | Use a structured ACP/communication course | Vital Talk; Respecting Choices / Center to Advance Palliative Care; Serious Illness Conversation Guide; patient-facing tools (PREPARE for Your Care) |
| **Pathways** (BPA 9) | Establish care pathways and referral triggers with the local specialty PC team **in advance** | — |
| **Specialty access** (BPA 9, 10) | Refer to specialty PC; refer to hospice | — |

## Symptom Burden and Screening

**Most physical symptoms in cirrhosis are best addressed via optimal management of cirrhosis-related complications** (e.g. treating fluid overload — see [[ascites]]); some, such as pruritus, need specific therapy [[aga-2020-palliative-care-cirrhosis]]. Drugs and doses: *Symptom-Based Management* below.

Commonest symptoms in adult end-stage liver disease, by prevalence across studies: **sexual dysfunction 53%–93% · breathlessness 20%–88% · fatigue 52%–86% · insomnia 26%–77% · pain 30%–77% · daytime sleepiness 30%–71% · muscle cramps 56%–68% · pruritus 47%–64% · depression 10%–64% · anxiety 14%–45%**.

Screening instruments the update tabulates (full table with domains and scales on the [[aga-2020-palliative-care-cirrhosis|source page]]). The ones it points at for routine liver-clinic use:

- **ESAS** — 10 items, **0–10 visual analogue scale**; physical, emotional, and total scores; includes pruritus
- **Distress Thermometer** — distress **0 (none) to 10 (extreme)** visual analogue scale, plus a 39-item problem list
- **PHQ-2 → PHQ-9** — PHQ-2 is the first 2 PHQ-9 items; **yes to either → proceed to the PHQ-9**; no to both → screen negative for depression. PHQ-9 is **9 items scored 0–3, total 0–27**, categorized minimal/none, mild, moderate, moderately severe, severe
- Liver-specific quality-of-life tools: Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ), Liver Disease Quality of Life (LDQOL), Short Form LDQOL (SF-LDQOL), Liver Disease Symptom Index (LDSI), Liver Cirrhosis Patient-Reported Outcome Measure (LC-PROM)

*The update states no cut-point on any of these instruments that should trigger action or referral, and says there are few data on which tool is best.* Ancillary clinic staff can administer the screens (BPA 8).

## Symptom-Based Management

Doses below are for decompensated cirrhosis, from [[aasld-2022-palliative-cirrhosis]], which **numbers its guidance statements and attaches no evidence grades to them**. Governing principle: impaired hepatic metabolism and the risk of precipitating [[hepatic-encephalopathy|hepatic encephalopathy (HE)]] mean **lower doses and less systemic therapy** throughout; treat reversible contributors before reaching for a drug.

| Symptom | Non-drug first | Drug and dose | Avoid / caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pain** | Treat reversible causes — ascites, local infection, musculoskeletal injury (Statement 28). Localized pain → local before systemic (Statement 29): lidocaine patch, capsaicin, topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), pain-specialist injections. Physical therapy, mindfulness, acupuncture (caution if platelets <50,000) | **Acetaminophen 500 mg every 6 h, max 2 g/d** — preferred first-line systemic pharmacotherapy (Statement 30). Warn that other over-the-counter and prescription products also contain acetaminophen | **Systemic NSAIDs avoided** (Statement 31) — renal injury, bleeding, ascites, loss of furosemide response |
| **Pain needing an opioid** | — | Avoid opioids for chronic pain where possible; when necessary, low dose, as needed, titrated to effect, often with pain-management input (Statement 32). **Hydromorphone 1 mg orally every 6 h as needed** (or 0.4 mg intravenously) · **oxycodone 2.5 mg orally every 6–8 h as needed**, with extended intervals. First prescription = **7-day supply** of short-acting opioid, close follow-up. Start [[lactulose\|lactulose]] prophylactically for constipation and HE | **Codeine, morphine, tramadol** generally avoided. **Methadone** only with specialist input. **Fentanyl patch** lowest outpatient dose 12 μg/h may already be too high, and cachexia is a relative contraindication. Once goals are comfort-focused, titrate opioids to need without concern for long-term impact |
| **Neuropathic pain adjuvant** | — | **Gabapentin ≤300 mg/d** (300 mg daily starting dose) or **pregabalin 50 mg twice daily**; renal dose adjustment, taper to stop | Gabapentinoids: somnolence, mental status change, habit forming. **Tricyclic antidepressants** generally precluded (anticholinergic, mental-status effects) |
| **Dyspnea** | Assess routinely, including impact on function and caregivers (Statement 34). Fan, **supplemental oxygen even if not hypoxic**, mindfulness (Statement 35). [[paracentesis\|Paracentesis]], thoracentesis, drains | End of life: **intravenous hydromorphone 0.2 mg every 3 h as needed**, titrated to relief; anxiolytic for dyspnea-associated anxiety (Statement 36) | **Morphine** relatively contraindicated in advanced cirrhosis — use hydromorphone or oxycodone |
| **Muscle cramps** | Check electrolytes and **replete potassium, magnesium, zinc** (Statement 40) | **Taurine 2–3 g/d** · **vitamin E 200 mg three times a day** · **[[baclofen\|baclofen]] 5–10 mg three times a day** — preliminary supporting data (Statement 41). Baclofen may be started 10 mg daily at night, increased 10 mg/d weekly to a maximum 30 mg/d | Statement 41 gives vitamin E as 200 mg three times a day; the same document's summary table prints 300 mg three times a day |
| **Insomnia / sleep–wake disturbance** | Evaluate and treat underlying HE, pruritus, obstructive sleep apnea, restless legs (Statement 42). Review timing of activity, meals and medications — evening diuretics and lactulose cause nocturnal awakenings (Statement 43). Mindfulness-based stress reduction, cognitive behavioral therapy (Statement 44) | Short-term **melatonin 3 mg** or **hydroxyzine 25 mg nightly** (Statement 45) — tested only in **Child-Pugh A and B**; no long-term data | **Zolpidem** generally avoided in decompensated cirrhosis (impaired clearance, potentiates HE). **Chronic benzodiazepines** avoided; exception is anxiety at the end of life when comfort is the stated priority (Statement 46) |
| **Pruritus** | Moisturizing creams, avoid hot baths and harsh soaps/detergents, loose clothing, cool humidified air (Statement 49) | **Cholestyramine 4 g/d titrated to 16 g/d** — first line (Statement 50); space from other drugs. Alternatives (Statement 51): **naltrexone 12.5 mg/d**, up-titrated every 3–7 days to max 50 mg/d · **rifampicin** started at quarter dose, titrated to 150–300 mg twice daily with liver biochemistry monitoring · **sertraline 25 mg start**, titrated to 75–100 mg | **Rifampicin not if bilirubin >2.5 mg/dL** (anicteric patients only); hepatotoxicity with longer use. **Naltrexone** inappropriate on opioids — blocks analgesia, precipitates withdrawal. **Antihistamines** are not efficacious for cholestatic itch, but sedation may help itch-related sleep loss |
| **Nausea / vomiting** | Correct electrolytes, treat adrenal insufficiency, manage constipation, stop triggers (lactulose, opioids). Ginger, relaxation, acupuncture (caution if platelets <50,000) | **Ondansetron up to 8 mg/d** is preferred — monitor for constipation and QTc prolongation. **Metoclopramide up to 60 mg/d**, with 50% dose reduction in hepatic impairment; extrapyramidal effects limit long-term use. Acid suppression if reflux-associated | **Prochlorperazine avoided with jaundice** — case reports of cholestatic liver injury |
| **Fatigue** | Evaluate and treat contributors — HE, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, depression, anemia, medications (e.g. beta-blockers); behavioral education; physical activity (Statement 47) | — | **Insufficient data to support stimulants** in cirrhosis (Statement 48) |
| **Depression / anxiety** | Routinely assess mood (Statement 55). Investigate medical contributors — vitamin deficiency, sleep disorders, HE (Statement 56). Cognitive behavioral therapy is the backbone, ± pharmacotherapy | Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor choice is guided by co-symptoms (sexual dysfunction, somnolence, weight loss) | **Low threshold for referral** to mental health, particularly when considering pharmacotherapy (Statement 57) |
| **Sexual dysfunction** | Ask directly — it is among the least evaluated symptoms (Statement 52). Remove contributors (beta-blockers, alcohol, tobacco); treat depression and diabetes | **Tadalafil 10 mg** may be a safe short-term option in select patients (Statement 53) | Contraindicated with hypotension. **Notable lack of research on sexual dysfunction in women** with cirrhosis (Statement 54) |

Complication-driven symptoms — palliative angles only; management lives on the owner page:

- **Refractory ascites / abdominal distension:** abdominal drains may be an alternative to serial large-volume [[paracentesis]] for patients **ineligible for transplant and [[tips\|transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS)]]** whose goals are comfort-focused, though more comparative effectiveness research is needed (Statement 33); the guidance does **not** recommend low-flow ascites pumps at the end of life. See [[ascites]]
- **[[hepatic-encephalopathy|HE]]:** onset is an opening to educate the patient/caregiver unit, identify a surrogate decision maker before cognition fails, and revisit trajectory and advance care planning (Statements 37, 38). Treatment may depart from standard care at the end of life to match goals (Statement 39) — polyethylene glycol instead of lactulose, or stopping it to avoid incontinence; [[rifaximin\|rifaximin]] may be unavailable under the capitated hospice payment model, where off-label neomycin or metronidazole is an alternative

> [!pitfall] The common drugs are the dangerous ones here
> Systemic NSAIDs, tramadol, morphine, zolpidem and chronic benzodiazepines are all specifically called out as agents to avoid in cirrhosis — tramadol is often chosen first precisely because it is not recognized as an opioid ([[aasld-2022-palliative-cirrhosis]]).

## Prognostication and Sentinel Events

- **BPA 6:** prognosis should be evaluated **during routine care visits and at sentinel events**
- **Scores alone are not enough.** MELD, MELD-Na, [[cirrhosis|Child-Pugh]], and [[acute-on-chronic-liver-failure|CLIF-C acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF)]] have recognized limitations applied to individual patients. Add **comorbidities, functional status/[[nutrition-in-liver-disease|frailty]], rapidity of functional decline, and potential for improvement with etiological therapy**
- Clinicians are **overly optimistic** about individual prognosis; in a large multicenter study both physician-estimated and modeled prognoses failed to predict mortality in cirrhosis
- **Quality of life independently predicts outcome**, so a worsening CLDQ, EuroQoL visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS) or SF-LDQOL is prognostic information, not just a comfort measure (study-by-study figures on [[aga-2020-palliative-care-cirrhosis|the source page]])

**Sentinel events** — the trigger list that drives both prognostic reassessment (BPA 6) and repeat goals-of-care discussions (BPA 7):

- Hospital admission
- ICU admission
- Before initiation of life-supporting therapies
- Before surgery (see [[cirrhosis]] → perioperative risk)
- New onset of a cirrhosis-related complication (including [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)]])
- After determination of transplant eligibility

A RAND/UCLA modified-Delphi panel's quality indicators add **use of a mechanical ventilator** and **initiation of hemodialysis** to that list.

**ICU care in ACLF** is run as a time-limited trial with palliative care built in, and the day 3–7 futility thresholds that trigger a structured transition to palliative care live on [[acute-on-chronic-liver-failure]] ([[aasld-easl-2026-aclf-global-consensus]], Statements 52–53; [[aasld-2024-aclf]]).

## Advance Care Planning and Goals of Care

- **BPA 4:** excellence in communication is integral to high-quality ACP, goals-of-care conversations, and the cultivation of prognostic awareness — **across the spectrum of cirrhosis**, not only in decompensated disease
- **BPA 7:** GCD should be **repeated** at every sentinel event above
- Patients' stated preferences frequently diverge from fully aggressive care: in SUPPORT, among patients with ≥2 features of advanced disease (serum albumin ≤3.0 mg/dL *as printed in the source*, uncontrolled ascites, [[hepatic-encephalopathy|HE]], cachexia, upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleed), **43% would rather die than receive care in a nursing home**, and most preferred death to living in a coma or with a ventilator or feeding tube
- Practice reality: most surveyed cirrhosis providers thought ACP discussions occurred **too late**, and in a population cohort **one third or less** had documented directives or goals of care
- Openers the update supplies: *"To make sure that both you and your family are prepared, I like to address both the best- and worst-case scenarios regarding how your cirrhosis may progress. Can we talk about these now?"*; where prognosis is ambivalent and the disease is worsening, **"what if" scenarios** help elicit preferred decisions

## Caregiver Burden

**BPA 5:** routine care — **particularly in decompensated disease** — should include assessment of caregiver support and screening for caregiver needs.

- **Instruments:** Zarit Burden Interview, Caregiver Reaction Assessment, Family Strain Questionnaire-Short Form, **Caregiver Strain Index**, Patient-Reported Outcomes Information System (PROMIS) measures
- **HE is a principal driver** — caregiver burden scores are significantly higher with either **previous overt HE or minimal HE**, and cognitive performance and MELD correlate with burden
- **Independent predictors of caregiver burden:** repeated hospital admissions, **alcohol as etiology** (see [[alcohol-associated-liver-disease]]), and lower socioeconomic status
- Around [[liver-transplantation|transplant]], caregiver strain **predominantly impairs mental rather than physical quality of life**
- Caregivers of patients with cirrhosis bear a measurable functional and financial cost, and a small trial found a 4-week mindfulness and supportive group therapy intervention improved caregiver burden, mental health, and sleep

## Referral Triggers — Specialty PC and Hospice

### Specialty Palliative Care

**BPA 9:** work with the local specialist PC team to establish **clear triggers and pathways for referral**, agreed in advance. Needs appropriate for **specialty** PC:

- Difficult ACP and goals-of-care discussions
- Management of **refractory symptoms**
- Complex care coordination
- Challenging family and treatment-team dynamics
- Hospice referrals that involve conflict

The **Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool (SPICT)** may guide referral, though evidence in cirrhosis is limited.

### Hospice

**BPA 10:** timely referral for patients with **comfort-oriented goals and a prognosis of 6 months or less**.

- Insurance coverage of hospice benefits **usually requires comfort-focused care without pursuit of further curative treatments**
- Referral should follow preceding ACP and GCD — **not** arrive within days of death or as a surprise
- Barriers to PC/hospice receipt in cirrhosis relate to **ethnicity, geography, and comorbid medical and psychiatric conditions**
- The AGA update gives no score-based threshold operationalizing "6 months or less," and notes that individual-level prognostication in cirrhosis is unreliable. The **operative US eligibility criteria are the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) criteria**, reproduced in [[aasld-2022-palliative-cirrhosis]]

**Hospice eligibility — terminal stage of liver disease (life expectancy ≤6 months).** Criteria 1 **and** 2 must both be present; criterion 3 supports documentation.

| # | Requirement | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Both** a and b | **a.** Prothrombin time prolonged >5 s over control **or** international normalized ratio (INR) >1.5 · **b.** Serum albumin <2.5 g/dL |
| **2** | End-stage liver disease **plus ≥1** of | Ascites refractory to treatment or patient noncompliant · [[spontaneous-bacterial-peritonitis\|spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)]] · hepatorenal syndrome (elevated creatinine and blood urea nitrogen with oliguria) · HE refractory to treatment or patient noncompliant · recurrent variceal bleeding despite intensive therapy |
| **3** | Supporting documentation | Progressive malnutrition · muscle wasting with reduced strength and endurance · continued active alcoholism (>80 g ethanol/day) · [[hepatocellular-carcinoma\|HCC]] · hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positive · hepatitis C refractory to interferon treatment |

Patients awaiting liver transplantation who otherwise meet these criteria may be certified for the Medicare hospice benefit, but **are discharged from hospice if a donor organ is procured**.

## Barriers and Efficiency Levers

**BPA 8** — lack of time is a major barrier; optimize delivery efficiency.

- **91%** of surveyed cirrhosis providers cited competing demands for time; **76%** cited insufficient reimbursement
- Levers named: identify **local billing codes** (chronic care management, ACP, transitional care management); **prescreening surveys carried out by ancillary staff**; **multidisciplinary teams** so PC is "shouldered by a group rather than an individual"; embed advance practice providers with PC training
- Preemptive scheduling of dedicated ACP visits, rather than as-needed schedule adjustment, is the mature-practice target
