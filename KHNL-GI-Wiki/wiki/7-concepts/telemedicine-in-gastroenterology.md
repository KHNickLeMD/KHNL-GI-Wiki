## Definitions and Scope

- **Telemedicine** (Federation of State Medical Boards) — practice of medicine using electronic communication, information technology, or other means between a physician in one location and a patient in another, with or without an intervening health care provider.
- **Telemedicine for reimbursement** (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services [CMS]) — "two-way, real time interactive communication between the patient and the physician or practitioner at [a] distant site that includes, at minimum, audio and video equipment." Both audio and video are required, so an audio-only encounter does not meet this narrower definition.
- **Telehealth** — parent term; electronic information and telecommunication technologies used to support long-distance clinical care, patient and professional education, public health, and health administration. Telemedicine is one form of telehealth.
- **Scope** — outpatient gastroenterology and hepatology. The American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) update places other forms of telehealth outside its scope.

## Formats of Virtual Care

Six formats surround the gastroenterology (GI) provider:
- Traditional in-person care
- Telemedicine
- Telemedicine group visits
- e-Consults
- Inpatient virtual care
- Remote patient monitoring

## Choosing the Visit Type

### Format to Use

| Format | Best uses | Not advised | Comments / challenges |
|---|---|---|---|
| **In-person visits** | Initial visits · Complex patients · Physical examination important · Technology unavailable · Patient preference | Low-value care (eg, routine pre-endoscopy visits in non–open access settings) | — |
| **Telehealth video** | Pre-visit planning · Follow-up appointments · Stable patients · Patients residing far from physician's office or with transportation challenges · Patients with inflexible work schedules | Patients without videoconferencing technology or broadband internet access · Patients unable to meet in a private setting | Out-of-state licensing · Loss of revenue for hospital-based ambulatory clinics · Technical challenges during visit requiring conversion to audio-only encounter |
| **Telehealth phone** | Post-visit follow-up · Short follow-up visits · Communication of laboratory or other testing results · Pre-visit planning | Complex patients · Patients with hearing difficulties | Out-of-state licensing · Loss of revenue for hospital-based ambulatory clinics · Decreased reimbursement compared with visits with a visual component |
| **Telehealth video group visits** | Disease management · Counseling · Patient education · New patient orientation | Sensitive medical information | Out-of-state licensing · Reimbursement may be limited |
| **E-consult** | Straightforward specialty questions · Determination of appropriateness of endoscopic evaluation · Determination of appropriateness of full consult | — | Reimbursement may be limited |

### Patient Suitability

| Patient | Advice |
|---|---|
| Stable chronic digestive disease — including but not limited to [[gerd\|gastroesophageal reflux]], [[irritable-bowel-syndrome\|irritable bowel syndrome]], [[chronic-constipation\|chronic constipation]], chronic liver disease, [[chronic-pancreatitis\|chronic pancreatitis]], [[inflammatory-bowel-disease\|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]] | Excellent candidates for telemedicine |
| Change in therapy contemplated for any chronic illness **and** patient requests an additional visit for more information | Telemedicine often appropriate |
| Remote location **with** good broadband internet access for video | Ideal candidates for telemedicine |
| Limited ability to access or use a smartphone or other device, or poor to no broadband access | Poor candidates for audiovisual visits; may still be candidates for audio-only. Same population often has difficulty reaching in-person visits too |
| Moderate to severe symptoms and/or a physical symptom that requires examination | Better served by an in-person visit |
| New patient | Many practices require an in-person visit first, to integrate multidisciplinary care and establish rapport |

*IBD = inflammatory bowel disease*

- The AGA update does not define "moderate to severe", nor the broadband speed a video visit requires.
- **Remote and rural** — a telemedicine clinic was appropriate for 99% of patients with IBD, with >90% eligible for further telemedicine visits; across 88 patients, >44,000 miles of travel and 1100 hours were saved.
- **Complex care coordination** — pediatric-to-adult IBD transition: a blended in-person/telemedicine program achieved successful transition after 20 weeks with 86% retention. Mental health services have been delivered readily by telemedicine in IBD.
- **What in-person still buys** — examination, vital signs, multidisciplinary care, point-of-care diagnostics, vaccination. A joint gastroenterology society statement has emphasized the practicality of a comprehensive virtual physical examination, and these advantages are expected to dissipate as remote diagnostics evolve.

### In-Person to Virtual Ratio

- Depends on three factors: **practice setting** (regulated vs unregulated space), **geography** (rural vs suburban vs urban), **complexity of care**.
- Hospital-based ambulatory clinics will likely prefer more in-person visits — facility fees are lost with telemedicine.
- Rural locations will likely shift toward more telemedicine — logistic barriers to in-person visits.
- No single correct ratio: best practice in one setting may differ from another, and some practices continue to offer telehealth to new patients without inferior care.
- The optimal ratio by disease state is an open question the AGA update calls out for further study.

## Outcomes, Virtual vs In Person

> [!pearl] Telemedicine suits chronic and stable, not new and acute
> New acute condition by telemedicine → *more* likely to need a follow-up in-person visit or emergency department encounter than a face-to-face visit.
> Chronic condition by telemedicine → *less* likely to need subsequent hospitalization.
> Large cohort of commercially insured United States (US) patients ([[aga-2023-telemedicine]]).

- **IBD** — meta-analysis of 17 randomized controlled trials (n = 2571): higher IBD-specific quality of life and fewer clinic visits per patient vs standard of care; **no difference** in remission rates, disease activity, or self-efficacy. The quality-of-life benefit was seen in adolescents but not adults.
- **[[liver-transplantation|Liver transplantation]]** — telemedicine shortened time from referral to hepatologist evaluation and to transplant listing. Randomized trial of 106 liver transplant recipients in a telemedicine-based home management program: lower 90-day readmission, improved physical function, and better general health vs standard of care.
- **Acceptance** — 67% of a national US survey (n = 2080) reported ongoing willingness to use video visits; >80% of GI telemedicine patients said their concerns were addressed and they would do it again; >70% of 509 surveyed gastroenterologists expected telemedicine to remain part of their practice. Telehealth cut work absenteeism in IBD (20.9% vs 60.4% with face-to-face visits).
- **Overall position** — limited data suggest telemedicine does not result in inferior outcomes vs face-to-face care, but may be better suited for chronic disease management. Long-term outcomes remain unverified.

## Access and Equity

- Video-visit preference is **not evenly distributed**. Younger adults, individuals with higher income, and Hispanic and Latino patients may be more likely than older adults, individuals with lower income, and non-Hispanic White or Black or African American patients to prefer video visits.
- Within GI populations (IBD, chronic liver disease), **older patients, Black and African American patients, and patients with Medicaid and Medicare insurance are more likely to complete a telephone visit than a video visit.**
- Racial and socioeconomic disparities in access — in particular to video visits — should be recognized, and barriers examined and addressed. When feasible, practices, institutions, and health systems should provide **technical solutions and individualized patient support** to reduce them.

## Regulatory Requirements and Best Practices

### Visit Best Practices

| Category | Tips / techniques |
|---|---|
| **Regulatory** | Determine patient location · Informed consent |
| **Technology** | Ensure adequate audio/visual transmission at the beginning of the call · Be prepared to pivot to an alternate method if the connection is broken/unreliable · Implement pre-visit questionnaires via secure messaging |
| **Establishing rapport and introduction** | Begin with introduction of provider and provider's expertise · Have the patient introduce themselves and any family members participating · Establish goals of the visit up front and address limitations and advantages of telemedicine visits · Maintain eye contact with the patient |
| **Documentation** | Include a telemedicine disclaimer in visit documentation · Verify whether a specific teaching statement is needed for encounters with trainees at your institution/practice location |
| **Ensuring suggestions are communicated and understood** | Send post-visit instructions via secure portal or mail |
| **Billing** | Bill based on complexity with appropriate Place of Service code and telehealth modifier (eg, CPT modifier 95) or time for audiovisual encounters · Bill based on time for telephone-only visits |
| **Provider** | Limit switching between in-person and telemedicine visits during a clinic session · Be cognizant of provider isolation/burnout related to telemedicine · Establish best practices to integrate multidisciplinary care |

### Licensure

> [!pitfall] Licensure follows the patient, not the clinic
> Many states require the physician to be licensed in the state the patient resides in — the main barrier to providing care virtually.
> Requirements were eased early in the COVID-19 pandemic; as the pandemic eased, **the restrictions returned in most states** ([[aga-2023-telemedicine]]).

- Determine the patient's location — a regulatory best practice and a required documentation item.
- Some practices support their clinicians in obtaining licenses in surrounding states. Broader regulatory compromise is needed so providers are not required to maintain licenses in multiple states.

### Documentation

Requirements are similar to in-person visits, with several additional components suggested:
- Patient consent to telemedicine
- Method of telemedicine — secure two-way interactive video connection or phone call
- Patient location
- Provider location
- All clinical participants' roles and actions
- Other individuals present at the visit

### Billing

- Variable by payer. In general use the **same CPT code as an in-person visit** (eg, 99202–5 or 99211–15), with a specific **Place of Service code** and **telehealth CPT modifier** (eg, 95, GQ, or GT) based on payer specifications.
- **Audiovisual encounters** — bill by complexity or by time. **Telephone-only visits** — bill by time.
- Billing based on time requires that **total face-to-face time with the patient is in the visit documentation**.
- The **2021 coding changes eliminated the requirement to document physical examination findings** to support coding levels — this further facilitated reimbursement for telemedicine visits.
- CMS reimbursed audiovisual telehealth at the same rate as in-person visits during the COVID-19 pandemic, and most commercial payers mirrored this. Reimbursement for **audio-only** visits is variable and unlikely to remain on par going forward.

### Trainees

- **Trainee in the same physical location as the provider** — the provider enters the physical room and completes the telemedicine visit with the trainee.
- **Trainee and provider in distant locations** — discuss the case on a separate platform (eg, FaceTime) before entering the virtual room with the patient.
- Some institutions require a **modified teaching physician statement** for telemedicine encounters with a trainee.

### Clinic Workflow and Staffing

- **Pre-visit contact by office staff** — connect with the patient ahead of the visit to troubleshoot technical issues and confirm they can connect properly; set up assistive services such as an interpreter; complete standard practices such as medication review. Preserves uninterrupted, focused provider time during the visit.
- **Dedicated virtual care clinic** — one author's practice staffs telemedicine with remote nursing staff who communicate with providers by instant messaging, update medical history, and complete medication reconciliation. Benefits: in-person clinical staff are not distracted or burdened by remote care, maximal room utilization for brick-and-mortar clinics, and expansion of practice without new space.
- A telemedicine clinic can incorporate dietitians, psychologists, and psychiatrists who already use telemedicine routinely — but **most practices are not able to integrate other providers synchronously into the telemedicine visit**.
