## Definitions

- **Telemedicine** (Federation of State Medical Boards) — practice of medicine using electronic communication, information technology, or other means between a physician in one location and a patient in another, with or without an intervening health care provider.
- **Telemedicine for reimbursement** (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, CMS) — "two-way, real time interactive communication between the patient and the physician or practitioner at [a] distant site that includes, at minimum, audio and video equipment." Narrower than the Federation of State Medical Boards definition; audio-only does not meet it.
- **Telehealth** — parent term; use of electronic information and telecommunication technologies to support long-distance clinical care, patient and professional education, public health, and health administration. Telemedicine is one form of telehealth.
- Scope below is **outpatient** gastroenterology and hepatology.

## Formats of Virtual Care

Six formats sit around the gastroenterology (GI) provider:
- Traditional in-person care
- Telemedicine (audiovisual or audio-only, patient at a distant site)
- Telemedicine group visits
- e-Consults (provider-to-provider)
- Inpatient virtual care
- Remote patient monitoring

## Choosing the Visit Type

| Format | Best uses | Not advised | Comments / challenges |
|---|---|---|---|
| **In-person visits** | Initial visits · Complex patients · Physical examination important · Technology unavailable · Patient preference | Low-value care (eg, routine pre-endoscopy visits in non–open access settings) | — |
| **Telehealth video** | Pre-visit planning · Follow-up appointments · Stable patients · Patients residing far from physician's office or with transportation challenges · Patients with inflexible work schedules | Patients without videoconferencing technology or broadband internet access · Patients unable to meet in a private setting | Out-of-state licensing · Loss of revenue for hospital-based ambulatory clinics · Technical challenges during visit requiring conversion to audio-only encounter |
| **Telehealth phone** | Post-visit follow-up · Short follow-up visits · Communication of laboratory or other testing results · Pre-visit planning | Complex patients · Patients with hearing difficulties | Out-of-state licensing · Loss of revenue for hospital-based ambulatory clinics · Decreased reimbursement compared with visits with a visual component |
| **Telehealth video group visits** | Disease management · Counseling · Patient education · New patient orientation | Sensitive medical information | Out-of-state licensing · Reimbursement may be limited |
| **E-consult** | Straightforward specialty questions · Determination of appropriateness of endoscopic evaluation · Determination of appropriateness of full consult | — | Reimbursement may be limited |

### Best and Least Suited Patients

- **Excellent candidates** — stable chronic digestive disease, including but not limited to [[gerd|gastroesophageal reflux]], [[irritable-bowel-syndrome]], [[chronic-constipation|chronic constipation]], chronic liver disease, [[chronic-pancreatitis]], and [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]].
- **Also appropriate** — a change in therapy is being contemplated for any chronic illness and the patient requests an additional visit for more information.
- **Remote geography** — ideal candidates *provided* good broadband internet access for video. One IBD telemedicine clinic was appropriate for 99% of patients, with >90% eligible for further virtual visits; across 88 patients it saved >44,000 miles of travel and 1100 hours.
- **Complex care coordination** — pediatric-to-adult IBD transition; a blended in-person/telemedicine program achieved transition after 20 weeks with 86% retention. Mental health services have been delivered readily by telemedicine in IBD.
- **Poor candidates for audiovisual visits** — limited ability to access or use a smartphone or other device, or poor to no broadband access. These patients may still be candidates for **audio-only** visits, and the same group often has difficulty reaching in-person visits too.
- **Better served in person** — moderate to severe symptoms, or a physical symptom that requires examination. Many practices also require new patients to be seen in person first, to integrate multidisciplinary care and build rapport. No numeric or scored threshold defines "moderate to severe" here.
- In-person visits retain value for physical examination, vital signs, multidisciplinary care, point-of-care diagnostics, and vaccination — though a joint gastroenterology society statement has emphasized the practicality of a comprehensive virtual physical examination, and these advantages are expected to narrow as remote diagnostics evolve.

### Setting the In-Person to Virtual Ratio

- Depends on three factors: **practice setting** (regulated vs unregulated space), **geography** (rural vs suburban vs urban), **complexity of care**.
- Hospital-based ambulatory clinics tend toward more in-person visits — facility fees are lost with telemedicine.
- Rural practices tend toward more telemedicine — logistic barriers to in-person visits.
- No single correct ratio: best practice in one setting differs from another, and some practices see new patients by telehealth without inferior care.

## What the Outcome Data Show

- **Acute vs chronic is the key split.** In a large cohort of commercially insured United States (US) patients, a **new acute** condition seen by telemedicine was *more* likely to need a follow-up in-person visit or emergency department encounter than a face-to-face visit; a **chronic** condition seen by telemedicine was *less* likely to need subsequent hospitalization. Telemedicine suits chronic stable disease better than acute illness.
- **IBD** — meta-analysis of 17 randomized controlled trials (n = 2571): telemedicine programs improved IBD-specific quality of life and reduced clinic visits per patient, with **no difference** in remission rates, disease activity, or self-efficacy. The quality-of-life benefit was seen in adolescents but not adults.
- **[[liver-transplantation|Liver transplantation]]** — telemedicine shortened time from referral to hepatologist evaluation and to transplant listing. A randomized trial of 106 transplant recipients in a telemedicine-based home management program showed lower 90-day readmission, improved physical function, and better general health vs standard of care.
- **Satisfaction** — 67% of a national US survey (n = 2080) reported ongoing willingness to use video visits; >80% of GI telemedicine patients said their concerns were addressed and they would do it again; >70% of 509 surveyed gastroenterologists expected telemedicine to remain part of their practice. Telehealth cut work absenteeism in IBD (20.9% vs 60.4% with face-to-face visits).

## Access and Equity

- Video-visit uptake is **not evenly distributed**. Younger adults, higher-income individuals, and Hispanic and Latino patients may be more likely to prefer video visits than older adults, lower-income individuals, and non-Hispanic White or Black or African American patients.
- Within GI populations (IBD, chronic liver disease), **older patients, Black and African American patients, and patients with Medicaid or Medicare are more likely to complete a telephone visit than a video visit.**
- Racial and socioeconomic disparities in access — particularly to video visits — should be recognized, and barriers examined and addressed. Where feasible, practices, institutions, and health systems should provide **technical solutions and individualized patient support** to reduce them.

## Regulatory and Licensure

- **The main barrier to virtual care is state licensure** — many states require the physician to be licensed in the state where the *patient* resides at the time of the visit.
- COVID-19 eased these requirements; as the pandemic eased, **the restrictions returned in most states**.
- Practical options: support clinicians in obtaining licenses in surrounding states. Broader regulatory compromise is needed so providers are not required to maintain licenses in multiple states.
- **Determine the patient's location** at every visit — it is both a regulatory and a documentation requirement.

## Documentation

Requirements mirror in-person visits, plus these items:
- Patient consent to telemedicine
- Method of telemedicine — secure two-way interactive video connection or phone call
- Patient location
- Provider location
- All clinical participants' roles and actions
- Other individuals present at the visit

Also: include a telemedicine disclaimer in the visit note; document the quality of the visit; verify whether your institution requires a specific teaching statement for encounters involving trainees.

## Billing

- Variable by payer. In general use the **same CPT code as an in-person visit** (eg, 99202–5 or 99211–15), with the appropriate **Place of Service code** and a **telehealth CPT modifier** (eg, 95, GQ, or GT) per payer specification.
- **Audiovisual encounters** — bill by complexity or by time. **Telephone-only visits** — bill by time.
- Billing based on time requires that **total face-to-face time with the patient is in the visit documentation**.
- The **2021 coding changes removed the requirement to document physical examination findings** to support coding level — a key enabler of telemedicine reimbursement.
- CMS reimbursed audiovisual telehealth at the same rate as in-person visits during COVID-19 and most commercial payers followed. Reimbursement for **audio-only** visits is variable and unlikely to remain on par going forward.

## Visit Best Practices

| Category | Tips / techniques |
|---|---|
| **Regulatory** | Determine patient location · Informed consent |
| **Technology** | Ensure adequate audio/visual transmission at the beginning of the call · Be prepared to pivot to an alternate method if the connection is broken/unreliable · Implement pre-visit questionnaires via secure messaging |
| **Establishing rapport and introduction** | Begin with introduction of provider and provider's expertise · Have the patient introduce themselves and any family members participating · Establish goals of the visit up front and address limitations and advantages of telemedicine visits · Maintain eye contact with the patient |
| **Documentation** | Include a telemedicine disclaimer in visit documentation · Verify whether a specific teaching statement is needed for encounters with trainees at your institution/practice location |
| **Ensuring suggestions are communicated and understood** | Send post-visit instructions via secure portal or mail |
| **Billing** | Bill based on complexity with appropriate Place of Service code and telehealth modifier (eg, CPT modifier 95) or time for audiovisual encounters · Bill based on time for telephone-only visits |
| **Provider** | Limit switching between in-person and telemedicine visits during a clinic session · Be cognizant of provider isolation/burnout related to telemedicine · Establish best practices to integrate multidisciplinary care |

Additional practices named in the text: verify the patient is in a **private setting**; conduct the visit from a private office with the door closed; attend to audiovisual connection quality; establish a **backup method** to conclude the visit if the initial method fails.

## Clinic Workflow and Staffing

- **Pre-visit contact by office staff** — connect with the patient ahead of time to troubleshoot technical problems and confirm they can connect; arrange assistive services such as an interpreter; complete medication review. Preserves uninterrupted, focused provider time during the visit.
- **Dedicated virtual care clinic** — one author's practice staffs telemedicine with a remote nursing team communicating with providers by instant messaging, updating medical history and completing medication reconciliation. Benefits: in-person staff are not burdened by remote care, room utilization is maximized for brick-and-mortar clinics, and practice can expand without new physical space.
- A telemedicine clinic can host dietitians, psychologists, and psychiatrists who already work virtually — but **most practices cannot integrate other providers synchronously into the telemedicine visit**.

## Trainees

- **Trainee in the same physical location as the supervising provider** — the provider enters the physical room and completes the telemedicine visit alongside the trainee.
- **Trainee and provider in separate locations** — discuss the case on a separate platform (eg, FaceTime) before entering the virtual room with the patient.
- Some institutions require a **modified teaching physician statement** for telemedicine encounters involving a trainee.
