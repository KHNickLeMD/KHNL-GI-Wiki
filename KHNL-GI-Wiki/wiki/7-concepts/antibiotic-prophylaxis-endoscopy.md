Framework for when periprocedural antibiotics are — and are not — indicated for gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy. Bottom line: prophylaxis is **not** given solely to prevent infective endocarditis; it is reserved for a handful of scenarios that prevent local infection (incomplete biliary drainage at endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography [ERCP], percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy or jejunostomy [PEG/PEJ], endoscopic ultrasound [EUS]-guided fine-needle aspiration [FNA] of cysts) plus [[cirrhosis|cirrhotics]] admitted with GI bleeding and patients on peritoneal dialysis. Per the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) 2015 guideline ([[asge-2015-antibiotic-prophylaxis]]).

## Key Principle — Not for IE
- **Do not give antibiotic prophylaxis solely to prevent infective endocarditis (IE)** for any GI endoscopic procedure, regardless of cardiac lesion (aligns with the American Heart Association [AHA]). *(Rec 1, Moderate quality)*
- No causal link between endoscopy and IE; ~25 temporally associated IE cases across millions of procedures; routine daily activity (brushing/flossing/chewing) causes bacteremia often exceeding endoscopy rates.
- **Exception:** patients with a high-risk cardiac condition (list below) **and an established GI tract infection** in which enterococci may be part of the infecting flora **should receive antibiotic coverage**. *(Rec 2 — "we suggest", Low quality)*

> [!pitfall] Prophylaxis is never for endocarditis alone
> No GI endoscopic procedure, and no cardiac lesion, justifies antibiotics given **only** to prevent IE — not prosthetic valves, not prior IE ([[asge-2015-antibiotic-prophylaxis]], Rec 1). The one carve-out treats an infection the patient already has.

### Highest-Risk Cardiac Conditions (the exception list)

The exception is conditional on this list, so the list is the decision ([[asge-2015-antibiotic-prophylaxis]], Table 2 — AHA's cardiac conditions with the highest risk of an adverse outcome from IE):

- **Prosthetic cardiac valve** (mechanical or bioprosthetic)
- **Previous infective endocarditis**
- **Cardiac transplant recipients who develop cardiac valvulopathy**
- **Congenital heart disease (CHD)**, limited to:
  - Unrepaired **cyanotic** CHD, including palliative shunts and conduits
  - Completely repaired CHD with prosthetic material/device (surgical or catheter) — **only for the first 6 months** after the procedure
  - Repaired CHD with **residual defects** at or adjacent to the site of a prosthetic patch or device

**What "coverage" means here:** when such a patient has an established GI infection (e.g. cholangitis), or is already receiving antibiotics to prevent wound infection/sepsis from a GI procedure, the regimen should **include an agent active against enterococci** — **penicillin, ampicillin, piperacillin, or vancomycin**. Enterococci are singled out because they are the only GI-tract organisms likely to cause IE; AHA reiterates that **no study has shown this prevents enterococcal IE**.

## Bacteremia Risk by Procedure

Bacteremia is a surrogate for IE risk, not an indication to treat — it drives none of the recommendations below. ASGE 2015 names the **highest** rates as [[esophageal-dilation|esophageal dilation]], variceal sclerotherapy, and instrumentation of obstructed bile ducts; everything else sits in the low range.

| Procedure | Bacteremia rate |
|---|---|
| [[esophageal-dilation\|Esophageal dilation]] (bougienage) | **12%–22%** — higher with malignant strictures and with passage of multiple dilators |
| Variceal sclerotherapy | up to **52%**, mean **14.6%** |
| Endoscopic variceal ligation | **1%–25%**, mean **8.8%** |
| [[ercp\|ERCP]], **obstructed** duct (stones/strictures) | **18%** |
| ERCP, non-obstructed duct | **6.4%** |
| [[upper-endoscopy\|Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD)]] ± biopsy | up to **8%**, mean **4.4%** (usually <30 min, no infectious events) |
| [[colonoscopy]] | up to **25%**, mean **4.4%** |
| Colonic stent insertion | **6.3%** |
| Flexible sigmoidoscopy | **<1%** |
| [[endoscopic-ultrasound\|EUS]] ± FNA, upper GI cystic or solid lesions | **4.0%–5.8%** |
| EUS-FNA, solid rectal / perirectal lesions | **2%** |
| *Brushing and flossing teeth* | **20%–68%** |
| *Chewing food* | **7%–51%** |

*Bacteremia rates by endoscopic procedure, against the rates of routine daily activity*
*Source: rates as reported in the ASGE 2015 guideline text (Bacteremia Associated with Endoscopic Procedures)*
*[[asge-2015-antibiotic-prophylaxis|ASGE Guideline: Antibiotic Prophylaxis for GI Endoscopy (2015)]]*
*ERCP = endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography · EUS = endoscopic ultrasound · FNA = fine-needle aspiration · GI = gastrointestinal*

- No rates are available for [[poem|per-oral endoscopic myotomy (POEM)]], [[endoscopic-submucosal-dissection|endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD)]], [[polypectomy|endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR)]], or device-assisted enteroscopy; the guideline expects enteroscopy to be comparable to routine upper and lower endoscopy.

## When Prophylaxis IS Indicated
- **[[ercp|ERCP]] in a patient with prior [[liver-transplantation|liver transplantation]], or with known/suspected biliary obstruction, where incomplete drainage is possible** → give prophylaxis covering biliary flora (**enteric gram-negative organisms and enterococci**) and **continue it after the procedure if biliary drainage is incomplete**. *(Rec 4, Moderate quality)* ASGE **recommends against** prophylaxis when obstructive biliary disease is not suspected or complete drainage is anticipated. *(Rec 3, High quality)* Drainage achieved with difficulty counts — [[biliary-stricture|hilar cholangiocarcinoma]] and [[primary-sclerosing-cholangitis|primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)]] are the worked examples.
  - **Already in [[acute-cholangitis|cholangitis]]?** Treat the cholangitis with antibiotics — **additional single-dose ERCP prophylaxis is not recommended** on top of treatment.
  - Even with prophylaxis, **liver transplant recipients remain at higher risk** (1.2% infection rate vs 0.28% overall).
  - **Updated 2026 — three supported scenarios, and one of them is new.** The published literature most strongly supports prophylaxis for: **(i)** anticipated or actual **incomplete relief of biliary obstruction** (PSC, biliary cyst), **(ii)** **[[cholangioscopy]]**, and **(iii)** prior **orthotopic liver transplantation**. ASGE has since **moved the post-transplant indication from universal to conditional** — antibiotics for anticipated incomplete drainage (ischemic cholangiopathy, multiple intrahepatic strictures, contrast injection without stent placement) — on newer data showing low ERCP-associated infection rates in that group. **There are no data supporting routine prophylaxis for pancreatoscopy**; individualize on patient risk factors and the nature of the intervention. Prophylaxis may also be worthwhile when **extraluminal contamination is suspected** — concern for intraprocedural perforation or bile duct injury. ([[acg-asge-2026-ercp-quality-indicators]])
  - **Withholding it where no benefit is likely is part of the same quality indicator** — inappropriate use drives antibiotic resistance and drug adverse events. Appropriate-indication prophylaxis is a measured ERCP indicator (target >98%; see [[ercp-quality-indicators]]).
  - **Unstudied ERCP scenarios (ASGE 2015 states no data either way):** pancreatic cystic lesions communicating with the main pancreatic duct (infectious events appear uncommon in practice), and prevention of post–self-expandable metal stent (SEMS) acute cholecystitis (incidence 1.9%–12%; antibiotics "may help" but have not been studied).
- **[[enteral-access|PEG/PEJ placement]]** → parenteral **cefazolin 1 g intravenous (IV) 30 minutes before** the procedure (or an antibiotic with equivalent coverage of cutaneous organisms); pooled odds ratio (OR) for peristomal infection **0.36** (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.26–0.50). Where **methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA) is endemic**, preprocedure screening (nose, throat, perineum, broken skin) with decolonization of positives reduces MRSA peristomal infection. PEJ is unstudied but treated the same. *(Rec 8, High quality)*
- **EUS-FNA of cystic lesions** (mediastinal and pancreatic/peripancreatic [[pancreatic-cysts|cysts]]) → antibiotics before, often continued 3–5 days, to prevent cyst infection. **Contested for pancreatic cysts:** the first randomized controlled trial (RCT) ([[colan-hernandez-2020-eus-fna-panc-cyst-antibiotics|Colán-Hernández 2020]], n=226) found infection risk low (**0.44%** overall, the single event in the *placebo* arm) and placebo **non-inferior** to ciprofloxacin (**200 mg IV** before/during FNA, then **500 mg orally every 12 h × 7 doses**) — arguing prophylaxis is **not** routinely needed for pancreatic-cyst FNA. ASGE 2015 still *suggests* it (Rec 7, Low quality); duplication/mediastinal cysts were excluded from the RCT, so it does not address them.
  - **Mediastinal cysts — conflict:** ASGE 2015 *suggests* prophylaxis for their FNA *(Rec 6, Low quality)*; the newer [[acg-2025-eus-quality|ACG/ASGE 2025 EUS quality indicators]] advise against routinely sampling them at all, because antibiotics have not been shown to prevent cyst infection or mediastinitis.
  - **Interventional EUS** (pseudocyst drainage, biliary drainage, fine-needle injection, fiducial placement): ASGE 2015 says the role of prophylaxis **has not been studied** — most published series gave periprocedural antibiotics plus a short course after, with uncommon postprocedural infection. [[acg-2025-eus-quality|ACG/ASGE 2025]] says to **strongly consider** antibiotics for all interventional EUS.
  - **Lower GI tract cystic lesions:** [[acg-2025-eus-quality|ACG/ASGE 2025]] reports an infection rate (including perirectal abscess) of **~15% despite prophylaxis** after transrectal FNA/fine-needle biopsy (FNB) of cystic lesions — same caution as mediastinal cysts.
- **Cirrhosis admitted with acute GI bleeding** → antibiotics for **all** such patients **at admission, regardless of endoscopy** — IV **ceftriaxone** (or oral **norfloxacin** if intolerant). The clearest benefit is fewer infections; the rebleeding and mortality signals rest on older trials — duration and the prophylaxis-vs-none evidence: [[antibiotic-prophylaxis-cirrhosis]]. See also [[variceal-upper-gi-bleeding]], [[portal-hypertension|portal hypertensive]] bleeding, and [[spontaneous-bacterial-peritonitis|spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)]].
- **Continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis** → *suggested* before lower-GI endoscopy to prevent peritonitis. Risk of peritonitis after colonoscopy **without** prophylaxis was **6.3%** in a retrospective study (biopsy/[[polypectomy]] did not further increase it); no peritonitis occurred in those given antibiotics, though the difference was not statistically significant. *(Rec 12; based on observational data only)*
  - **Regimen (International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis):** **ampicillin 1 g** plus a **single dose of an aminoglycoside**, ± **metronidazole**, IV **immediately before** the procedure. Acceptable alternative: prophylactic antibiotics **intraperitoneally the night before**.
  - **Also required:** the **abdomen must be emptied of dialysate** before any procedure involving the abdomen or pelvis, including colonoscopy.

> [!pearl] Incomplete drainage, not the ERCP, is the trigger
> Incomplete biliary drainage predicted **91%** of all post-ERCP sepsis in one study, and in the few trials showing benefit antibiotics were **continued for several days after** the procedure — so a single preprocedure dose for an ERCP that drains the duct is the wrong intervention ([[asge-2015-antibiotic-prophylaxis]]).

### Regimens

The only agents and doses ASGE 2015 actually specifies:

| Indication | Regimen as stated |
|---|---|
| PEG/PEJ | **Cefazolin 1 g IV, 30 min before** (or equivalent cutaneous coverage) |
| [[cirrhosis]] + acute GI bleed | **IV ceftriaxone** on admission; **oral norfloxacin** if allergic/intolerant |
| Peritoneal dialysis, lower-GI endoscopy | **Ampicillin 1 g + single-dose aminoglycoside ± metronidazole IV** immediately before |
| High-risk cardiac + established GI infection | Include an anti-enterococcal agent: **penicillin, ampicillin, piperacillin, or vancomycin** |

> ASGE 2015 names **no specific agent, dose, or duration for ERCP or for EUS-FNA of cysts**. For ERCP it gives only the spectrum — enteric gram-negative organisms and enterococci. For cystic FNA it gives only the timing: "before, and often for 3 to 5 days following"; the retrospective series it cites used a fluoroquinolone for 3 days after pancreatic-cyst FNA. It gives no ceftriaxone dose — see [[antibiotic-prophylaxis-cirrhosis]].

## When Prophylaxis Is NOT Indicated
- Solely to prevent IE (any procedure, any cardiac lesion).
- ERCP with complete drainage and no suspected obstruction.
- Diagnostic EUS / EUS-FNA of **solid** lesions (upper or lower GI).
- **Synthetic vascular grafts** or other nonvalvular cardiovascular devices (eg, implantable electronic devices) — any procedure.
- **Orthopedic prostheses** — any procedure (American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons [AAOS] blanket-prophylaxis statement was withdrawn for lack of evidence). *(Rec 11, Moderate quality)*

## Immunocompromise and Neutropenia

There is no ASGE recommendation here — the guidance is graded by neutrophil count, so the count is the decision point ([[asge-2015-antibiotic-prophylaxis]]):

| Group | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Immunocompromised with **normal neutrophil count** (eg, organ transplant recipients, human immunodeficiency virus [HIV]) | Routine prophylaxis **not recommended** — no data that they are at increased risk |
| **Severe immunosuppression** — absolute neutrophil count (ANC) **<500 cells/µL**, advanced hematologic malignancy, bone marrow transplant | **Insufficient evidence** for or against; **individualize** |

- Patients with **severe neutropenia (ANC <500 cells/µL)** and/or advanced hematologic malignancy *are* at increased risk of bacteremia and sepsis after endoscopy; the guideline calls prophylaxis **"prudent"** in this group — especially before **high-bacteremia-risk procedures** — while stating plainly that it **has not been studied in clinical trials**. Flag this as expert opinion, not evidence.
- **Note the internal tension:** the ERCP section calls prophylaxis prudent in severe neutropenia, while the immunocompromised-patient section says the evidence is insufficient and the decision must be individualized. Both sentences are the same guideline; neither is a graded recommendation.

## Quick Reference Table
| Scenario | Procedure | Prophylaxis | Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bile duct obstruction, complete drainage | ERCP | Not recommended | High quality |
| Bile duct obstruction, incomplete drainage | ERCP | **Recommended**, continue after | Moderate quality |
| Solid lesion, upper GI / lower GI | EUS-FNA | Not recommended | High / Moderate (Table 3); Rec 5 grades both Moderate |
| Mediastinal cyst | EUS-FNA | **Suggested** | Low quality |
| Pancreatic/peripancreatic cyst | EUS-FNA | **Suggested** (Low quality) — but RCT ([[colan-hernandez-2020-eus-fna-panc-cyst-antibiotics\|2020]]) shows not needed | Low quality |
| All patients | PEG/PEJ placement | **Recommended** (cefazolin) | High quality |
| Cirrhosis + acute GI bleed | Any/none | **On admission** (ceftriaxone) | High quality |
| Synthetic vascular graft / nonvalvular cardiovascular (CV) device | Any | Not recommended | High quality |
| Prosthetic joint | Any | Not recommended | Moderate quality |
| Peritoneal dialysis | Lower GI endoscopy | **Suggested** (ampicillin 1 g + aminoglycoside) | Low quality* |
| Solely to prevent IE | Any | Not recommended | Moderate quality |
| High-risk cardiac lesion **+ established GI infection** | Any | Include anti-enterococcal agent | Low quality |
| ANC <500/µL, advanced heme malignancy, bone marrow transplant (BMT) | Any | **Insufficient evidence — individualize** | — |

\* The guideline grades this inconsistently: **Table 3 lists Low quality**, while **Recommendation 12 lists Very low quality**. Treat it as the weakest-supported item on the list either way (observational data only).
