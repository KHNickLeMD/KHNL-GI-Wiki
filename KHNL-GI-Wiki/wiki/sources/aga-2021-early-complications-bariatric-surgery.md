## Summary

AGA Clinical Practice Update (CPU) Expert Review on the endoscopic evaluation and management of **early (<90 days)** complications after bariatric/metabolic surgery. Approximately 205,000 primary bariatric operations were performed in the United States in 2018, ~95% (198,000) of which were sleeve gastrectomy (SG, 155,000) or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB, 43,000). The 90-day mortality rate is 0.1%–0.3% and the 90-day overall complication rate 7%–10%, of which 4% are major; in patients >65 years of age, 90-day mortality is 1.3% and the overall complication rate 20.1%. Flexible endoscopy is the principal method of diagnosing and treating many of these complications, but evaluation and treatment algorithms are not standardized and high-quality prospective data are scarce.

The update's clinical core is the pathophysiology of the **staple-line leak** and its corollary, **downstream functional stenosis**. Leaks occur most often along the staple line of the proximal stomach just below the angle of His after SG, and at the gastrojejunal (GJ) anastomosis after RYGB. Mechanisms include ischemia from take-down of the short gastric arteries, mismatch in staple-height selection against the thinner fundic wall, downstream stenosis at the incisura angularis, and high intraluminal pressure in a poorly compliant sleeve. Consequently, most leaks are not present at the completion of the operation but develop over subsequent weeks — and therapy must address the propagating downstream stenosis, not only the leak orifice.

A **paradigm shift** runs through the leak recommendations: rather than closing the leak orifice, the goal is to lower intragastric pressure relative to the perigastric collection so that contents drain preferentially into the stomach and the leak closes by secondary intention ("keeping the fistula open"). Response is judged by the ability to tolerate diet and by a decrease in the size of the perigastric cavity — *not* by a decrease in the size of the leak orifice. Techniques are staged by defect size (<10 mm vs >10 mm), time since surgery (<6 weeks vs >6 weeks), and the presence of downstream stenosis (Figure 1).

**The document is ungraded.** Seven Best Practice Advice statements are provided with no evidence grades, strength ratings, or GRADE assessment; the methods state only that the advice is "based on available published evidence, including observational studies and systematic reviews, and incorporates expert opinion where applicable." It must not be cited as graded evidence.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Best Practice Advice (verbatim; 7 statements, ungraded)

1. **Best Practice Advice 1:** "Clinicians performing endoscopic approaches to treat early major postoperative complications should do so in a multidisciplinary manner with interventional radiology and bariatric/metabolic surgery co-managing the patient. Daily communication is advised."
2. **Best Practice Advice 2:** "Clinicians embarking on incorporating endoscopic management of bariatric/metabolic surgical complications into their clinical practice should have a comprehensive knowledge of the indications, contraindications, risks, benefits, and outcomes of each of the endoscopic treatment techniques. They should also have knowledge of the risks and benefits of alternative methods such as surgical and interventional radiological based approaches."
3. **Best Practice Advice 3:** "Clinicians incorporating endoscopic management of bariatric/metabolic surgical complications into their clinical practice should have expertise in interventional endoscopy techniques, including but not limited to: using concomitant fluoroscopy, stent deployment and retrieval, managing stenosis, and managing percutaneous drains."
4. **Best Practice Advice 4:** "Clinicians should screen all patients undergoing endoscopic management of bariatric/metabolic surgical complications and dietary intolerance for comorbid medical (nutrient deficiencies, infection, pulmonary embolism) and psychological (depression, anxiety) conditions."
5. **Best Practice Advice 5:** "Endoscopic approaches to managing complications of bariatric/metabolic surgery may be considered for patients in the immediate, early and late postoperative periods depending on hemodynamic stability."
6. **Best Practice Advice 6:** "Clinicians incorporating endoscopic management of bariatric/metabolic surgical complications into their clinical practice should have a detailed understanding of the pathophysiologic mechanisms initiating and perpetuating conditions such as staple-line leaks. This will allow for a prompt diagnosis and appropriate therapy to be targeted not only at the area of interest, but also any concomitant downstream stenosis."
7. **Best Practice Advice 7:** "Clinicians should recognize that the goal for endoscopic management of staple-line leaks are often not necessarily initial closure of the leak site, but rather techniques to promote drainage of material from the perigastric collection into the gastric lumen such that the leak site closes by secondary intention."

### Operative detail by complication

- **Comorbid screening (BPA 4):** patients with severe persistent vomiting about to undergo emergent general anesthesia (often with paralysis) should be **tested and treated for potassium deficiency**. In the weeks after surgery, individuals should be **screened regularly and prophylactically treated for thiamine deficiency** (Wernicke's encephalopathy has been reported). Severe food intolerance plus concomitant proton pump inhibitor use → also test for **magnesium and calcium deficiency**.
- **Immediate postoperative endoscopy (BPA 5):** use **carbon dioxide insufflation**; minimize pressure along fresh staple lines when advancing into the small bowel; if the patient is critically ill or the endoscopist lacks experience with the scenario, perform endoscopy **in the operating room with a surgeon present** (preferably the operating surgeon).
- **Upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage:** most commonly at staple lines or from an acute marginal ulcer after RYGB. Low threshold for general anesthesia with airway protection; **overtube** for massive bleeding and clot removal. Prefer **through-the-scope (TTS) clips over thermal ablative methods** to minimize the risk of a consequent leak. Where TTS clips fail: hemostatic powder (TC-325), over-the-scope clips, Padlock clip, or endoscopic suturing. Mechanical hemostasis also allows prompt restart of venous thromboembolism prophylaxis.
- **Acute luminal obstruction** (postoperative edema or hematoma at the anastomosis): consider a self-expandable metallic stent (SEMS). Obstruction after SG at the incisura angularis or at a prior laparoscopic adjustable gastric band site → **covered esophageal SEMS secured with endoscopic suturing or an over-the-scope stent-fixation clip** is preferred; securing is mandatory because the distal end may lie beyond the pylorus or GJ anastomosis. Obstruction at the GJ anastomosis after RYGB → **lumen-apposing metal stent** if the anatomy permits. **Large-caliber stents (15–20 mm) are safe and will not cause dehiscence of fresh staple lines.**
- **Functional stenosis — pneumatic dilation technique (BPA 6):** balloons **8–10 cm long, 30–40 mm in diameter**, though **dilation should rarely exceed 35 mm**. Performed with the endoscope **side-by-side with the balloon** (not through-the-scope, as would be used for a GJ anastomotic stricture) so endoscopic and fluoroscopic views are maintained throughout. Fluoroscopy is recommended though not mandatory, to confirm the distal balloon has not crossed the pylorus. The endoscope is placed flush on the proximal aspect of the balloon; an **isolated ischemic ring should be seen at the stenosis** — confluent mucosal blanching means the sleeve lumen may be too small for that balloon. **Inflate to 20 psi for 1–3 minutes**, then reinspect for a tear. A tear involving the **muscularis propria or beyond** → attempt endoscopic suturing to close the defect. If perforation is uncertain, inject contrast to flood the stomach and assess for extravasation. Dilations may be performed **as early as 2 weeks after surgery**.
- **Refractory incisura stenosis:** not responding adequately after **2–3 dilations (each 2–4 weeks apart) to a maximum of 35 mm** → deploy a **large-diameter fully covered SEMS (FCSEMS) up to 60 mm long** across the stenosis, secured, for **2 months**. Its sole purpose is to improve pressure and flow dynamics, not to cover a leak site; because such stents should not cross the gastroesophageal junction, they are generally well tolerated.
- **Why primary closure fails (the evidence behind BPA 7):** historically, treatment was percutaneous drainage (interventional radiology or surgical) followed by endoscopic closure of the leak — endoscopic suturing and over-the-scope clip (OTSC). **Recurrent leak is frequent when primary-closure methods are used in isolation.** Mechanisms behind failure are thought to be: **(1) poor integrity of the tissue surrounding the leak as a result of ischemia and inflammation; (2) difficulty obtaining a perpendicular endoscopic view of the leak for optimal repair; (3) failure to address high intraluminal pressures caused by downstream gastric stenosis or anastomotic stricture.** Attempts to close the leak site may not be the ideal strategy particularly **after 6 weeks, when the leak site is mature and epithelialized** — hence internal drainage "**maybe a superior approach**" (as printed) than closing the leak orifice.
- **Leak timing:** endoscopic approaches are generally **more successful for early and acute leaks (<6 weeks from surgery)**; chronic leaks more often require multiple endoscopies and a variety of strategies, with recalcitrant cases benefitting from salvage surgery.
- **Diversion with stents:** SEMS plus percutaneous drainage for early leaks; facilitates early diet and, with longer stents, can address downstream gastric stenosis. FCSEMS must be secured to reduce migration, and diversion may be suboptimal because the seal is not watertight. Partially covered SEMS (PCSEMS) negate those concerns but are hard to remove: PCSEMS should have a **maximum diameter of 18 mm in the body of the stent and dwell for a maximum of 3–4 weeks**. Removal = inverting the stent by grasping its terminal end and pulling with sufficient force, or placing a larger-diameter (**20-mm body**) FCSEMS inside the PCSEMS and removing both **1 week later**. Regardless of stent type, tissue invagination and ulceration at the distal aspect often cause poor tolerability.
- **Internal drainage — when it cannot work alone:** a disorganized perigastric collection, high intragastric pressure secondary to downstream stenosis, or an existing **gastropleural fistula**. Once the cavity empties it contracts until obliterated. Worsening clinical state with an enlarged cavity means gastric-lumen pressure is not sufficiently lower than the collection — usually persistent downstream stenosis.
- **Internal drainage — the three approaches:** double pigtail stents through the leak, septotomy, or endoscopic vacuum therapy (EVT). Infected debris in the cavity → **necrosectomy every 2 weeks until the cavity is clean**. If a percutaneous drain exists and the patient is not responding, **lavage the collection via the drain every 4–6 hours**; a percutaneous drain on continuous free drainage reduces perigastric pressure and prevents internal drainage, so it should be **clamped and opened only for lavage**.
- **Double pigtail stents:** preferred over septotomy when a small-caliber tract connects the leak to the collection. Use **short, small-caliber stents (7F × 3 cm or 7F × 5 cm)** to minimize gastric and extragastric damage; 1 or 2 stents depending on orifice size. **Routine exchange every 2–4 weeks** until the cavity has contracted and is too small to accommodate a stent (**usually <2 cm**); frequent endoscopies also allow downstream stenosis or GJ stricture to be addressed.
- **Septotomy:** the septum is cut along the staple line to the base of the perigastric cavity to equalize pressures. **Cautery-enhanced TTS scissors** are the current best accessory; ideally the entire septum is cut in a single session. Bleeding at the staple line is rare and the technique is safe **as long as the cut does not extend beyond the base of the perigastric cavity**. May be superior to double pigtail stenting for large leak orifices and where the collection is in immediate proximity to the stomach.
- **Endoscopic vacuum therapy:** sponge on a nasogastric tube, **success rates exceeding 80%**; intracavitary and intraluminal approaches. A traditional sponge needs replacing **every 3 days** to avoid adherence to tissue; a handmade **open-pore film drainage system** (gauze wrapped around a nasogastric tube, wrapped in thin plastic film) is less adhesive, reduces ingrowth and bleeding on removal, and can be replaced **every 7 days**. Nutrition should be provided parenterally regardless of technique.
- **Salvage:** where none of the above succeeds, a **cardiac septal occluder** has been placed across the leak site (limited experience; unclear whether it will embed in the digestive tract as it does in the vascular one). Surgery should also be offered, since several weeks or months may have passed since leak diagnosis. Three commonly proposed revision operations: **fistulojejunostomy**, **conversion of SG to RYGB (without gastrectomy)**, and **total or near-total gastrectomy with esophagojejunal anastomosis**. **Direct surgical repair of the chronic leak site is rarely effective and is not advised.** If a leak has not closed after reasonable endoscopic attempts, surgical revision is required.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No contradiction found with existing wiki content.** [[aga-2020-bariatric-surgery-cirrhosis]] addresses candidate selection and [[asge-2024-gerd]] the late reflux consequences of SG; this update addresses the early (<90 day) postoperative period and does not overlap their claims.
- **No standardized algorithm exists.** The authors state explicitly that evaluation and treatment algorithms are not standardized and that there is a paucity of high-quality prospective studies; Figure 1 is the authors' proposed approach, not a validated pathway.
- **Leak-specific evidence gaps:** the comparative effectiveness of double pigtail stents vs septotomy vs endoscopic vacuum therapy is not established, and experience with the cardiac septal occluder is described as limited with unclear long-term behavior.
- **No diagnostic criteria for leak or stenosis are given.** The update is a management document; it does not define the imaging or endoscopic criteria that establish either diagnosis, nor a severity grade.
