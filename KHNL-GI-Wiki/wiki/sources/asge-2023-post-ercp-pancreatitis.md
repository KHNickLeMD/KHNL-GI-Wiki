## Summary

First ASGE guideline dedicated to preventing **post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP)** — the most feared and most frequent serious adverse event of [[ercp]], occurring in ~8% of average-risk and ~15% of high-risk procedures, with mortality in ~1 in 500 patients. Using the GRADE framework, the panel answered five clinical questions covering four interventions across the periprocedural timeline: **rectal NSAIDs** (pre-procedure, all patients), **wire-guided cannulation** (intra-procedure), **prophylactic pancreatic-duct stents** (intra-procedure, high-risk), and **aggressive periprocedural hydration** (post-procedure).

Two strong recommendations (rectal NSAIDs in unselected and high-risk patients; PD stents when the PD is repeatedly/deeply accessed) and three conditional ones (wire-guided cannulation; PD stents when access is easily achievable; aggressive hydration). All are backed by moderate-quality evidence. A recurring theme is the **underuse** of these proven measures in North American practice — PD stents are placed in <10% of high-risk patients — even as PEP incidence and PEP-related mortality have not fallen.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Recommendations (verbatim, with GRADE strength/quality; "recommend" = strong, "suggest" = conditional):**

1. **Among unselected patients undergoing ERCP, the ASGE recommends periprocedural rectal NSAIDs to prevent PEP** (Strong / Moderate).
2. **For high-risk patients undergoing ERCP, the ASGE recommends periprocedural rectal NSAIDs to prevent PEP** (Strong / Moderate).
3. **In unselected patients undergoing ERCP, the ASGE suggests wire-guided cannulation over contrast-guided cannulation to minimize PEP** (Conditional / Moderate).
4a. **In patients undergoing ERCP with repeated or deep PD access, or performance of ampullectomy, the ASGE recommends PD stents to reduce PEP** (Strong / Moderate).
4b. **Otherwise, in high-risk groups (difficult cannulation, history of PEP, or precut sphincterotomy without fistulotomy technique), the ASGE suggests PD stent placement as long as PD access can be easily achieved** (Conditional / Moderate).
5. **In unselected patients undergoing ERCP, the ASGE suggests aggressive periprocedural and postprocedural IV hydration to prevent PEP** (Conditional / Moderate).

**Summary of evidence (pooled effects):**

- **Rectal NSAIDs, unselected patients** — 18 RCTs, 4817 patients. PEP odds roughly halved (OR 0.49; 95% CI 0.37-0.65). Trend toward less moderately-severe/severe PEP (OR 0.47; 0.21-1.06), not significant. No excess renal failure or postsphincterotomy bleeding. Cost-effective (ICER ~$33,812/QALY); modeled to remain cost-effective up to ~$1134/suppository despite the wholesale price of rectal indomethacin rising from $2 to $340 (2005→2019).
- **Rectal NSAIDs, high-risk patients** — 10 RCTs, 1008 patients. OR 0.49 (0.30-0.83), a 50% relative reduction. Trend toward less moderate/severe PEP (OR 0.53; 0.27-1.05). No excess renal failure or bleeding.
- **Wire-guided vs contrast-guided cannulation** — updated Cochrane meta-analysis (Tse et al), 15 RCTs, 4426 patients. PEP reduced (RR 0.50; 0.31-0.72) with no increase in bleeding or perforation. Benefit was seen in trials that did **not** permit PD stents (RR 0.24) but not in trials that allowed stents (RR 0.78) — i.e., cannulation technique matters most before a stent is in play. Greatest benefit when the wire is used to *confirm* catheter position once the cannulatome is already seated in a duct (RR 0.29) rather than advancing the wire ahead to negotiate access (RR 0.66, NS).
- **Prophylactic PD stents, high-risk patients** — 17 RCTs, 1595 patients. Overall PEP reduced 65% (OR 0.35; 0.26-0.46). Uniquely among the interventions, stents also reduced **moderate** (OR 0.38) and **severe** (OR 0.20) PEP. Severe PEP: 1% (13/1303) no-stent vs 0/1292 stent. Technical success 97%. Cost-effective (ICER ~$9,316-11,766/QALY).
- **Aggressive hydration, unselected patients** — 12 RCTs, 3524 patients. PEP reduced (OR 0.47; 0.34-0.66) vs moderate hydration, with no excess volume overload (OR 1.14; 0.49-2.67). Trend toward less moderately-severe/severe PEP (OR 0.60; 0.34-1.08). Cost-effective in high-risk (ICER ~$28,002/QALY) but **not** unselected (ICER ~$139,004/QALY) patients, given the assumed extra inpatient day. The FLUYT trial found no added PEP reduction when aggressive hydration was layered on NSAIDs (trend toward less severe PEP only).

**Practical execution pearls (from Table 1 "General concepts"):**

- **Rectal NSAIDs** — use **indomethacin 100 mg** (or diclofenac 100 mg) in adults; give **>30 min before or during** the procedure (timing 90 min pre → recovery room did not change efficacy). Pediatric dosing per pediatrician. **Avoid** with recent peptic ulcer disease, renal insufficiency (Cr >1.4), or aspirin/NSAID allergy. Oral/IV routes are **not** reliably effective — the rectal route is what the evidence supports.
- **Wire-guided cannulation** — cannulate, then advance the wire; an experienced operator should manipulate the wire; avoid forceful or repeated wire advancement into the PD (side-branch perforation risk).
- **PD stent** — use a **3F-5F stent (preferably 5F)** without internal flange, 3-7 cm long; if the wire cannot pass beyond the head, a short 2-3 cm stent may be used; obtain an **abdominal x-ray** to confirm spontaneous migration and **remove in 2-4 weeks** if retained. Imaging can be skipped if a follow-up ERCP is already planned.
- **Aggressive hydration** — **lactated Ringer's**, periprocedural bolus **20 mL/kg** then **3 mL/kg/h for 8 h**. Most feasible for inpatients; a shorter, higher-rate 2-3 h protocol (Brown et al) may suit outpatients but is less well studied. **Avoid** in CHF, renal insufficiency, or advanced liver disease.

**Future directions flagged by the panel:** single-dose NSAID safety in currently excluded groups (PUD, renal insufficiency, prior pancreatitis) and true low-risk patients; NSAID mechanism/route; head-to-head of the two wire-cannulation techniques; timing/indications for PD stents (place regardless of prior PD access vs only after inadvertent access); shorter high-feasibility hydration protocols; combination vs single-agent prophylaxis (large ongoing NSAID ± stent trial); standardized high/intermediate/low-risk definitions; and a validated personalized PEP risk calculator.

## Relevance to Wiki

- **Directly governs PEP prophylaxis for [[ercp]]** — the four pillars: rectal indomethacin/diclofenac 100 mg for **all** comers, wire-guided cannulation, 5F PD stent for high-risk (especially after repeated/deep PD access or ampullectomy), and LR-based aggressive hydration. Supersedes the PEP-prophylaxis fragments in [[asge-2015-benign-pancreatic-disease]] and [[asge-2015-ercp-benign-biliary]].
- **Reinforces the "difficult cannulation" risk pathway** — repeated/deep guidewire PD access is a leading modifiable PEP risk factor, which is why 4a is a *strong* stent recommendation.
- **Hydration principle parallels [[acute-pancreatitis]]** — LR preferred over normal saline, mirroring early goal-directed fluid management in AP (and the moderation signal from the WATERFALL trial for established AP — aggressive resuscitation for *prevention* differs from *treatment* of established pancreatitis).
- Cross-links [[endoscopic-ultrasound]] where EUS substitutes for diagnostic ERCP to avoid PEP risk entirely.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **NSAIDs in low-risk patients:** trials excluded very-low-risk indications (e.g., biliary stent exchange); the Levenick RCT of consecutive/unselected patients — which included such patients — showed **no** protective effect, so the benefit in genuinely low-risk cases is unproven.
- **Additivity of interventions:** FLUYT showed no incremental PEP reduction from aggressive hydration added to NSAIDs; whether NSAIDs + PD stent beats NSAIDs alone is being tested in a large ongoing RCT. The panel could not confirm combination benefit.
- **When to place a PD stent:** debate persists over placing a stent regardless of prior PD access vs only after the PD has already been accessed — seeking out the PD purely to stent may itself provoke PEP.
- **Underuse gap:** despite strong evidence, PD stents are used in <10% of high-risk patients and PEP incidence/mortality have not declined — an implementation problem the guideline explicitly calls out.
- **Evolving "high-risk" definition:** older stent trials used risk definitions (heavily weighted to sphincter of Oddi dysfunction) that predate current practice, where SOD is now recognized as a suboptimal ERCP indication (post-EPISOD).
