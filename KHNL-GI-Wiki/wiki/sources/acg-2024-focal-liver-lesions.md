## Summary

This ACG guideline covers the diagnosis and management of focal liver lesions (FLLs) — solid or cystic — found mostly incidentally, and is focused predominantly on people **without known liver disease**. A lesion in a patient with [[cirrhosis]] is referred to American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) guidance (Figure 1). It prefers the term "lesion" over "mass", in keeping with the Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System lexicon.

The workup is history (prior cancer, constitutional symptoms, hormones/steroids, liver-disease and metabolic risk factors), labs, and **multiphasic contrast-enhanced imaging — preferably magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) with late arterial, portal venous and delayed phases** (Rec 1). CT and MRI show no significant difference in diagnostic accuracy, though MRI has advantages; contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is another option. A hepatobiliary MRI agent is the tool for **focal nodular hyperplasia (FNH) vs hepatocellular adenoma (HCA)**; otherwise an extracellular agent is favored by expert opinion. Lesions that stay uncharacterized go to a multidisciplinary liver tumor board.

[[hepatocellular-adenoma|HCA]] gets the most management detail: hormone withdrawal and weight loss, molecular subtype (Table 4), and pathways set by **sex, size (5 cm), β-catenin status and pregnancy**. [[focal-nodular-hyperplasia|FNH]] and [[hepatic-hemangioma|hemangioma]] need no follow-up once confirmed (hemangioma exception: cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B). Cystic lesions are covered from simple cysts (expectant management regardless of size) through polycystic liver disease (PCLD; somatostatin analogs), mucinous cystic neoplasm of the liver (MCN-L), choledochal and hydatid cysts. Rarer malignant solid lesions — hepatic epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (HEHE), fibrolamellar HCC (FLHCC), angiosarcoma — get key concepts only.

## Key Findings / Claims

### General Workup and Imaging

- Incidental liver lesion on routine non-surveillance imaging in up to **15%** of patients (American College of Radiology); up to **52%** of patients without cancer have a benign liver lesion at autopsy
- No further workup needed when ultrasound (US) or single-phase CT appearance is characteristic of a hemangioma or a benign uncomplicated cyst
- Up to **95%** of FLLs seen on grayscale US can be diagnosed with proper contrast-enhanced imaging, without biopsy
- **Multiphasic protocol:** intravenous contrast with late arterial, portal venous and delayed phases — on CT a **triple-phase liver protocol** (routine abdominal CT has only a single portal venous phase); on MRI a dynamic postcontrast liver MRI
- CEUS for benign FLL characterization: sensitivity **97.8%**, specificity **83.9%**, positive predictive value **82.2%**
- CT vs MRI: no statistically significant difference in diagnosis (KC 3); MRI is generally favored for suspected benign FLLs, and its lack of ionizing radiation suits younger patients who need ongoing surveillance
- **Contrast agent choice:**
  - Hepatobiliary agent → FNH is hyperintense on the hepatobiliary phase (HBP), most other lesions hypointense → in a young patient with no malignancy risk factors, hepatobiliary MRI separates FNH from adenoma
  - Other scenarios: no consensus; **extracellular agent favored** (expert opinion) — hepatobiliary agents cause acute transient dyspnea (respiratory motion) and less adequate dynamic-phase enhancement
  - Follow-up after a confirmed diagnosis may use either agent
- Diagnosis not made on imaging → multidisciplinary discussion, consider core biopsy; not characterizable and not amenable to biopsy → **surveillance imaging, interval individualized, generally 3–6 months**
- **Figure 1a (solid lesion flowchart) entry branches:** cirrhosis → AASLD HCC guidance; **age <40 years with unremarkable history, exam and labs → MRI with hepatobiliary imaging**; all other patients → triple-phase contrast-enhanced CT, or dynamic MRI with hepatobiliary or extracellular contrast; history of malignancy → guidance for that malignancy or multidisciplinary discussion

### Hepatocellular Adenoma

**Epidemiology and risk:**

- Prevalence ~**0.007%–0.012%**; oral contraceptive pill (OCP) users **3–4 per 100,000** vs **0.13–1.0 per 100,000** in nonusers (modern OCPs carry far less estrogen/progesterone than 1960s pills)
- Risk factors: OCPs, anabolic steroid use (men and women), obesity, glycogen storage disease (GSD), polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS); limited data on hormonal or steroid therapy in transgender individuals
- GSD (especially types Ia and III): high lifetime incidence, including adenomatosis; male predominance 2:1; presents as early as the second or third decade
- Lean patients with HCA: weight gain discouraged (HCA growth associated with higher body mass index)
- No formal recommendation for HCA screening by metabolic risk factors or OCP duration
- Hemorrhage risk up to **15%**; malignant transformation up to **5%** (reported as <5% elsewhere in the text); complications typically in lesions **>5 cm**, but can arise in smaller ones
- Stabilization or regression seen with removal of hormonal stimuli and aggressive metabolic risk-factor management

**Subtypes (Table 4)** — 3 main subtypes plus 2 less well understood (sonic hedgehog, unclassified); imaging and histology subtype up to **90%** of HCAs:

| Subtype | Frequency | Risk factors | Molecular | Hemorrhage | Malignancy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inflammatory | ~35%–45% | Excess estrogen (moderate association), obesity, hepatic steatosis, excess alcohol, GSD | Activated JAK/STAT; elevated alkaline phosphatase, C-reactive protein (CRP), ± fibrinogen | Low | Moderate |
| HNF-1α | ~35%–40% | Excess estrogen (**strong** association: OCP use, excess estrogen, anabolic steroids); MODY3 | Inactivated HNF-1α transcription factor | Low | Low |
| β-catenin activated | Exon 3 ~10%, male predominant (CTNNβ1 exon 7/8 <10%) | Anabolic steroid use, androgen therapy, male sex, GSD | β-catenin mutation → increased glutamine synthase | Low | **High, up to 46% (exon 3)** |
| Sonic hedgehog | <5% | Exogenous hormonal stimuli, obesity | Sonic hedgehog activation; INHβE/GLI1 fusion | **High** | Low |
| Unclassified | 5%–10% | — | — | — | — |

*JAK/STAT = Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription · HNF-1α = hepatocyte nuclear factor-1α · MODY3 = maturity-onset diabetes of the young type 3 · INHβE = inhibin βE chain · GLI1 = glioma-associated oncogene homolog 1*

- Text detail: inflammatory up to 44% of cases, prone to hemorrhage (large, subcapsular, vascular), malignant risk low unless a concomitant β-catenin mutation; evidence insufficient to check CRP or fibrinogen routinely
- HNF-1α: up to 40%; steatosis often prominent; hemorrhage and malignant risk among the lowest
- β-catenin exon 3: up to 10%; most prevalent in men, GSD, anabolic steroid use; young, often large; **highest risk of transformation to HCC**; exon 3 ≈ ≥10% malignant risk vs no significant increase with exon 7/8
- Next-generation sequencing proposed to aid subtyping when tissue allows

**Imaging (Table 5 and text):**

| Feature | Inflammatory | HNF-1α | β-catenin |
|---|---|---|---|
| T2 | Marked hyperintense; atoll sign (T2-bright rim, isointense center) | Isointense / mildly hyperintense | Heterogeneously hyperintense |
| Intralesional lipid | −/+ (17%) | + (78%) | +/− |
| Arterial phase | ++ | + | ++ |
| Portal venous / delayed | ++ | +/− | — |
| HBP | 11% iso- to hyperintense (OATP1B3) | Hypointense | 59% iso- to hyperintense (OATP1B3) |

*HBP = hepatobiliary phase · OATP1B3 = organic anion transporting polypeptide 1B3*

- Extracellular-agent CT/MRI: arterial phase hyperenhancement (APHE) becoming isoenhancing on portal venous and equilibrium phases; HBP usually devoid of signal (unlike FNH)
- HBP-hyperintense atypical adenomas are a diagnostic dilemma → biopsy, follow, and/or multidisciplinary tumor board
- CEUS can aid HCA diagnosis but cannot subtype
- MRI cannot reliably separate β-catenin HCA from well-differentiated HCC (both APHE, washout, capsule); without chronic liver disease risk factors, adenoma is more likely
- Any lesion in a cirrhotic liver is HCC until proven otherwise
- **Imaging change suggesting malignant transformation:** rapid size increase, new intralesional lipid, new washout → consider biopsy. Pathology features favoring HCC; glypican-3 / heat shock protein 70 / glutamine synthetase panel helps; indeterminate → "atypical hepatocellular neoplasm" / "hepatocellular neoplasm of uncertain malignant potential" → multidisciplinary discussion
- No diagnostic serum biomarker; order **alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)** if malignant transformation is suspected (low sensitivity; elsewhere described as not reliable)

**Management by group (text):**

- **Women, HCA <5 cm:** monitor conservatively unless imaging suggests β-catenin; stop exogenous hormones; encourage weight loss if body mass index elevated (no established target); **repeat contrast-enhanced MRI after 6 months**; biopsy if indeterminate or malignancy concern; β-catenin exon 3 or HCC on biopsy → resection (not a candidate → locoregional therapy); **routine biopsy for subtyping not recommended** in this group
  - Growth **≥20%** (extrapolated from Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors [RECIST]) → consider resection or definitive treatment; also reasonable if the patient prefers treatment
  - Surveillance **every 6 months for 2 years, then annually**; after 2 years US could be considered, with contrast CT/MRI if anything changes; no data on when to stop (possibly after menopause)
- **Women, HCA ≥5 cm:** **6 months of observation** with cessation of OCPs, hormonal intrauterine devices and anabolic steroids, plus weight loss if applicable; non-β-catenin lesions observed at 6-month intervals; features concerning for malignancy → immediate treatment; **still ≥5 cm → surgical resection** (not a candidate → e.g. embolization); **regressed to <5 cm → continued contrast-enhanced MRI**, initially every 6 months; subtype biopsy not shown beneficial in the regressed group
- **Men:** **resect or treat definitively regardless of size or subtype**; stop anabolic/exogenous steroids, weight loss; **biopsy not advised before resection** but needed before nonsurgical therapy (ablation by size, or embolization); **laparoscopic resection strongly preferred** over nonsurgical modalities; ablation limited in larger lesions, embolization may be incomplete → bridge to definitive therapy
- Elective locoregional therapy (ablation, embolization): only case reports/small series → **cannot be recommended routinely**
- After resection, embolization or ablation: **surveillance every 6–12 months** (frequency and duration not well established)
- **Pregnancy:** HCA may grow; greatest complication risk in the **third trimester**; no single validated approach — **US every 6–12 weeks** is a recommended strategy
  - <5 cm, nonexophytic, no growth → safely monitored; no effect on mode of delivery (prospective study of 51 pregnant women with <5 cm HCA: a quarter grew, a quarter regressed, no hemorrhage)
  - Growth, particularly **>6.5 cm**, or high-risk features for rupture (including exophytic) → **resection if early in pregnancy, or embolization before 26 weeks' gestation**; third-trimester rupture → emergent surgery with obstetrics; lesions >6.5 cm bleed during pregnancy, delivery and postpartum
  - Preemptive treatment during pregnancy of HCA **without** high-risk features is **not recommended**
  - **Before planned conception:** HCA <5 cm → consider resection or embolization only if particularly high risk of growth or hemorrhage (prior hormonal growth, exophytic); **HCA ≥5 cm should be treated**
- **Rupture:** risk factors ≥5 cm, growth, exophytic location; supportive care, hemodynamic stabilization, transfusion; persistent instability → **emergent transarterial embolization (TAE)** until resection; after spontaneous cessation the course is not well defined — surgical candidates should have residual HCA resected laparoscopically; post-treatment monitoring
- **Malignant transformation:** <5%; highest risk in men (**10× females**), β-catenin exon 3, lesions **≥5 cm**; GSD also higher risk; non-surgical candidates → HCC therapies (ablation, embolization, radiation, transplant)
- **Adenomatosis (≥10 adenomas):** often HNF-1α on a steatotic background, or GSD (undiagnosed GSD → genetic testing); bleeding up to **46%**, malignancy up to **7%**; unilobar → resection; bilobar → treat the largest/dominant or complicated lesion; imaging **at least annually**
- **Liver transplantation:** rare indication per Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) policy — GSD, unresectable β-catenin-positive adenoma, or unresectable progressive HCA with hemorrhagic or malignant complications; priority score may be offered

### Focal Nodular Hyperplasia

- **Second most common solid liver lesion**; **0.3%–3%** at autopsy; up to **20%** also have a hemangioma; female preponderance (women: larger lesions, earlier)
- Local reaction to aberrant hemodynamics (dystrophic artery or vascular injury) → hyperplastic microenvironment
- Sex-hormone causation disproven: no correlation of OCP use with growth or prevalence; no change in pregnancy
- Hepatobiliary-agent MRI (e.g. gadoxetic acid) is the preferred modality — FNH vs adenoma accuracy **>90%**; MRI specificity almost **100%**; MRI contraindicated → multiphase CT; CEUS more accurate in lesions **<3 cm**
- Imaging: homogeneous arterial hyperenhancement → isoenhancing on portal venous/delayed; central scar hypodense/hypointense on noncontrast, delayed enhancement, iso- to hyperintense on T2; **HBP hyperintense at 20 minutes** (only **2%** hypointense)
- HBP-hyperintense lesion with atypical dynamic appearance → follow closely (11% of inflammatory, 59% of β-catenin adenomas express OATP1B3)
- Imaging non-diagnostic → biopsy after tumor board; diagnosis rate as low as **58%** in some series, aided by immunohistochemistry
- Confirmed → no follow-up; no need to avoid or stop OCPs/hormonal therapy; no follow-up in pregnancy
- Growth: **extremely slim chance of malignancy** → resection not recommended; growing lesions do not cause life-threatening complications
- Symptoms (e.g. pain) → resection may be considered, with counseling that symptoms may not improve; TAE logical (single feeding artery) — small series: TAE with bleomycin ~**50%** size decrease; bland embolization often used

### Hemangioma

- **Most common benign noncystic liver lesion** — up to **20%** of the population; female preponderance **4:1**; few millimeters to **>20 cm**; congenital, growth from vascular ectasia
- Usually asymptomatic; large lesions → pain, poor appetite, fullness; **Kasabach–Merritt syndrome** (consumptive coagulopathy: thrombocytopenia, systemic bleeding, disseminated intravascular coagulation), usually giant cavernous
- No causative link to female sex hormones → **OCPs and pregnancy need not be avoided**
- Types: **cavernous, capillary, sclerosed**
- Modality performance: **MRI** sensitivity **92%–100%**, specificity **85.7%–99.4%** (best); **CT** sensitivity **98.3%**, specificity **55.0%**; **CEUS** accuracy **97.3%** (other series 92.7%; diagnostic rate 90.2% in 103 patients)
- Cross-sectional confirmation usually needed, especially with hepatitis B, cirrhosis or a history of malignancy
- US: homogeneous hyperechoic with posterior acoustic enhancement (usually <3 cm); avascular on color Doppler. **Atypical/nondiagnostic US or lesion >2 cm → CEUS, CT or MRI**
- CT/MRI: hypodense/T1-hypointense; peripheral nodular enhancement → progressive centripetal fill; light-bulb bright on T2; hyperintense on low b-value diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), signal falls with rising b-value
- **Giant cavernous:** >10 cm (some authors >4–5 cm); calcification, central nonenhancement, heterogeneous enhancement possible
- **Capillary (flash-filling):** usually <1 cm; rapid homogeneous arterial enhancement; may show flow on US
- **Sclerosed:** thick peripheral rim, non-centripetal non-nodular progressive enhancement, heterogeneous T2, restricted diffusion, capsular retraction → mimics intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma or hypovascular metastasis; compare prior imaging, occasionally biopsy
- Biopsy generally avoided; bleeding risk **0.15%** with small needles
- Confirmed → no follow-up imaging, **except cirrhosis or HCC risk** (lesions can mimic hemangioma early in malignancy) → follow AASLD HCC guidance for FLLs
- **Surgery avoided when the main indication is discomfort or anxiety** (symptoms nearly always recur); indications = complications (rupture, intratumoral hemorrhage, consumptive coagulopathy, organ/vessel compression); spontaneous rupture exceedingly rare
- Surgery open or laparoscopic; enucleation most common, then nonanatomical resection; **>15 cm** → more surgical complications
- Not a surgical candidate → ablation (radiofrequency or microwave) for lesions **<3.5 cm**, radiation, TAE; liver transplant in a few reports of life-threatening Kasabach–Merritt
- **Asymptomatic hemangiomas: no intervention or follow-up regardless of size**

### Solid Lesions of Malignant Potential

- Underlying liver disease → HCC tops the differential; refer to AASLD HCC guidance; cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) → CCA guidance
- **Neuroendocrine tumor (NET)** liver metastases (portal-drained primary resected, disease stable): liver transplant 5-year survival ~**50%**, up to **70%** with selection → refer early
- **Metastases:** MRI with hepatocyte-specific contrast + DWI most sensitive and specific; gadobenate per-lesion sensitivity **77.8% / 88.1% / 95.1%** (precontrast / dynamic / combined with delayed HBP), similar to gadoxetate; contrast CT sensitivity **74%**. Known primary + new FLL → most likely metastasis → restaging and/or biopsy, no dedicated liver imaging
- **Colorectal liver metastases:** transplant may be considered if nonresectable, molecular criteria met, and response to chemotherapy for **≥6 months**, at an experienced center
- **HEHE:** incidence 1–2 per million; incidental in 25%; distant metastases at diagnosis ~**37%**, regional 27.5%; median survival **182 months**; poor prognosis (often <1 year) with lung/multiorgan disease, ascites with serosal metastasis, age >55, male sex. Signs: capsular retraction, **lollipop sign**, **target sign**; pathology required; R0 resection **70%–80%** cure; liver transplant 5-year survival **77%**; extrahepatic disease not a transplant contraindication
- **FLHCC:** <1% of US primary liver tumors; highest in White men <40 years (bimodal 15–19 and 70–74 years); noncirrhotic liver; DNAJB1-PRKACA fusion; AFP usually not elevated (elevated → poor survival); **must be diagnosed by biopsy**; HBP hypointense; central scar in up to half (FNH confusion); surgery even with nodal metastases (recurrence 5%–86%); transplant 5-year survival **48%** → only liver-localized unresectable disease; systemic therapy via clinical trials
- **Angiosarcoma:** <1% of primary malignant liver tumors; risk factors thorium dioxide, vinyl chloride, arsenic, radiation; ~80% older than 50; 1-year survival **12.8%**; resection prolongs survival; adjuvant therapy not routine; refer to a sarcoma center

### Cystic Lesions

- Prevalence **2.5%–18%** in recent US/CT series (0.2%–1% in early laparotomy series)
- High-risk features: **septations, fenestrations, calcifications, mural thickening or nodularity, heterogeneity, daughter cysts**

**Simple cysts:**

- Cuboidal biliary epithelium, no biliary communication; female predominance; no established OCP or pregnancy link; prevalence rises with age
- Grayscale US sensitivity and specificity **90%**; anechoic, through transmission, smooth margins; **2.5%–5%** have up to 2 septa → still no further imaging or follow-up
- CT: no internal architecture, fluid attenuation **<20 Hounsfield units**, no enhancement; MRI: T1 hypo, T2 hyper, no enhancement, falling signal at high b-value
- No intervention or follow-up regardless of size unless symptoms or high-risk features; high-risk US features → CT or MRI to exclude infected cyst, pyogenic abscess, cystic metastasis, hydatid cyst, MCN-L
- Symptomatic → surgical fenestration (deroofing/marsupialization; lowest recurrence, allows histology; open vs laparoscopic by candidacy, preference, expertise) or aspiration sclerotherapy (100% ethanol, tetracycline, others; benefit may take **6 months** → no repeat intervention within 6 months); **aspiration alone not recommended** as definitive therapy; no postintervention imaging
- Serum carbohydrate antigen (CA) 19-9 elevated in up to **50%** of these patients; cyst-fluid CA 19-9 does not track malignancy

**PCLD:**

- Autosomal dominant PCLD: at least **>10–20** simple-type cysts, few or no kidney cysts; ciliopathy spectrum
- Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD): **60%–80%** have hepatic cysts → **screen ADPKD with abdominal US**; PCLD → initial kidney US to exclude ADPKD
- Isolated PCLD prevalence 1–10 per million; genetic testing generally not recommended (6 known genes explain 30%–45%), may help in a young patient with few renal cysts not meeting ADPKD criteria
- Female **>80%**; estrogen-containing OCPs in premenopausal women → **15.5%** higher height-adjusted total liver volume per 10 years of use; pregnancy effect unclear
- CA 19-9 elevated in ~50% without malignancy; gamma-glutamyl transferase and alkaline phosphatase the most common enzyme rises
- **No imaging surveillance in asymptomatic PCLD** (no malignant potential)
- Aspiration sclerotherapy for large cysts (high recurrence); fenestration (**30%** recurrence; ascites, bile leak, pleural effusion, bleeding); resection when fenestration unlikely to work (laparoscopic preferred); **liver transplant ± kidney the only curative option**
- Severe symptoms + dialysis, glomerular filtration rate (GFR) <20 mL/min, or kidney transplant need → **simultaneous liver–kidney transplant**, exception points
- **Table 6 — Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) exception points** (PCLD **not clinically eligible for resection/fenestration or alternative therapy**): severe PCLD symptoms **plus any of** — hepatic decompensation or severe portal hypertensive complications; concurrent hemodialysis; **GFR <20 mL/min**; previous kidney transplant; moderate–severe protein-calorie malnutrition documented by a registered dietitian (Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition [GLIM] phenotypic criteria, American Society for Enteral and Parenteral Nutrition [ASPEN] criteria, Nutrition Focused Physical Examination [NFPE], or Subjective Global Assessment [SGA-C] score); severe sarcopenia — skeletal muscle index **<39 cm²/m² (women)**, **<50 cm²/m² (men)**, or equivalent
- **Somatostatin analogs:** largest effect in the first 6 months, lasting up to 2–4 years; largest RCT — **lanreotide long-acting release 120 mg intramuscularly every 4 weeks** → height-adjusted liver volume down almost **6%** (≈**140 mL**), a further **3.87%** 4 months after the last dose; symptom gains reported with >100 mL reduction; symptoms recur after stopping; <5% stop for intolerance; steatorrhea-type symptoms most common, self-limited
- mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors: everolimus added to octreotide — no significant benefit; ursodiol — no benefit in isolated PCLD (post-hoc benefit in ADPKD)

**MCN-L (formerly biliary cystadenoma/cystadenocarcinoma; World Health Organization [WHO] 2019):**

- <5% of liver cysts; ovarian-type stroma in the more common form (women); multilocular 90%; no biliary communication; strong female predominance; fifth–sixth decade
- Malignant transformation historically 20%–30%; **up to 10%** with updated WHO criteria
- CA 19-9 elevated in 28%–73% of biliary cystadenocarcinomas but does not discriminate
- Up to **76%** in the left lobe (segment IV); MRI preferred for cystic lesions; septations **95%** sensitive; septations arising from the wall **100%** sensitive / **56%** specific vs simple cyst; mural calcifications **90%** specific (seen in up to 65%); upstream biliary dilation highly specific
- **Complete surgical resection** (laparoscopic or open) is the gold standard; aspiration, sclerosis, partial resection or fenestration not recommended (recurrence up to **81%**); non-surgical candidates → surveillance imaging (no defined interval)

**Biliary hamartomas, peribiliary cysts, intraductal papillary neoplasm of the bile duct (IPNB):**

- Hamartomas (von Meyenburg complexes) usually <1.5 cm, peripheral; peribiliary cysts <1 cm, perihilar "string of pearls", often with chronic liver disease/portal hypertension — no surveillance
- IPNB: 40%–80% harbor malignancy; risk factors hepatolithiasis, clonorchiasis, primary sclerosing cholangitis, choledochal cysts, familial adenomatous polyposis, Gardner syndrome; treatment = bile duct resection ± hepatectomy; surveillance even after resection

**Choledochal cysts:**

- 1:4 male-to-female; more common in Asian populations; 80% present in the first decade; anomalous pancreaticobiliary junction (APBJ) reflux
- Todani types (Figure 15): **I** extrahepatic cystic/fusiform (most common); **II** supraduodenal diverticulum; **III** intraduodenal choledochocele (no female predominance, rarely malignant); **IV** IVA extra- + intrahepatic, IVB multiple extrahepatic (second most common); **V** intrahepatic only (Caroli disease; least common; Caroli *syndrome* adds congenital hepatic fibrosis and kidney cysts)
- Imaging: MRI with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography; MRCP detects APBJ without instrumentation; malignancy → diffuse, cyst → focal intrahepatic dilation
- Abdominal pain most common symptom (60%)
- Malignancy **7.5%–30%** (<1% in young children; 30%–40% over 50 years); CCA ~70%, gallbladder cancer 23.5%; types I and IV highest, II and III extremely rare; APBJ raises risk
- Treatment: I → complete excision + Roux-en-Y hepaticoenterostomy; II → excision or diverticulectomy; III → endoscopic unroofing (endoscopic or transduodenal sphincteroplasty) or transduodenal excision; IV → extrahepatic excision ± partial hepatectomy + hepaticoenterostomy, rarely transplant; V → hepatic resection or selected transplant. Types I and IV: surveillance even after resection (Figure 1b: continued MRI/MRCP surveillance for type I)

**Hydatid (echinococcal) cysts:**

- *Echinococcus granulosus*; liver 70%, lungs 20%; grow to 10–15 cm; high-pressure, rupture with trauma or manipulation → anaphylaxis, peritonitis, septic shock
- Imaging: water-lily sign (detached endocyst); serology useful but limited
- **Table 7 (WHO Informal Working Group on Echinococcosis):**

| Class | Echographic aspect | Stage | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| CE 1 | Univesicular fluid collection / simple cyst | Active | <5 cm: albendazole; >5 cm: PAIR + albendazole |
| CE 2 | Multivesicular with daughter cysts or septae (honeycomb) | Active | Catheterization or surgery + albendazole |
| CE 3A | Detached membrane (water lily sign) | Transitional | <5 cm: albendazole; >5 cm: PAIR + albendazole |
| CE 3B | Daughter cysts in solid matrix | Transitional | Catheterization or surgery + albendazole |
| CE 4 | Heterogeneous matrix, no daughter cysts ("wool clew") | Inactive/degenerative | Watch and wait |
| CE 5 | Solid matrix with calcified wall | Inactive/degenerative | Watch and wait |

*CE = cystic echinococcosis · PAIR = puncture, aspiration, injection of scolicidal agent, reaspiration*

- Albendazole superior to mebendazole; start before surgery or puncture and continue **1–6 months** afterward (duration varies by expert); **cyclical regimens no longer recommended**; monitor white cell count and liver tests (hepatic dysfunction, agranulocytosis); no dose given
- Medical therapy alone: **>40%** remain active or reactivate after 2 years
- Percutaneous or surgical treatment for cysts **>5 cm**, likely to rupture, unresponsive to medical therapy, or with contraindications to medical therapy; PAIR contraindicated with biliary fistula/communication, complex multiseptated, or inaccessible cysts; treat at centers with surgical and infectious-disease expertise
- Asymptomatic, inactive or calcified cysts may be observed

| Rec 12 | Asymptomatic simple hepatic cysts, **regardless of size** → **recommend** expectant management without routine surveillance or intervention | Strong | Low |
| Rec 13 | Simple cysts with high-risk US features (septations, fenestrations, calcifications, mural thickening or nodularity, heterogeneity, daughter cysts) → **recommend** further investigation with **CT or MRI** | Strong | Low |
| Rec 14 | Symptomatic simple hepatic cysts → **suggest** surgical cyst fenestration or aspiration with sclerotherapy | Conditional | Low |
| **Polycystic liver disease** | | | |
| Rec 15 | **Suggest** discontinuing exogenous estrogen in women with PCLD | Conditional | Very low |
| Rec 16 | PCLD with numerous small- to medium-sized cysts not amenable to resection, fenestration or aspiration sclerotherapy, **or** symptomatic ADPKD with concurrent PCLD → **recommend** medical management with somatostatin analogs | Strong | Moderate |
| **Hydatid/echinococcal cysts** | | | |
| Rec 17 | Complicated hydatid cysts (biliary fistula or biliary communication, multiseptated, rupture or hemorrhage, secondary infection, percutaneously inaccessible) → **suggest** surgical management, provided no contraindication to surgery | Conditional | Very low |
| Rec 18 | Uncomplicated hydatid cysts when surgery is not an option → **suggest** percutaneous PAIR with adjunct antihelminthic therapy | Conditional | Low |

## Key Concepts

All key concepts (Table 3 and text), numbered as in the guideline. **All are ungraded** (GRADE not applied).

| # | Key concept (ungraded) |
|---|---|
| **General** | |
| KC 1 | Asymptomatic patient, no known liver disease, incidental lesion → workup = history (prior cancer), constitutional symptoms (weight loss, appetite loss, fevers), medications (OCPs, steroids), chronic-liver-disease risk factors (viral hepatitis, transfusion, tattoos, intravenous drug use, alcohol excess), metabolic-syndrome features, blood tests (liver enzymes, tumor markers, viral hepatitis panel), and contrast-enhanced imaging (CEUS, MRI, CT) |
| KC 2 | Inadequately characterized and/or atypical FLLs → review at a multidisciplinary liver tumor board |
| KC 3 | Multiphasic postcontrast MRI and CT: no statistically significant difference in diagnostic accuracy, although MRI confers many advantages |
| KC 4 | Most solid FLLs in patients without risk factors are benign (hemangioma, adenoma, FNH) |
| **Hepatic adenoma** | |
| KC 5 | Risk factors: anabolic steroids (Table 3: in men), obesity, PCOS, GSD, and possibly exogenous hormonal therapy in men, women and transgender individuals |
| KC 6 | Generally benign, but can carry increased risk of hemorrhage and/or malignant transformation |
| KC 7 | Incidental solid mass, no known risk factors → multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT or MRI is the first step and often sufficient to diagnose and subtype an adenoma |
| KC 8 | Biopsy when an adenoma looks uncharacteristic on imaging or changes in a way concerning for malignant transformation |
| KC 9 | MRI features help subtype inflammatory and HNF-1α adenomas but are **not specific** for β-catenin, sonic hedgehog or unclassified adenomas |
| KC 10 | Inflammatory HCA risk factors: obesity and/or metabolic syndrome, heavy alcohol use, and/or GSD |
| KC 11 | β-catenin–mutated HCAs carry higher malignant-transformation risk than other subtypes and **should be resected regardless of size** |
| KC 12 | Adenomas in men are commonly β-catenin mutated, with higher malignant-transformation risk |
| KC 13 | Women with adenomas **≥5 cm**: modify risk factors, observe **6–12 months**, resect if the lesion does not regress to **<5 cm** |
| KC 14 | Men with adenomas: surgical resection **regardless of size** (malignant-transformation risk) — Table 3 says "should undergo"; the text box says "should consider" |
| KC 15 | Monitor adenomas regularly in pregnancy; treat if growth to **>6.5 cm** or high-risk features for hemorrhagic rupture |
| KC 16 | Adenoma of any size with imaging features of malignant transformation → treat as HCC (resection, locoregional therapy and/or transplantation) |
| KC 17 | Hepatic adenomatosis = **10 or more** adenomas; more often with background steatosis or GSD |
| KC 18 | Consider liver transplantation for patients meeting OPTN policy — especially GSD, unresectable β-catenin-positive adenoma, or unresectable adenoma with hemorrhagic or malignant complications |
| **Focal nodular hyperplasia** | |
| KC 19 | Advanced imaging (e.g. contrast-enhanced multiphase MRI with hepatobiliary-specific contrast) diagnoses FNH accurately in most cases; biopsy not routinely needed |
| KC 20 | FNH confirmed on imaging → no further follow-up |
| KC 21 | Confirmed FNH → resection not required even with growth; if resection is considered for symptoms, counsel that symptoms may not improve (FNH rarely causes symptoms) |
| KC 22 | Symptomatic FNH, surgery not an option (comorbidity, anatomy) → TAE with or without bleomycin may be considered to decrease size |
| KC 23 | Men with FNH: no different evaluation, monitoring or treatment from women |
| **Hemangioma** | |
| KC 24a | Small echogenic avascular lesion **<2 cm** with well-defined borders, normal liver, no medical history or risk factors for liver disease or malignancy → can be diagnosed as hemangioma on US |
| KC 24b | Lesion not meeting those criteria → multiphasic contrast-enhanced imaging to confirm |
| KC 25 | Suspected hemangioma not confirmed on cross-sectional imaging → monitor and review at a multidisciplinary tumor board |
| KC 26 | Avoid biopsy of a suspected hemangioma when possible (bleeding risk) |
| KC 27a | Confirmed hemangioma → no further follow-up **unless** cirrhosis or other malignancy risk such as hepatitis B |
| KC 27b | Pregnancy → no hemangioma monitoring, even for large cavernous hemangiomas |
| KC 28 | Asymptomatic large cavernous hemangioma (generally **>10 cm**) → surgical resection not indicated; no further follow-up |
| KC 29 | Resection indications = lesion complications (rupture, intralesional hemorrhage, consumptive coagulopathy, organ or vessel compression), which are rare; open or laparoscopic |
| KC 30 | Complications but surgery not an option → consider ablation (microwave or radiofrequency), radiation therapy, TAE, or very rarely liver transplantation; decide at a multidisciplinary tumor board |
| **Solid liver lesions of malignant potential** | |
| KC 31 | HCC, CCA, NET and metastatic colon cancer within transplant guidance/consensus criteria → refer early to an experienced liver transplant center |
| KC 32 | Suspected liver metastases → MRI with hepatobiliary contrast and DWI is the recommended modality |
| **Hepatic epithelioid hemangioendothelioma** | |
| KC 33 | Resection planned for imaging highly suspicious for HEHE → preoperative needle biopsy not necessarily needed |
| KC 34 | Diagnosed HEHE → stage with whole-body contrast-enhanced CT or MRI; positron emission tomography (PET)-CT/MRI may be considered (HEHE only mildly or moderately PET avid) |
| KC 35 | Resect HEHE whenever possible; not feasible → liver transplantation offers the best survival, even with extrahepatic disease |
| KC 36 | Nonresectable, nontransplantable HEHE → refer to a specialty center; ablation and stereotactic body radiotherapy show some response in small studies; no systemic therapy can be recommended |
| **Fibrolamellar HCC** | |
| KC 37 | FLHCC → surgical resection is the treatment of choice; limited liver-localized unresectable disease → transplant case by case |
| KC 38 | Neoadjuvant or adjuvant systemic therapy not recommended for FLHCC outside a clinical trial |
| KC 39 | FLHCC → biopsy to confirm the diagnosis; molecular analysis to guide systemic therapy not beneficial |
| **Hepatic angiosarcoma** | |
| KC 40 | Primary hepatic angiosarcoma → resection whenever feasible |
| **Hepatic cystic lesions** | |
| KC 41 | Asymptomatic complex hepatic cysts, regardless of size → multidisciplinary tumor board and consider surveillance imaging in **6–12 months** (worded "we recommend", but listed as an ungraded key concept) |
| **Polycystic liver disease** | |
| KC 42 | PCLD treatment goals: symptom relief and preservation of quality of life |
| KC 43 | Choice among aspiration sclerotherapy, fenestration or resection of dominant cyst(s) rests on cyst characteristics, hepatic reserve and center expertise |
| KC 44 | Liver transplant ± simultaneous kidney transplant → consider as a curative option for refractory symptoms from significant cyst burden |
| **Mucinous cystic neoplasms of the liver** | |
| KC 45 | Fluid aspiration or biopsy of MCN-L not recommended to tell benign from malignant (low sensitivity) |
| KC 46a | MCN-L with thick septations, fenestrations, nodularity, calcifications or mixed solid-cystic components → prompt evaluation for complete surgical resection |
| KC 46b | Not a surgical candidate → surveillance imaging (no interval can be recommended); changes suggesting malignant degeneration → multidisciplinary tumor board for nonsurgical options |
| **Biliary hamartomas and peribiliary cysts** | |
| KC 47 | Biliary hamartomas and peribiliary cysts are benign; no surveillance imaging |
| KC 48 | IPNB is premalignant with high transformation risk → continued surveillance imaging even after resection |
| **Intrahepatic choledochal cysts** | |
| KC 49a | Choledochal cyst management is based on cyst type and malignant-transformation risk |
| KC 49b | Types I and IV are most associated with malignancy → surveillance imaging (no interval can be recommended) |
| KC 50 | Types IV and V, resection not feasible → consider liver transplantation |
| **Hydatid/echinococcal cysts** | |
| KC 51a | Antihelminthic therapy is indicated before surgery or cyst puncture in symptomatic or active hydatid cysts (prevent recurrence and secondary seeding, lower cyst pressure) or in inoperable cases |
| KC 51b | Medical therapy alone not recommended (ineffective) unless percutaneous aspiration or surgery is contraindicated |

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **KC 14 wording:** Table 3 says men with adenomas "should undergo" resection regardless of size; the key-concept box in the text says "should consider"; the management text says adenomas in men "should be surgically resected or treated definitively"
- Inflammatory HCA malignancy: Table 4 lists "Moderate"; the text says the risk is low unless a β-catenin mutation coexists
- HCA malignant transformation: "up to 5%" in one section, "<5%" in another
- When to stop HCA surveillance: insufficient data (possibly after menopause)
- Elective locoregional therapy for HCA: case reports and small series only — not routinely recommended, yet Rec 7 suggests embolization or ablation when resection is not possible
- After spontaneous cessation of HCA hemorrhage, the treatment course is not well defined; HCA subtype does not clearly predict future hemorrhage
- Hydatid cysts: no prospective randomized comparison of surgical vs medical management; duration of perioperative antihelminthic therapy varies by expert
