## Summary

Expert commentary on managing patients with an enteral stoma (colostomy or ileostomy) beyond the immediate perioperative period — a gap area, despite ~750,000 Americans living with an ostomy and ~130,000 new ostomy surgeries/year in the US. Adequate stomal care improves outcomes and reduces hospitalizations.

The update reviews stoma types and configurations (end vs loop vs mucus fistula vs continent ileostomy/Kock pouch), output expectations (colostomy: formed stool ~once daily, appliance change q6–7 days; ileostomy: liquid effluent emptied 3–4×/day, wafer change ~q4 days), and complications by time course. **Early high ostomy output (HOO)** — output exceeding fluid intake, typically **>1.5 L/d**, within 3 weeks of stoma formation — is the key early ileostomy complication; the most important treatment is **hydration to prevent renal failure**, usually IV. **Chronic HOO** (>3 weeks) prompts a search for infection, structural problems, recurrent [[crohns-disease|Crohn's disease]], or other diarrhea etiologies, plus a stepwise medical ladder (bulking → antimotility → antisecretory → anti-inflammatory → GLP-2 analogues → surgical reversal). Long-term complications covered: peristomal dermatoses (leak-related excoriation, appliance allergy, fungal rash, folliculitis, peristomal pyoderma gangrenosum, peristomal varices), parastomal hernia (up to 50% at 5 years), and stomal prolapse (5%–10%). Preoperative wound ostomy continence (WOC) consultation with stoma site marking improves quality of life and reduces peristomal skin/pouching complications.

## Key Findings / Claims

*Commentary format — no numbered Best Practice Advice; key advice captured in full below.*

**Ostomy types and output expectations:**

- **Colostomy** (most common enteral stoma): from sigmoid, descending, or transverse colon; right colon avoided (large diameter, liquid effluent → large leak-prone stoma). Indications: [[colorectal-cancer|CRC]], perforated diverticulitis, trauma, Crohn's disease, fecal diversion (for [[fecal-incontinence|fecal incontinence]], sacral wound, spinal cord injury). Output **formed, bowel movements commonly once daily**; appliance changed **once every 6–7 days**.
- **Ileostomy:** constructed from terminal ileum close to the ileocecal valve (maximizes nutrient absorption). Indications: CRC, [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|IBD]], colonic dysmotility. Easier to construct and reverse than colostomy but **more dehydration and skin excoriation**. **Liquid effluent — empty 3–4 times daily**, change wafer **every 4 days on average**.
- Stoma must sit **above skin level** so effluent doesn't seep under the appliance (Brooke technique = suturing to protrude above skin).
- **End ostomy:** intestine divided, proximal end brought out; easiest to pouch; used for permanent stomas and perforation requiring resection. Reversal requires more invasive surgery (locate stapled-off distal end). **Mucus fistula** (distal end also brought out through a separate incision) generally avoided (2 stomas/abdominal wall defects) unless significant leak risk in the stapled-off end (distal obstruction, poor tissue integrity).
- **Loop ostomy:** continuous bowel loop through one aperture, 2 side-by-side openings; proximal end Brooked/dominant. Used for distal obstruction (e.g., obstructing cancer) and temporary diversion (protect distal anastomosis, heal sacral decubitus). Relatively easy to create and reverse.
- **Continent ileostomy (Kock pouch / Barnett Continent Intestinal Reservoir):** internal pleated-intestine pouch with nipple valve; no stool passage until intubated with a catheter; usually no appliance needed. Uncommon — high complication rate leading to revision surgery and intestinal loss.

**High ostomy output (HOO):**

- **Early HOO** = output greater than fluid intake (**typically >1.5 L/d**) within **3 weeks** of stoma formation, resulting in dehydration. Common with ileostomy; rare with colostomy.
- Prompt evaluation for infection (postoperative abdominal infection, [[clostridioides-difficile|C. difficile]]), ileus, or medication-related adverse effects when HOO occurs with a colostomy or is high with an ileostomy.
- **Most important treatment: hydration to prevent renal failure** — repletion volumes are large, so typically **IV**, requiring hospital admission or long-term IV access for home health care.
- Persistent early HOO → consider **early stoma reversal**, but reversal **before 6 weeks** from the index surgery carries increased complication risk.
- **Chronic (late) HOO** = persistent HOO **>3 weeks** after surgery, or new-onset HOO **>3 weeks** after creation. More likely with a jejunostomy, **short bowel (<200 cm small intestine remaining)**, or failed post-surgical adaptation. Ileal adaptation begins over days–weeks (mucosal changes altering electrolyte transport, absorptive capacity, motility). Etiologies of late HOO: infection, structural (stomal stricture/dysfunction, intestinal obstruction, ileus, enteric fistula), recurrent Crohn's disease, and common adult diarrhea causes ([[microscopic-colitis|microscopic colitis]], [[bile-acid-diarrhea|bile acid diarrhea]]).

**Table 1 — Treatment strategies for high ostomy output:**

| Type of treatment | Examples |
|---|---|
| Bulking agents | Psyllium fiber; guar gum; marshmallows |
| Antimotility agents | [[loperamide\|Loperamide]]; diphenoxylate + atropine; codeine; tincture of opium |
| Antisecretory agents | Proton pump inhibitors / H2 agonists; somatostatin analogues (octreotide) |
| Anti-inflammatory agents (if from recurrent Crohn's) | Consultation with IBD specialist |
| Adaptation-promoting agents | GLP-2 analogues (teduglutide, elsiglutide, glepaglutide, apraglutide) |
| Surgical | Reversal of the ostomy with restoration of intestinal continuity when possible |

**Short-term complications:**

- **Ostomy leakage** — most common and dreaded. Predisposing: obesity, placement within a skin crease, loop configuration, liquid effluent (ileostomy, chronic diarrhea), flush stoma. Best strategy is prevention (preoperative stomatherapist marking, meticulous technique). Management: thicken stool with antidiarrheals; bolster stoma height (convex appliance, ostomy belt, paste, barrier rings); heat appliance with hair dryer before application; lie flat several minutes after application; dry peristomal skin; stomal powder + skin sealant. Appliance opening cut **one-eighth inch larger than the stoma**; re-measure stoma at each change for the **first 8 weeks** (stoma shrinks). Refractory leakage may need revision.
- **Stomal retraction** — with ischemia or thick abdominal wall (obesity). Early retraction below the abdominal wall → sepsis, emergent surgical revision. Retraction within skin but above fascia → pouching issues/stricture; endoscopy or cross-sectional imaging delineates depth. Temporary stoma: manage symptomatically (convex appliance, belt, barrier ring) until reversal; permanent stoma often needs revision.
- **Mucocutaneous separation** — maturation sutures detach; manage with pouching solutions until healing by secondary intention; immunosuppressed patients at particular risk.

**Long-term complications:**

- **Peristomal skin:** most issues from appliance leakage (manage per above). Appliance **allergy** — itching/redness in the shape of the offending product; skin sealant, pouching-system change, temporary steroid spray. **Fungal infection** (moisture/heat) — itchy maculopapular rash with satellite borders; antifungal powder sealed with sealant; no improvement at 2 weeks → surgeon/enterostomal therapist. Painful ulcers with purple halo (esp. IBD) → assess for **peristomal pyoderma gangrenosum** (interdisciplinary GI/dermatology/wound-care management). Minor bleeding at appliance changes is normal; persistent/severe bleeding or purplish concentric peristomal discoloration → work up **peristomal varices**.
- **Parastomal hernia:** up to **50% of ostomates within 5 years**. Risk factors: obesity, smoking, steroid use, transverse colostomy. Prevention: place stoma **through the rectus**; avoid transverse colon when possible. **Incarcerated hernia** (painful, irreducible → obstruction/ischemia) = **surgical emergency**. Small reducible hernias → hernia belt. Elective repair reserved for significant pouching issues, pain, or recurrent obstruction. Most effective repair = **ostomy reversal**; otherwise minimally invasive repair usually with mesh; recurrence after repair **>25% at 2 years**. Stoma relocation has fallen out of favor (high hernia risk at new site).
- **Stomal prolapse** (elongation of the stoma's intestinal portion): rate **5%–10%**. Acute prolapse with incarceration/ischemia (pain, obstipation, purple/black stoma) = **emergency surgery**. If no ischemia: reduce with patient relaxed, gentle pressure; if irreducible, **table sugar applied to the stoma ×20 minutes** reduces edema and facilitates reduction. Mild, easily reducible, non-interfering prolapse → no surgery; otherwise revision or reversal.

**Perioperative considerations:**

- Address psychological impact (fear of leakage, odor, disclosure, clothing, intimacy, travel, self-care) with pre- and postoperative education.
- **Preoperative education + stoma site marking** improves quality of life and decreases peristomal skin and pouching complications. WOC marking assesses the abdomen in multiple positions for a site within the rectus, away from scars, skin folds, bony prominences, and umbilicus.
- WOC consultation manages expectations (output, appliances, regular mucus passage from the native rectum); community-based and online ostomy support groups exist.
