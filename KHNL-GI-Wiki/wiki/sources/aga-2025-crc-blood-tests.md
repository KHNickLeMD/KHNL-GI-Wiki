## Summary
AGA Expert Commentary reviewing the current evidence on blood-based tests for colorectal cancer (CRC) screening and their implications, leveraging decision-analytic modeling to identify the test performance, interval, and uptake needed for blood tests to approach the effectiveness of stool tests and colonoscopy. As an Expert Commentary it issues no Best Practice Advice and grades nothing; its five Summary Points and the narrative claims below are its content.

Two blood tests have population-based clinical-validation data. **Guardant Shield** (cell-free DNA, methylation) — validated in 22,877 adults aged 45–84; CRC sensitivity 83.1%, specificity 89.6%, advanced-precursor-lesion (APL) sensitivity 13.2%; stage 1 CRC sensitivity only 55%. It is Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved (May 2024) and Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS)-covered, commercially available. **Freenome** (cell-free DNA methylation) — validated in 48,995 adults aged 45–85; CRC sensitivity 79.2%, specificity 91.5%, APL sensitivity 12.5%; stage 1 sensitivity ~57%; expected to seek FDA/CMS approval. Both detect APLs and early-stage CRC poorly compared with stool tests, limiting cancer prevention and curative-stage detection.

Decision-analytic modeling (4 validated models) projects that triennial Guardant Shield yields substantially smaller reductions in CRC incidence (24%–47%) and mortality (53%–59%) than annual fecal immunochemical test (FIT) (45%–77% / 67%–81%), triennial Cologuard, or 10-yearly colonoscopy. At its $1450 list price triennial Shield is cost-effective vs no screening ($74,800–$102,300/quality-adjusted life-year [QALY]) but is less effective and more costly than FIT or colonoscopy; even at $300 it would remain inferior to FIT/colonoscopy. For triennial blood-based screening to approximate annual FIT, its APL sensitivity would need to rise to ~50%–80% and cost fall to <$125–$140.

The main attraction is potential improved adherence (only 60%–65% of eligible U.S. adults are up to date; offering a blood test to those who decline other screening increased uptake by 17.5% in one study). But population outcomes are inferior if blood tests *substitute* for FIT/colonoscopy: to match FIT/colonoscopy, participation would need to be ≥35% higher; outcomes are similar only if for every 3 people substituting a blood test there are 2 new screenees. Blood testing requires a *programmatic* approach: abnormal tests must be followed by colonoscopy (almost 50% of patients fail to complete timely colonoscopy within 3–6 months of a positive stool test), and normal tests need repeat testing at appropriate intervals. Multicancer detection tests (e.g., Galleri) remain investigational. The bottom line: novel blood tests may improve participation but could cause net harm if substituted for more effective tests; for patients who decline all other screening, they are an acceptable option.

## Summary Points (the document's only named statements)

Unnumbered and ungraded in the original; numbering here is the wiki's.

1. Currently, one blood test (Guardant Shield) is approved by FDA and CMS for CRC screening in **average-risk individuals every 3 years**.
2. Blood tests for CRC screening **may improve participation** in screening programs because of perceived ease of use.
3. Patients should understand that **if a blood test is positive, colonoscopy would be recommended**.
4. Based on one-time test performance characteristics and long-term modeling projections, programmatic screening with current blood tests **every 3 years is substantially better than no screening**, but would give **lower prevention rates for CRC or CRC death than programmatic FIT, Cologuard, or colonoscopy**.
5. The first available CRC screening blood tests are **best viewed as acceptable in persons who decline the established forms of CRC screening**.

*Conclusions add:* sensitivity of current blood tests for APLs and stage 1 CRC is low; uptake and optimal repeat testing remain unknown; novel tests **could result in net harm if substituted for more effective screening tests**.

## Key Findings / Claims
- **Guardant Shield (Guardant):** cell-free DNA methylation blood test. Validation: population-based study, 22,877 average-risk adults due for screening, 26 U.S. sites, ages 45–84, blood draw ≤60 days before screening colonoscopy; clinical-validation cohort 7861 (65 CRC, 1116 APLs). CRC sensitivity 83.1%, specificity 89.6%, APL sensitivity 13.2%; stage 1 CRC sensitivity 55%. FDA-approved May 2024, CMS-covered, commercially available. List price $1450.
- **Freenome:** cell-free DNA methylation blood test. Validation: 48,995 average-risk men and women across nearly 200 U.S. centers, ages 45–85, blood draw ≤120 days before screening colonoscopy; clinical-validation cohort 27,010 (72 CRC, 2567 APLs). CRC sensitivity 79.2%, specificity 91.5%, APL sensitivity 12.5%; stage 1 sensitivity 57.1%. Expected to seek FDA/CMS approval.
- **APL = advanced precancerous lesion:** adenoma or sessile serrated lesion ≥10 mm, villous histology, high-grade dysplasia, or carcinoma in situ.

### Table 1 — Comparative diagnostic performance of current CRC tests vs novel blood and stool tests

| Modality | Sens. CRC | Sens. advanced precursor lesion | Specificity | Invasive | Endorsed by U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) | Covered by CMS | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[colonoscopy]] | 96% | 95% | 90% | Yes | Yes | Yes | $800–$1500+ (CMS/commercial) |
| FIT (threshold 20 µg Hb/g stool) | 74% | 24% | 96% | No | Yes | Yes | $20–$25 |
| multitarget stool DNA (MT-sDNA; Cologuard) | 92% | 42% (advanced adenomas and sessile serrated lesions) | 87% | No | Yes | Yes | $500–$600 (CMS/commercial) |
| Next-generation MT-sDNA | 94% | 43% (advanced adenomas and sessile serrated lesions) | 93% | No | — | — | Unknown |
| multitarget stool RNA (MT-sRNA; ColoSense) | 94% | 46% (advanced adenomas) | 88% | No | — | — | Unknown |
| Shield blood test | 83.1% (55% for stage 1) | 13.2% | 89.6% | No | — | Yes | $1450 list price |
| Freenome blood test | 79.2% (57% for stage 1) | 12.5% | 91.5% | No | — | — | Unknown |

- Emerging stool tests (next-generation MT-sDNA, MT-sRNA) are tabulated for comparison but not reviewed in detail by this Clinical Practice Update (CPU).
- **Both blood tests:** lower sensitivity for stage 1 CRC and for APLs than stool tests → reduced potential for cancer prevention (poor APL detection) and curative treatment (suboptimal early-stage detection). APL sensitivity ≈13% was lower than expected. Neither study was **powered to evaluate differences in CRC sensitivity by stage**, so the stage 1 figures are descriptive. Performance figures are one-time test performances; optimal repeat interval unknown.
- **CMS coverage:** acceptable CRC-screening blood tests covered **every 3 years**; CMS minimum performance threshold is CRC sensitivity 74% and specificity 90% (implies ~10% APL positivity rate).
- **How novel tests are judged:** decades-long mortality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are not realistic, so novel CRC screening tests are judged on **one-time sensitivity for CRC and APL plus specificity vs an established comparator**, with decision-analytic modeling projecting long-term benefits, harms and costs. The hypothetical **anchor case** = the CMS minimum (74%/90%) and behaved very similarly to Guardant Shield across models. Testing frequency is a critical variable, driven by the CMS 3-year coverage decision; test cost is a critical determinant of cost-effectiveness.

### Table 2 — Summary results across 4 validated decision-analytic models

| Screening program | Reduction in risk of developing CRC vs no screening | Reduction in risk of dying from CRC vs no screening |
|---|---|---|
| FIT every year | 45%–77% | 67%–81% |
| MT-sDNA (Cologuard) every 3 y | 40%–73% | 64%–78% |
| Colonoscopy every 10 y | 57%–81% | 72%–88% |
| Guardant Shield every 3 y | 24%–47% | 53%–59% |

- Freenome projections for reducing CRC incidence and mortality were very similar to Guardant Shield.
- **Cost-effectiveness:** Triennial Shield at $1450 = cost-effective vs no screening ($74,800–$102,300/QALY) but less effective and more costly than FIT/colonoscopy. At $300 it could be cost-saving vs no screening but still inferior to FIT/colonoscopy. Shorter intervals (biennial/annual) improve benefit but costs rise faster than benefits.
- **Threshold to match annual FIT:** triennial blood test would need APL sensitivity ~50%–80% AND cost <$125–$140.
- **Substitution vs supplementation:** If blood tests substitute for currently recommended stool tests/colonoscopy, CRC outcomes are inferior. To equal FIT/colonoscopy, participation must be ≥35% higher; population outcomes similar only if for every 3 substituters there are 2 net-new screenees. The other routes to comparable effectiveness — **APL detection of ≥30%–50%, or annual testing** — would require technical improvement of the tests or changes in coverage decisions.
- **Real-world determinants:** benefits and costs are substantially affected by uptake (screened at least once vs never), participation per round over time (consistent vs intermittent), and completion of follow-up colonoscopy after an abnormal noninvasive screen. Neither FDA approval, CMS coverage determination, nor USPSTF recommendations weigh programmatic effectiveness, benefit/cost trade-offs and participation in aggregate → real-world implementation will rest largely on health systems, patients, doctors and payers.
- **Adherence:** Only 60%–65% of eligible U.S. adults up to date. In a study of patients who declined other screening, offering a blood test increased CRC screening by 17.5% vs usual care; an RCT showed adherence up to 11% higher for blood vs stool testing.
- **Programmatic requirement:** Abnormal blood test → must complete colonoscopy; normal → repeat at appropriate interval. ~50% of patients do not complete colonoscopy within 3–6 months of a positive stool test; delays raise CRC incidence/mortality. Whether those who decline screening colonoscopy will complete it after a positive blood test is unknown.
- **Multicancer detection tests (MCDs):** cell-free DNA (cfDNA) / circulating tumor DNA traced to organ of origin → possible early detection of CRC, esophageal, gastric, pancreatic, lung, breast, prostate and other cancers. Galleri (Grail) early validation — overall cancer sensitivity 51.5%, specificity 99.5%; Grail partnered with the UK National Health Service (NHS) to recruit **150,000 participants**, interim analyses expected. Galleri is available in the U.S. as a **laboratory-developed test with an out-of-pocket cost**. Remaining challenges: confirmatory imaging can be imperfect, cost/resource needs for confirming a cancer signal substantial, harm possible, and it is unclear whether screening for many cancer types reduces mortality or incidence once lead-time and length-time bias are considered. MCDs still require careful evaluation and validation.
- **Interval blood tests between screening colonoscopies:** premature to consider.
- **Framing the choice for patients:** the informed-decision process may become complicated for primary care providers, who play a key role in CRC screening. If screening options are presented **agnostic to effectiveness**, patients may pick the apparently simple blood test believing all tests are equal — the CPU poses whether patients would still choose it if told stool tests or colonoscopy are more likely to detect curable CRC or prevent CRC by detecting precursor lesions.
- **Disparities:** multiple studies document racial and ethnic disparities in screening; a recent study suggests the Black–White gap may be closing. Screening has improved across races/ethnicities, but the **lowest rates (50%) are in Hispanics**. Whether blood tests can overcome barriers of geography, cost or cultural norms — and whether they worsen or lessen disparities — remains to be seen.
- **Rural vs urban:** uptake and colonoscopy follow-up vary with access, setting and resources. **One-third or more of eligible patients do not complete a mailed FIT.** A blood test may remove barriers (no stool collection/shipping), but where access to primary care is limited or geographically distant a **lab visit for a blood draw may be harder than collecting a stool sample at home and mailing it**; incomplete payer coverage is a further access barrier. FIT can be implemented effectively in rural settings, though challenges remain — chiefly timely colonoscopy after a positive noninvasive test.
- **Organized vs opportunistic programs:** organized U.S. systems (health maintenance organizations such as Kaiser Permanente, Veterans Health Administration, Federally Qualified Health Centers, safety-net hospitals) have successfully run noninvasive-test programs — a Kaiser Permanente organized mailed-FIT outreach program increased CRC screening over 15 years and decreased CRC incidence and mortality. Whether a blood test performs as well in these settings vs opportunistic screening is unclear.
- **Hepatitis C screening as a parallel:** USPSTF recommended one-time "baby boomer" hepatitis C blood-test screening in 2013; nearly a decade later uptake remained variable — **84% in the Veterans Health Administration vs <20% in community and safety-net hospitals**. Diseases differ, but this illustrates the potential successes and failures of blood-test-based public health efforts across settings.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Positioning of blood tests vs the existing "Not Recommended — Septin-9" note in [[colorectal-cancer-screening]]:** American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2021 recommended against the older Septin-9 blood test; this 2025 CPU addresses newer cfDNA tests (Shield FDA/CMS-approved) without endorsing them as equivalent to FIT/colonoscopy — it positions them as acceptable mainly for patients who would otherwise decline all screening. Not a direct contradiction; the newer test class is distinct.
- **USPSTF status:** Table 1 marks colonoscopy, FIT and MT-sDNA as USPSTF-endorsed and leaves Shield and Freenome unendorsed ("—"); CMS covers Shield but not Freenome. So FDA approval and CMS coverage have run ahead of USPSTF endorsement for blood tests.
- **Author conflicts of interest are extensive and disclosed:** all four authors report financial relationships with blood- or stool-test companies, including Guardant and Freenome (royalties, consulting, stock/stock options). Weigh the "acceptable option" framing accordingly.
- **Unknowns flagged by the source:** optimal repeat interval, real-world uptake, whether improved participation compensates for lower efficacy, and downstream colonoscopy completion after a positive blood test.
