## Summary
- Second half of the first European consensus on malabsorption as a syndrome. **35 statements**, numbered by the document's own section headings: **2.1.1–2.3.1** (screening and special populations, 14), **3.1.1–3.3.7** (nutritional supplementation, treatment goals, supportive care, 16) and **4.1–4.5** (primary care perspective, 5). Each is followed by explanatory text. None is graded.
- **Numbering collides with Part 1:** Part 1 also has statements numbered 3.1.1–4.2.6 with different content. Cite as "Part 2 statement 3.1.1".
- **Key positions:**
  - **Who to screen:** celiac disease (CD) in defined high-risk symptoms/conditions and in first-degree relatives (2.1.1, Table 1); bile acid malabsorption after terminal ileal resection/right hemicolectomy, cholecystectomy, and in irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) or functional diarrhea — empiric bile acid sequestrant if testing unavailable (2.1.2); exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI) in patients with gastrointestinal symptoms plus diabetes, metabolic syndrome, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, primary pancreatic disorders or alcohol abuse (2.1.3); sprue-like enteropathy after olmesartan and other drugs (2.1.4).
  - **Special populations:** children (no-biopsy CD diagnosis at tissue transglutaminase immunoglobulin A (IgA) >10 times the upper limit of normal; carbohydrate intolerance; EPI; intestinal failure; inflammatory bowel disease; liver disease), the elderly (vitamin B12 and folate deficiency), and pregnancy after bariatric surgery (micronutrient screening; conceive ≥1 year after surgery).
  - **Diagnostic algorithm:** history, examination, screening laboratory tests and abdominal ultrasound, then stepwise tests by availability, disease frequency, invasiveness and cost (2.3.1, Figure 1A adults / 1B children).
  - **Nutrition ladder:** dietetic advice → oral nutritional supplements → enteral nutrition → parenteral nutrition only for intestinal failure (3.1.2); formula choice polymeric → oligomeric → elemental (3.1.6); tube and route choice (3.1.7); micronutrient monitoring and replacement, including vitamin B12 dosing (3.1.3–3.1.5).
  - **Supportive care:** antimotility drugs (loperamide preferred), acid suppression in high-output short bowel syndrome (SBS), glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) analogues (teduglutide) after intestinal adaptation, restoration of continuity and lengthening surgery; no role shown for probiotics, prebiotics or routine oral rehydration outside SBS (3.3.1–3.3.7).
  - **Primary care:** symptoms that should raise suspicion, point-of-care nutritional assessment, and a first-assessment algorithm with referral within 1 month (4.1–4.5, Figure 2).

### 2 — Screening and special populations

**2.1 Which high-risk groups should be screened for malabsorption?**

| # | Statement | Page |
|---|---|---|
| 2.1.1 | Patients with high-risk symptoms and conditions — **iron deficiency anemia, megaloblastic anemia, chronic diarrhea, type 1 diabetes mellitus, dermatitis herpetiformis, cryptogenic hypertransaminasemia, short stature or growth retardation in children, premature or male osteoporosis, or unexplained infertility** — **should be screened for CD or other malabsorptive disorders**. **First-degree relatives** of CD patients **should be screened** (high risk of asymptomatic CD) | 2 |
| 2.1.2 | **Symptomatic** patients with **terminal ileum resection/right hemicolectomy, cholecystectomy, IBS and functional diarrhea** should be **tested for bile acid malabsorption**. **If testing is not available, a trial with bile acid sequestrants should be considered** | 3 |
| 2.1.3 | Patients with **gastrointestinal symptoms and** type 1 or 2 diabetes mellitus, metabolic syndrome, HIV infection, primary pancreatic disorders, or alcohol abuse **should be screened for EPI** | 3 |
| 2.1.4 | Malabsorption symptoms after drug exposure — especially **olmesartan or other angiotensin-II receptor blockers (ARBs), angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, mycophenolate, and immune checkpoint inhibitors** — **should be investigated for sprue-like enteropathy**. Gastrointestinal symptoms after **pelvic radiotherapy** may reflect malabsorption | 3 |

- **CD yield (text to 2.1.1, p. 2):** CD prevalence 2.7%–19.5% in anemic patients at endoscopy and 8.5% with diarrhea; about 10%–15% of obscure iron deficiency anemia (IDA); a meta-analysis found about 1 in 31 IDA patients with histologic CD. Low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) raises CD likelihood in IDA. Relatives: up to 23% of asymptomatic siblings have biopsy-proven CD; pooled prevalence siblings 8.9%, offspring 7.9%, parents 3.0%. About 3.2% in unexplained infertility; 4% in adults with cryptic hypertransaminasemia or autoimmune hepatitis (higher in children).
- **Autoimmune gastritis (text to 2.1.1, p. 2):** pernicious anemia may be its first manifestation (adults/elderly); IDA common, especially in younger patients. **Screen for autoimmune gastritis:** pernicious anemia, or IDA with other autoimmune disorders, a first-degree family history of autoimmune gastritis, infertility/miscarriage, dyspepsia, or neurological alterations.
- **Bile acid malabsorption (text to 2.1.2, p. 3):** associated with Crohn's disease, CD, right hemicolectomy, cholecystectomy, microscopic colitis, post-radiotherapy fibrosis. 38% prevalence reported in diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D) and functional diarrhea. In patients having a 23-seleno-25-homotaurocholic acid (SeHCAT) scan for chronic diarrhea, odds ratio for bile acid malabsorption 2.5 after cholecystectomy, 12.4 after Crohn's ileal resection/hemicolectomy, 7.9 after ileal resection/hemicolectomy for other reasons; 27.3% of 77 IBS-D patients positive; meta-analysis pooled rate 28.1% (22.6%–34%) in IBS-D. **Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) can co-occur after right hemicolectomy/terminal ileal resection → consider it.** No SeHCAT cut-off given.
- **Other causes of EPI to consider (text to 2.1.3, p. 3):** chronic smoking; pancreatic resection, radiotherapy or chemotherapy; pancreatic adenocarcinoma; chronic pancreatitis; intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasms (IPMN); acute necrotic-hemorrhagic or autoimmune pancreatitis; bilio-digestive derivative surgery; steatorrhea after pancreaticoduodenectomy.
- **Drug-induced enteropathy (text to 2.1.4, p. 4):** in a 465,443-patient study, 23 cases of intestinal malabsorption, highest incidence with ARBs; a recent systematic review attributes most cases to **olmesartan**, other ARBs exceptional. Villous atrophy emerging with immune checkpoint inhibitors.
- **Radiation (p. 4):** small bowel irradiation → diarrhea or malabsorption in **up to 30%**; after pelvic radiotherapy, malabsorption via functional short bowel, bacterial overgrowth, vitamin B12 deficiency, pancreatic insufficiency, fatty acid or bile salt malabsorption.

**2.2 Are there any specific issues for special populations (i.e., childhood, pregnancy, elderly)?**

- Treatment goal in children (p. 4): treat the underlying cause (e.g., CD, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), chronic liver disease), promote maximal adaptation of the remaining gut (e.g., intestinal failure (IF)), and substitute and monitor energy, macro- and micronutrient deficiencies.

| # | Statement | Page |
|---|---|---|
| 2.2.1 | **Children and adolescents with clinical signs of malabsorption:** initial CD testing = **anti-tissue transglutaminase-IgA (tTG-IgA) and total serum IgA**, "is recommended". **If tTG-IgA >10 times the upper limit of normal (ULN), duodenal biopsies can be omitted** | 4 |
| 2.2.2 | Genetic and non-genetic carbohydrate malabsorption can be quite common in childhood and adolescence; symptoms after ingestion depend on the dose and concurrent diseases such as IBS. **Dietary restriction of the specific carbohydrate(s) is recommended when intolerance to it is proven by validated symptom assessment** | 4 |
| 2.2.3 | **Children and adolescents with clinical signs of malabsorption (especially fat malabsorption):** assessing exocrine pancreatic function **with fecal elastase** "is recommended". **In EPI:** monitor fat-soluble vitamin levels and screen for micronutrient deficiency regularly; substitute if necessary ("recommended") | 5 |
| 2.2.4 | IF in childhood and adolescence is rare, with heterogeneous manifestations, and can include significant fluid, electrolyte and nutrient malabsorption **requiring intravenous supplementation**. A **multidisciplinary team approach is recommended** | 5 |
| 2.2.5 | **Children and adolescents with IBD (especially Crohn's disease):** screening and prevention of malnutrition and micronutrient deficiencies, prevention of osteoporosis, and promotion of optimal growth and development are **"strongly suggested"** | 6 |
| 2.2.6 | Malnutrition is common in **cholestasis and cirrhosis in childhood and adolescence**. "We recommend" **avoiding prolonged fasting**, and using dietary supplements such as **medium chain triglyceride (MCT) oil** or more intensive support with enteral or parenteral nutrition, plus supplementation of micronutrient deficiencies, **especially fat-soluble vitamins** | 7 |
| 2.2.7 | **Elderly** individuals are more likely to develop diseases, or use medications, causing malabsorption — among others **EPI, vascular disease and diabetes mellitus** | 7 |
| 2.2.8 | **Vitamin B12 deficiency is common in the elderly**, mainly from autoimmune atrophic gastritis and **food-cobalamin malabsorption syndrome**. **Folate deficiency** is also common (malabsorption plus low intake). **Folate and B12 deficiency are a reversible cause of neuropsychiatric symptoms and hematological alterations** in the elderly | 7 |
| 2.2.9 | **Women with a history of bariatric surgery** should be **screened for nutritional deficiencies before, during and after pregnancy** and should **plan a pregnancy at least 1 year after bariatric surgery**. **Women with infertility and obstetric complications of apparently unknown cause** should be screened for **CD, vitamin B12 deficiency and autoimmune atrophic gastritis** | 7–8 |

#### Children: CD (text to 2.2.1, p. 4)
- tTG-IgA + total IgA is "sufficient and the most accurate" initial test.
- **No-biopsy diagnosis (text):** tTG-IgA **>10 times ULN in two lab samples** **along with positive endomysial antibody-IgA (EMA-IgA)**. HLA testing and symptoms are no longer mandatory for it.
- **Elevated tTG-IgA but <10 times ULN → duodenal biopsies** (to avoid a false-positive diagnosis).
- **Type 1 diabetes:** tTG-IgA may be low or fluctuating without biopsy abnormalities → **complement serology with histology**.
- Biopsy changes: intraepithelial lymphocytosis, crypt hyperplasia, variable villous height reduction, fully restored on a gluten-free diet. Increased CD prevalence in children with IBS-D-like symptoms, IDA, chronic constipation and enamel defects.

#### Children: carbohydrate malabsorption (text to 2.2.2, pp. 4–5)
- Maldigestion = enzyme deficiency (lactase, sucrase-isomaltase); malabsorption = overloaded brush-border transporter. Symptoms: abdominal pain, nausea, bloating, flatulence, increased motility, diarrhea; extraintestinal symptoms (headache, vertigo, memory impairment, lethargy) in <20%.

| Condition | Key points as printed |
|---|---|
| Congenital lactase deficiency | Rare, autosomal recessive; symptoms from first lactose feed (breast milk or formula); diagnosis by **genetic testing** |
| Adult-type lactase deficiency | Most frequent cause of carbohydrate malabsorption; developmentally regulated decline, varies by ethnic group |
| Secondary lactase deficiency | Transient (gastrointestinal infection, CD, Crohn's disease, SIBO); **lactose restriction only for a limited time and only if symptoms are attributed to lactose intolerance by validated symptom assessment** |
| Lactose malabsorption vs intolerance | Malabsorption diagnosed by **hydrogen breath testing**; intolerance by **validated symptom testing**; same tests available for adults |
| Congenital sucrase-isomaltase deficiency (CSID) | Rare, autosomal recessive; symptoms after weaning (sucrose, starch); genetic testing; dietary restriction + enzyme replacement with **sacrosidase (Sucraid)** oral solution |
| Glucose-galactose malabsorption | Very rare, autosomal recessive (sodium/glucose co-transporter defect); diarrhea soon after birth; genetic testing; low glucose-galactose diet and a fructose formula early in life |
| Fructose and sorbitol intolerance (non-genetic) | Dietary history + validated symptom questionnaire before and after ingestion; reduce intake, balance fructose with glucose, or low-sorbitol diet; **D-xylose isomerase** food supplement available in some countries for fructose intolerance |

- **Children on a lactose-reduced diet** risk lower calcium intake → **calcium supplementation and calcium-fortified foods recommended**.

#### Children: EPI (text to 2.2.3, p. 5)
- Mechanisms: insufficient pancreatic stimulation (e.g., CD, reduced cholecystokinin release), reduced pancreatic juice synthesis (cystic fibrosis, chronic pancreatitis), duct obstruction (cystic fibrosis, ductal anatomical defects).
- **Direct test:** pancreatic enzymes in duodenal secretions after secretin stimulation — high sensitivity and specificity, measures bicarbonate; invasive, specialist centers only.
- **Indirect tests:** fecal elastase (simple, non-invasive; **not useful for monitoring pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT)**; **false positive in watery stools or intestinal inflammation**); fat absorption coefficient; ¹³C-mixed triglyceride breath test (can monitor response to PERT; drawbacks: 6-h test, cost, very limited availability).
- **Treatment:** individualized dietary counselling + PERT. Proton pump inhibitor (PPI) co-therapy may improve bile acid absorption and reduce acid inactivation of pancreatic enzymes. Fat-soluble vitamins when necessary.

#### Children: IF (text to 2.2.4, pp. 5–6)
- **Definition:** reduction of functional gut mass below the minimum needed for digestion and absorption adequate for nutrient and fluid requirements for growth.
- Goal: adequate nutrition, fewer complications, **independence from parenteral nutrition (PN)**. **Start enteral nutrition early after bowel surgery** with mother's own milk or amino-acid-based formula; micronutrient supplementation critical — resection site determines which deficiencies.
- **Better adaptation with:** longer residual small bowel, younger age at surgery, preserved ileocecal valve, necrotizing enterocolitis as the diagnosis, no severe liver disease, normal gastrointestinal motility.

#### Children: IBD (text to 2.2.5, p. 6)
- Malnutrition common at Crohn's diagnosis and may persist; growth failure from inflammation, malnutrition and prolonged corticosteroids.
- **Check regularly and supplement:** iron stores, zinc, vitamin D. **Vitamin B12:** monitor and replace with significant terminal ileal disease or terminal ileal resection. **Folic acid** with methotrexate or sulfasalazine. **Calcium and vitamin D** with steroids.
- **Exclusive enteral nutrition = recommended first-line induction** in children and adolescents with acute Crohn's disease.
- Crohn's in remission: oral nutritional supplements (ONS) or enteral nutrition if undernutrition not corrected by dietary counselling. Severe malnutrition → watch for **refeeding syndrome** (monitor and treat electrolytes). Dietitian counselling for all IBD patients in remission.

#### Children: chronic liver disease (text to 2.2.6, p. 7)
- Cholestasis → deficiencies of protein, essential fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins; protein requirements increased.
- Essential fatty acid deficiency can be iatrogenic with **high-MCT, low long-chain-triglyceride diets**.
- Unabsorbed free fatty acids bind calcium → metabolic bone disease and oxalate nephrolithiasis.
- Optimized pre-transplant nutrition may hasten post-transplant recovery. Support options: MCT oil, nasogastric feeds, total PN.

#### Elderly (text to 2.2.7–2.2.8, p. 7)
- **Overt malabsorption is not part of normal ageing.** Increased intestinal permeability is common even without symptoms.
- Higher-risk causes in the elderly: EPI, enteropathies (especially drug-induced), small bowel ischemia, diabetes, lactose malabsorption, infections such as Whipple's disease; more drug and surgical exposure.
- **Most common causes:** lactose malabsorption, EPI and CD — EPI and CD together up to 60% of cases (up to 30% each). Symptoms subtle (anorexia, nausea, diarrhea, abdominal pain, bloating, flatus) → diagnostic delay.
- **Vitamin B12:** deficiency 5%–20% in the elderly (depending on cut-off), rising with age. Causes: **food-bound cobalamin malabsorption (>60% of cases; prevalence 10%–30% over age 50)** and autoimmune atrophic gastritis; aggravated by SIBO and chronic PPI-induced hypochlorhydria.
  - **Parenteral B12 only for severe anemia or neurological alterations; oral crystalline B12 may suffice in less severe cases.**
  - Screen for B12 in the elderly — some neurological abnormalities reverse with supplementation, though dementia progression is not changed.
- **Folate:** low absorption (possibly hypochlorhydria) + low intake → mild cognitive impairment, dementia, depression, macrocytic anemia; whether supplementation slows cognitive decline is debated.

#### Pregnancy (text to 2.2.9, p. 8)
- **After bariatric surgery** — screen **before conception or at the first antenatal visit** for: blood cell count, ferritin, iron, vitamin B12, folate, thiamine, vitamin A, calcium, vitamin D. **Repeat at least once per trimester**, and **post-partum if breastfeeding**.
- Purely restrictive surgery (gastric banding): no increase in small-for-gestational-age infants.
- **Early pregnancy loss → screen for vitamin B12 deficiency** (low B12 and hyperhomocysteinemia are each risk factors; both together odds ratio (OR) 4.98, P = 0.002). Autoimmune atrophic gastritis reported as a cause in pregnant women with adverse outcomes.
- **CD:** infertility evidence inconclusive and variable; adverse pregnancy outcomes (miscarriage, intrauterine growth retardation, low birth weight, small for gestational age, stillbirth, preterm birth) up to 4 times more frequent than in controls; early diagnosis and gluten-free diet reverse the effects; more studies needed before specific recommendations.

**2.3 What is a reasonable algorithm which combines screening tests, laboratory and endoscopic evaluations and radiological tests?**

| # | Statement | Page |
|---|---|---|
| 2.3.1 | Initial assessment of every patient with suspected malabsorption syndrome: **detailed history, physical examination, screening laboratory tests, and abdominal ultrasound in selected patients**. In **clinically- and laboratory-proven** malabsorption syndrome, a **stepwise approach is recommended** to find the cause, tests grouped by availability, frequency of disease in the population, invasiveness and cost (Figure 1A adults, 1B children) | 8 |

- Only 3 published malabsorption algorithms found, all within systematic reviews (one for chronic diarrhea only). With the first step, an expert can reportedly diagnose **70%–80%** of malabsorption syndromes (p. 8).
- Figure 1A gives illustrative examples; the process may differ case by case (p. 8).

#### Figure 1A — Schematic algorithm for the diagnosis of malabsorption in adults (p. 9)

**Initial assessment in adults (any physician):**
1. **History:** family history of CD, autoimmune gastritis, other autoimmunity; predisposing conditions (e.g., gastrointestinal surgery, chronic pancreatitis, alcohol abuse, immunodeficiency); drug intake; travel history; dietary habits.
2. **Physical examination:** malnutrition, sarcopenia, edema, paleness, glossitis, hair loss, skin changes.
3. **Laboratory tests:** complete blood count, electrolytes, liver enzymes, albumin, total proteins, urea, creatinine, iron, ferritin, transferrin, folic acid, vitamin B12, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), international normalized ratio (INR), cholesterol, C-reactive protein, tTG-IgA, total IgA, fecal calprotectin.
4. **Imaging:** abdominal ultrasound.

**Then by clinical example (first-line → second-line tests, the latter recommended by specialists):**

|---|---|---|
| **Weight loss, osteoporosis, diarrhea and/or steatorrhea, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency** | Stool: microscopy and culture; fecal calprotectin; fecal occult blood test (FOBT); fecal elastase and chymotrypsin · Lab: EMA-IgA if tTG-IgA positive; 25-hydroxyvitamin D · Imaging: abdominal computed tomography (CT); magnetic resonance (MR); CT or MR enterography · Endoscopy: esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) (+ duodenal biopsies with specific stainings when needed), colonoscopy + ileoscopy and biopsies · Special: SeHCAT test for bile acid malabsorption | Lab: immunoglobulins, HIV, antinuclear antibodies (ANA), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), cortisol, chromogranin A, gastrin, urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), anti-parietal cell antibodies, anti-enterocyte antibodies, total IgG4 · Imaging: positron emission tomography (PET); CT/MR angiography; Tc-99m human serum albumin scintigraphy · Endoscopy: video capsule endoscopy; device-assisted enteroscopy; endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP); endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) · Special: HLA DQ2/DQ8 (selected cases); stool pH; fecal alpha-1 antitrypsin |
| **Bloating and flatulence, with/without diarrhea** | Stool: microscopy and culture; fecal elastase; FOBT · Lab: EMA-IgA if tTG-IgA positive · Imaging: abdominal CT; MR; CT or MR enterography · Endoscopy: EGD (+ duodenal biopsies), colonoscopy + ileoscopy and biopsies · Special: H₂ breath test if FODMAPs involved | Lab: food allergen-specific IgE; anti-parietal cell antibodies; gastrin; chromogranin A |
| **Anemia of unknown etiology, with/without other signs of malabsorption** | Stool: microscopy and culture; fecal calprotectin; FOBT · Lab: EMA-IgA if tTG-IgA positive; gastrin, chromogranin A, anti-parietal cell antibodies · Imaging: abdominal CT, MR, CT or MR enterography · Endoscopy: EGD (+ duodenal and gastric biopsies), colonoscopy + ileoscopy and biopsies | Endoscopy: video capsule endoscopy, device-assisted enteroscopy · Special: HLA DQ2/DQ8 (selected cases) |

- FODMAPs is not expanded in the figure.

#### Figure 1B — Schematic algorithm for children and adolescents (p. 9)

1. **Child/adolescent with suspicion of malabsorption → initial work-up (can be performed by a general pediatrician):**

   - Physical examination including anthropometry and growth curves.

Stool: fecal calprotectin, fecal elastase.
   - Imaging: abdominal ultrasound.
   - Dietary assessment (3-day food protocol; diagnostic lactose/fructose elimination).
   - Psychosocial evaluation.
2. **→ Advanced diagnostic testing (ordered by a pediatric gastroenterologist):**
   - Blood: zinc, immunoglobulins, EMA-IgA, ammonia, cortisol, D-lactic acidosis, essential fatty acids, HIV, interferon release assay, alpha-1 antitrypsin, ANA, autoantibodies for liver disease, hepatitis serology, anti-parietal cell antibodies, gastrin, chromogranin A.
   - Stool: fecal alpha-1 antitrypsin, fat malabsorption coefficient in 72-h stool collection, FOBT, stool cultures including pathogenic bacteria and parasites, stool reducing substances. Urine analysis; sweat chloride test; tuberculin skin test.
   - Endoscopy: EGD + biopsies, ileocolonoscopy + biopsies, video capsule endoscopy.
   - Imaging: abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), MR enterography, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP).

   - Genetic testing: congenital diarrhea panel (e.g., congenital lactase deficiency, CSID, Na⁺/glucose co-transporter defect, microvillus inclusion disease, tufting enteropathy); cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR); Shwachman-Bodian-Diamond syndrome; Johanson-Blizzard syndrome; hereditary pancreatitis; others.

- **Figure 1 legend (p. 9):** expert-based; order varies with clinical situation and local test availability. If one cause is highly likely, test for it first (e.g., overt malabsorption with a first-degree family history of CD → test for CD first, other tests second-line if CD not confirmed; single B12 deficiency with pernicious anemia → autoimmune gastritis most likely). Initial assessment by any physician; second-level testing recommended by specialists. No universal algorithm; follow disease-specific guidelines.

### 3 — Nutritional supplementation, treatment goals, supportive care

**3.1 When is nutritional supplementation needed and which kind?**

| # | Statement | Page |
|---|---|---|
| 3.1.1 | Many patients with malabsorption have enough absorptive function for **oral or enteral** nutritional support. Some develop **IF** = reduction of intestinal function below the minimum needed to maintain body function, composition and homeostasis, **requiring intravenous supplementation** to maintain health and/or growth | 8 |
| 3.1.2 | Malabsorption with malnutrition, or at risk of it → **dietetic advice**. Unable to meet requirements with conventional foods → **medical nutrition therapy**. **ONS are the first step**; **enteral nutrition** if oral feeding is insufficient; **PN reserved for IF** | 10 |
| 3.1.3 | Micronutrient deficiencies depend on the mechanism and the gut segment(s) affected. **Screen for them and correct any deficiency.** Supplement **orally or enterally** if safe and effective; otherwise other routes (e.g., intramuscular, sublingual, parenteral) | 10 |
| 3.1.4 | **Monitor fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) regularly and supplement if deficient** in disorders with fat malabsorption: cystic fibrosis and other causes of EPI, cholestatic liver disease, SBS, congenital intestinal lymphangiectasia, abetalipoproteinemia, malabsorption due to bariatric surgery | 10 |

#### IF and intestinal insufficiency (text to 3.1.1, pp. 8, 10)
- **Intestinal insufficiency:** persistently reduced intestinal function still manageable with diet modification and oral or enteral supplementation.
- **IF requires both, simultaneously:** decreased absorption of macronutrients and/or water and electrolytes from loss of gut function, **and** the need for intravenous supplementation.
- Extensive small bowel mucosal disease is one of five pathophysiological conditions that can cause IF (the other four are not listed).
- **Severity** rises with the content and volume of intravenous support: fluids and electrolytes alone = less severe than PN with macronutrients; severity increases with weekly PN volume. The severity categories are not printed.

#### Malnutrition and the nutrition ladder (text to 3.1.2, p. 10)
- Identify risk with **any validated screening tool** (most assess involuntary weight loss, low body mass index (BMI), reduced intake, disease burden).
- **Malnutrition diagnosis = at least one phenotypic criterion** (weight loss, low BMI or reduced muscle mass) **plus at least one etiologic criterion** (reduced food intake/assimilation, or acute or chronic inflammation). The criteria set is not named and its cut-offs are not printed.
- Diet: food modification and fortification; malabsorption often needs **high-protein, high-calorie, low-to-modified fat** diet to limit steatorrhea.
- ONS need safe swallowing and a functional gut; limited by severe anorexia, swallowing problems, obstruction or dysmotility. Tube feeding needs an accessible gut, adequate motility and sufficient absorptive capacity.

#### Formulae (text to 3.1.6, pp. 11–12)
- **IBD:** ONS first step alongside a normal diet; ONS or enteral nutrition in Crohn's disease in remission if malnutrition is not corrected by counselling. **Exclusive enteral nutrition (usually oral) recommended first-line to induce remission in children and adolescents with mild active Crohn's disease, and as adjunctive therapy in adults.** Polymeric formulae with moderate fat preferred (palatability); no evidence an oligomeric diet beats a standard formula.
- **Chronic pancreatitis or EPI:** semi-elemental formulae containing MCT may be needed in addition to enzyme replacement.

#### Enteral route (text to 3.1.7, p. 12)
- **Short-term (4–6 weeks): nasogastric tube preferred.** **Long-term: feeding ostomy (endoscopic or radiologically inserted gastrostomy).**
- Gastric placement allows bolus feeding (more physiological); continuous pump infusion when needed.
- **Jejunal (post-pyloric) route:** gastroduodenal dysmotility (mainly gastroparesis) or high aspiration risk (gastroesophageal reflux). **Chronic pancreatitis needing enteral nutrition with pain, delayed gastric emptying, persistent nausea and vomiting, or gastric outlet obstruction → jejunal.** **After gastrectomy, jejunal is the only option.**

#### Parenteral support (text to 3.1.8, pp. 12)
- IF severity categorized by type and volume of intravenous supplementation (fluids alone or PN formula); tailor composition to avoid metabolic complications.

**3.2 What are the treatment goals and how shall treatment be monitored?**

| # | Statement | Page |
|---|---|---|
| 3.2.1 | **Monitor nutritional status regularly** — a treatment goal is to maintain or improve it. **Children: normal growth and development** is a goal. Monitoring = **weight, height, BMI, muscle mass and muscle function**, and laboratory tests when applicable | 12 |

- Adult goals: prevent weight loss, improve BMI and/or increase muscle mass (p. 12).
- **Sarcopenia** = loss of muscle mass with loss of muscle strength; common in IBD (low fat-free mass in 28% of Crohn's patients across 19 studies, 926 IBD patients). Assess muscle mass by bioelectrical impedance, dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), CT, muscle ultrasound, or calf / mid-upper-arm circumference with validated ethnic- and sex-specific cut-offs where available; cut-offs not printed (p. 12).

#### Antimotility drugs (text to 3.3.1, p. 13)
- **Loperamide preferred over opiates** (codeine, opium, diphenoxylate) — no central nervous system (mainly sedative) effects; slows small-intestinal transit.
- **Dose: loperamide 4 mg three to four times a day "in general" recommended.** Small placebo-controlled trials (mainly ileostomy or ileocecal resection with diarrhea): fecal wet weight reduced by **15%–30%**.
- Loperamide and codeine studied in small trials as an adjunct to bile acid sequestrants in bile acid diarrhea. **Octreotide proposed for severe, refractory diarrhea** (anti-motility and anti-secretory).

#### Probiotics, prebiotics, butyrate (text to 3.3.2–3.3.3, p. 13)
- Two small adult studies (*Lactobacillus rhamnosus* GG, *Bifidobacterium longum*) as adjuncts in mild-to-moderate Crohn's: no difference from placebo in remission at 6 months; two meta-analyses: probiotics not an option for maintenance.
- Oligofructose-enriched inulin 15–20 g/day: no benefit for remission in adult Crohn's (2 randomized trials). 10 g in children with CD on a gluten-free diet: no effect on barrier integrity at 12 weeks.
- Butyrate: small studies of enteric-coated butyrate in Crohn's; microencapsulated sodium butyrate available in some European countries; clinical impact unproven.

#### Oral rehydration (text to 3.3.4, p. 13)
- Sodium-glucose cotransport is preserved in SBS → glucose plus sodium drives water absorption.
- **Acute IF due to SBS, especially without colon: oral rehydration fluid as the main oral hydration source.** Sip fluids; **separate liquids from solids**.
- **IBD with high-output jejunostomy:** decrease hypotonic fluid and increase saline solution.
- No composition (sodium concentration) is printed.

#### Acid suppression (text to 3.3.5, p. 14)
- After resection: hypergastrinemia and gastric hypersecretion add to losses; acid may denature pancreatic enzymes and impair bile salts.
- **Use especially in the first 6 months after surgery and in SBS with fecal output >2 L/day.** Two studies: fecal wet weight and sodium excretion reduced by **20%–25%**.
- **Long-term use case by case** — weigh vitamin B12 deficiency, hypomagnesemia, hypokalemia, impaired calcium absorption.

#### GLP-2 analogues (text to 3.3.6, p. 14)
- **Who:** IF due to SBS on PN/parenteral fluids, **stable after postoperative intestinal adaptation — usually 12–24 months after the last intestinal resection**.
- Teduglutide = recombinant GLP-2 receptor agonist resistant to dipeptidyl-peptidase IV; the only recombinant GLP-2 analogue approved in the United States and Europe for SBS with chronic IF. No dose is printed.
- Evidence: three randomized controlled trials, one controlled trial, cohort studies; reduces PN dependence in adults after adaptation. **Children:** systematic review of 14 studies — appears safe and effective; long-term data needed.
- **Efficacy = stable reduction in intravenous volume of 20% from baseline.**
- **Before starting (all patients):** **colonoscopy (if remnant colon and/or rectum), abdominal ultrasound and gastroscopy** — detect polyps, exclude neoplasia. **Surveillance for intestinal neoplasia is mandatory on treatment**; no interval is printed.
- **Inform the patient about:** chance of reducing or stopping home PN, quality-of-life gain, expected duration, effects after stopping, adverse effects and risks, cost-effectiveness, need for regular monitoring. **Highlight the risk of new gastrointestinal, hepatobiliary and pancreatic benign or malignant neoplasms** and follow-up screening.
- Adverse events very common, mostly mild-moderate: abdominal pain, nausea, abdominal distension, mainly early.
- **On treatment:** body weight and composition, electrolytes and renal function should stay stable as intravenous support falls; monitoring by an expert multidisciplinary team using standardized protocols. **Likely lifelong; stopping → return to baseline intravenous requirements.**

#### Surgery (text to 3.3.7, pp. 14–15)
- Restore continuity (ostomy reversal, recruit distal bowel) once stable, to reduce home PN — including patients with diversion colitis; may improve PN-induced chronic cholestasis. Only by experienced surgeons in specialized interdisciplinary units.
- **Lengthening/tapering in selected patients:** longitudinal intestinal lengthening and tailoring (LILT) or serial transverse enteroplasty (STEP) — little difference in enteral autonomy, cholestasis improvement or complications; choice follows surgical team experience. Segmental dilatation with poor peristalsis is common in pediatric SBS.
- **Crohn's strictures: prefer strictureplasty over small bowel resection when feasible** to avoid short bowel.
- Small bowel or multivisceral transplantation: few very severe, refractory cases; limited by long-term immunosuppression, technical difficulty and complications.

- Non-gastrointestinal clues (text to 4.1, p. 15): unexplained abnormal liver function tests, anemia of obscure origin, skin conditions, unexplained infertility, osteoporosis.

- **If first tests are negative (text to 4.4, p. 16):** consider malnutrition and **drug-related malabsorption such as PPIs or metformin**; no improvement → refer to secondary/tertiary care.
- Primary care can also provide special nutrients, psychological support, and post-surgical monitoring (p. 16).

#### Figure 2 — Algorithm for primary care professionals (p. 16)

1. **Early recognition** of signs, symptoms or clinical manifestations of malabsorption: chronic diarrhea; abdominal pain; bloating, abdominal distension; flatulence; hair loss; weight loss and low weight; peripheral edema; failure to thrive and/or short stature; unexplained anemia; single nutrient deficiencies; osteoporosis of obscure etiology; infertility, recurrent miscarriage; delayed menarche or amenorrhea; skin changes; neuropsychiatric alterations; at-risk medical conditions for malabsorption; drugs responsible for malabsorption; alcohol abuse; cryptic hypertransaminasemia.
2. **→ First-line tests:** point-of-care tests (if available); fecal elastase or fecal fat content; anti-tissue transglutaminase antibodies + total IgA, TSH; complete blood count, fecal calprotectin, fecal occult blood test; imaging or other laboratory tests depending on the clinical picture.
3. **Any alteration → refer to the gastroenterologist or specialist physician.**
4. **All normal → treat symptoms + nutritional supplementation → no improvement within 1 month, or persistence of the clinical picture → refer.**

- **Figure 2 legend (p. 16):** expert-based; tests vary with clinical situation and local availability. Iron, vitamin B12, albumin and folic acid may also help. **Celiac serology should always be performed.** Fecal calprotectin helps separate inflammatory colonic disorders. **Refer within 1 month (or earlier if needed)** depending on the picture and first-line results, to avoid treatment delay.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

### Inconsistencies within the document
- **Pediatric no-biopsy rule (2.2.1, p. 4):** the statement allows omitting biopsies at tTG-IgA >10 times ULN alone; the text requires >10 times ULN **in two lab samples plus positive EMA-IgA**.
- **Exclusive enteral nutrition population:** "acute Crohn's disease" in children (2.2.5 text, p. 6) vs "mild active Crohn's disease" (3.1.6 text, p. 11).
- **Fecal chymotrypsin:** Figure 1A lists "faecal elastase and chymotrypsin" as first-line, while Part 1 says chymotrypsin has been replaced by elastase.
- **Octreotide** is listed among "antimotility drugs" (3.3.1) but its text describes anti-motility and anti-secretory action, for severe refractory diarrhea only.
- **Elderly causes (p. 7):** three common causes are named (lactose malabsorption, EPI, CD), then "these two latter" are said to make up to 60% together (up to 30% each).
- **2.1.1 vs Table 1:** the statement lists megaloblastic anemia and chronic diarrhea; Table 1 has "unexplained iron deficiency anaemia or other nutrient-deficient anaemia" and no chronic diarrhea entry (it lists IBS and functional diarrhea).
- **IF severity** is said to be graded by type and volume of intravenous support (pp. 10, 12) but no categories are printed; "five pathophysiological conditions" causing IF are referred to but not listed.
- **Malnutrition criteria** (phenotypic + etiologic) are used without naming the criteria set or giving cut-offs.
- **Statement numbering collides with Part 1** (both have 3.1.1 etc.).

### Differences from US sources on file
- **Pediatric CD no-biopsy diagnosis** — [[acg-2022-celiac|ACG 2022]] (with European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) 2020) on [[celiac-disease]]: tTG-IgA >10× ULN **and** positive EMA in a second blood sample, both required, and only if the family agrees. Statement 2.2.1 omits EMA and the second sample (its text includes them). **ACG wins; the statement alone is not sufficient.**
- **Loperamide dose** — this source: 4 mg three to four times a day "in general". [[aga-2022-short-bowel-syndrome|AGA 2022]]: high doses often needed in SBS, up to 16 tablets (32 mg)/day, about 30 minutes before meals and at bedtime. AGA wins for SBS.
- **Octreotide** — this source: proposed for severe, refractory diarrhea. [[aga-2022-short-bowel-syndrome|AGA 2022]]: not shown to improve absorption or reduce PN need, may worsen malabsorption, **avoid during intestinal adaptation**, reserve for large-volume losses with problematic fluid management. Compatible only for refractory high output; AGA's adaptation caution stands.
- **Acid suppression in SBS** — this source: especially first 6 months and output >2 L/day; long term case by case. AGA 2022: hypersecretion lasts 6–12 months; use sparingly beyond 12 months, particularly with documented SIBO. Compatible; different time frame.
- **Vitamin B12 parenteral dose** — this source: intramuscular 1000–2000 mcg every 1–3 months. [[aga-2022-short-bowel-syndrome|AGA 2022]] (SBS micronutrient table): subcutaneous/intramuscular 300–1000 µg monthly. Different dose and interval; AGA wins in SBS.
- **Teduglutide pre-treatment work-up** — this source: colonoscopy (if colon/rectum remain), gastroscopy and abdominal ultrasound, no intervals. AGA 2022: colonoscopy; [[gattex-2025-teduglutide-label|teduglutide label]]: colonoscopy and upper endoscopy within 6 months before starting, repeat at 1 year, then at least every 5 years if no polyp. Abdominal ultrasound is only in this source.
- **Nasal tube duration** — this source: nasogastric for short-term (4–6 weeks). On [[enteral-access]]: [[aga-2025-endoscopic-enteral-access|AGA 2025]] prefers the nasal route for short-term (<4 weeks) enteral feeding; [[asge-2011-enteral-feeding|ASGE 2011]] suggests nasoenteric feeding when peroral nutrition is expected to resume within 30 days. US threshold stands.
- **EPI screening** — this source adds HIV, metabolic syndrome and alcohol abuse (with gastrointestinal symptoms) to the populations to screen; [[aga-2023-epi|AGA 2023]] BPA 1–2 list high- and moderate-risk conditions without these. Not contradictory; AGA framework stands.
- **Bile acid sequestrants in IBS** — this source: test symptomatic IBS/functional diarrhea for bile acid malabsorption, empiric sequestrant if no test. [[acg-2020-ibs|ACG 2020]] does not suggest sequestrants for **global IBS-D symptoms**; [[aga-2019-lab-evaluation-functional-diarrhea-ibs-d|AGA 2019]] suggests testing for bile acid diarrhea in chronic diarrhea. Compatible (different populations).
