## Summary

The Rome V chapter that **prints the gastroduodenal diagnostic criteria**, in five categories: **B1 functional dyspepsia (FD)** with its two overlapping subtypes postprandial distress syndrome (PDS) and epigastric pain syndrome (EPS); **B2 nausea and vomiting disorders** — chronic nausea vomiting syndrome (CNVS), cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS), cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS); **B3 excessive belching disorders** (gastric and supragastric); **B4 inability to belch syndrome**, new in Rome V; and **B5 rumination syndrome**.

The Rome Foundation Global Epidemiology Study found **10.6%** of the general population has symptoms consistent with a gastroduodenal disorder; FD symptoms alone are at **7.2%** (95% confidence interval 7.0–7.4).

The headline change in FD is that **the subgroup terminology PDS or EPS, or both, should preferentially be used** rather than the umbrella term, and that **postprandial epigastric pain in the presence of PDS symptoms is now characterized as PDS, not EPS** — resolving the Rome IV overlap problem. Three management algorithms are supplied: a diagnostic approach to dyspeptic symptoms (Figure 2) and separate treatment algorithms for PDS (Figure 3) and EPS (Figure 4), plus a diagnostic approach to chronic or recurrent nausea and vomiting (Figure 5).

## Key Findings / Claims

### B1. Diagnostic criteria for functional dyspepsia

> 1. One or more of the following:
>    a. Bothersome postprandial fullness
>    b. Bothersome early satiation
>    c. Bothersome epigastric pain
>    d. Bothersome epigastric burning
> 2. **No evidence of structural, systemic, or metabolic disease** that is likely to explain the symptoms
>
> Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset ≥6 months before diagnosis. **Must fulfill criteria for PDS or EPS, or both.**

- Patients with dyspeptic symptoms are classified as having **secondary dyspepsia** if a structural, systemic or metabolic disease can be identified, or as FD if diagnostic procedures identify no explanation.
- **The specifier categories PDS and EPS should be used, guided by the symptom profile** — this is the Rome V preference, with FD remaining only the umbrella term.

### B1a. Postprandial distress syndrome

> 1. Must include **one or both** of the following **at least 2 days per week**:
>    a. Bothersome postprandial fullness (severe enough to impact usual activities)
>    b. Bothersome early satiation (severe enough to prevent finishing a regular-sized meal)
> 2. In the presence of 1a or 1b, or both, **other bothersome symptoms** — postprandial epigastric pain or burning, postprandial nausea, postprandial upper abdominal bloating, or postprandial excessive belching — that are **elicited or worsened by food ingestion are part of PDS**
> 3. **Predominant nausea and/or persisting vomiting excludes the diagnosis of PDS** → consider CNVS or gastroparesis
> 4. Heartburn is not a gastroduodenal symptom but often coexists
> 5. No evidence of structural, systemic, or metabolic disease likely to explain the symptoms on routine investigations
>
> **"Postprandial" means symptoms triggered within 2 hours of meal intake.**

### B1b. Epigastric pain syndrome

> 1. One or both of the following symptoms **at least 1 day per week**:
>    a. Bothersome epigastric pain (severe enough to affect usual activities)
>    b. Bothersome epigastric burning (severe enough to affect usual activities)
> 2. Both 1a and 1b can be **induced or worsened postprandially, or occur independently from meal ingestion**
> 3. **PDS criteria are not fulfilled**
> 4. **Predominant nausea or persisting vomiting, or both, excludes the diagnosis** → consider CNVS or gastroparesis
> 5. Heartburn is not a gastroduodenal symptom but often coexists
> 6. No evidence of structural, systemic, or metabolic disease likely to explain the symptoms on routine investigations
>
> **Epigastric is limited to the central part of the abdomen above the navel.** When epigastric pain or burning is **consistently relieved by bowel movements, consider a bowel disorder.**

**Provisional subdivision of EPS** (research-grade, in the absence of PDS):

| Provisional category | Rule |
|---|---|
| Postprandial EPS | Epigastric pain or burning starts or gets worse after meals in **≥50%** of the times |
| Meal-unrelated EPS | Epigastric pain or burning starts or gets worse after meals in **<50%** of the times |

This subdivision requires further research; provisional criteria were developed because in 4 cohorts of >1300 FD patients, of whom 21% had EPS by Rome IV, **most reported epigastric pain unrelated to meals, but a substantial proportion reported pain triggered or worsened by meal ingestion**.

### Changes from Rome IV in FD

- The subgroup terms PDS or EPS, or both, **should preferentially be used**.
- **Postprandial epigastric pain in the presence of PDS symptoms is characterized as PDS, not EPS** — Rome IV allowed epigastric pain after meals to sit in EPS, creating considerable overlap.
- Supporting evidence: meal-related epigastric pain in PDS can respond to a **prokinetic**, and PDS patients with or without postprandial epigastric pain show similar benefit — so the same treatment approach can be used.
- Meal-related symptoms emerge or worsen **within the first 2 hours** after meal ingestion; later onset suggests a different pathophysiologic background.
- The PDS **2 days/week** threshold comes from patient focus-group interviews on bothersomeness.

### FD — clinical evaluation

- Management is guided by history. **In the absence of alarm features, discontinue unnecessary drugs and supplements** that could be responsible for dyspepsia **before** proceeding to further diagnostic testing.
- ***Helicobacter pylori* status should be determined in every patient with dyspeptic symptoms.** Whether to test and treat depends on local positivity rates and national guidelines.
- **Routine laboratory testing, including celiac serology, is not recommended** — low diagnostic yield.
- **Upper endoscopy is not compulsory** in patients without risk or alarm symptoms; yield is low with typical symptoms and no alarm or risk factors. It **is mandatory** when alarm features are present, or when dyspeptic symptoms are investigated for a clinical or mechanistic trial. For research purposes, the diagnosis strictly requires normal findings on upper endoscopy.
- **No international consensus recommends routine duodenal biopsies.**
- **Routine imaging is not supported** in the workup of symptoms compatible with FD; consider additional testing case by case in individuals with abnormal findings on clinical examination or when treatment has failed.
- **Motility studies are not recommended** in the routine diagnosis of FD.
- ***H. pylori*-associated dyspepsia:** eradication is effective in ~**30%** of individuals with FD symptoms, number needed to treat **15**. If symptom remission remains for **half a year or longer** after eradication, the dyspepsia is considered due to *H. pylori* infection and the condition is diagnosed as *H. pylori*-associated dyspepsia.

### FD — treatment

- Stepwise. Evidence for diet as therapy is low, but it is reasonable to advise **frequent small-size meals and avoiding high-fat food items**.
- **Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)** are generally the recommended first-line treatment for patients who remain symptomatic after *H. pylori* eradication or who are *H. pylori*-negative: a **standard once-daily dose for 4 to 8 weeks, discontinued if improvement is insufficient, without a dose-escalation strategy**. PPIs beat placebo for global FD symptoms, **number needed to treat 11**, but the evidence within FD subgroups is limited and **PPIs may be less effective for PDS symptoms**.
- **Histamine-2 receptor blockers** have a number needed to treat of 7, but study quality is low and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) was likely included; most guidelines suggest PPI rather than H2 blockers first-line, except the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidelines, which propose both.
- **Prokinetics** — number needed to treat **12** when cisapride is excluded. Tegaserod showed benefit in female patients. **Itopride** (combined dopamine-2 antagonist and cholinesterase inhibitor) significantly increased responders in PDS–EPS overlap. **Acotiamide** enhances gastric accommodation and emptying, is well tolerated and is **most effective for PDS**.
- **Neuromodulators** — number needed to treat **6**, with the benefit limited to **tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) and antipsychotics such as levosulpiride** (which also exerts prokinetic activity). Longest duration studied: **12 weeks**. Adverse events and withdrawals were significantly higher than with psychotropic drugs generally. **Two studies of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors in FD were negative.** Amitriptyline's effect on pain may be more useful in EPS.
- **Mirtazapine** improved early satiation scores and nutrient tolerance vs placebo, with weight recovery, better quality of life and a GI-specific anxiety score.
- **Tandospirone** (5-hydroxytryptamine-1A agonist) improved the abdominal symptom score, overall dyspeptic severity, postprandial fullness, early satiation and upper abdominal bloating; it increased gastric accommodation and delayed emptying of liquids. Meta-analysis: FD treated with **5-HT₄ or 5-HT₁A agonists improved significantly more than placebo**.
- **Herbal therapies** with trial support: STW 5 and STW 5-II, peppermint oil plus caraway oil, the Japanese KAMPO preparation **rikkunshito**, ZhiZhu KuangZhong (PDS), Biling Weitong granules (EPS pain severity).
- **Probiotics:** higher rates of improvement in PDS — but **not EPS** — with daily *Lactobacillus gasseri* OLL2716 (LG21) for 3 months, and with *Bacillus subtilis* plus *Bacillus coagulans*.
- **Psychological and behavioral therapy:** at least 4 cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) studies show positive short-term effects, with benefit sustained to 6 months in one study. Hypnotherapy was positive in one small randomized trial.
- **Acupuncture:** meta-analyses of numerous low-quality trials suggest manual and electric acupuncture are effective, but selection, performance, reporting and attrition bias are major concerns.

**Figure 3 — PDS treatment algorithm:** dietary and lifestyle advice → **PPI (short term, standard dose) or a first-line prokinetic or herbal medicine** → endoscopy/blood/other tests if not yet done → **neuromodulator and/or brain–gut behavior therapy** (early satiety → 5-HT₁A agonist; weight loss → mirtazapine; pain or postprandial fullness → TCA) → **gastric emptying test** (stop medications that affect gastric emptying before the test) → if delayed, **PDS with delayed gastric emptying** and second-line prokinetics (prucalopride, erythromycin), which are only studied in delayed emptying. **The term gastroparesis is reserved for predominant nausea or recurrent vomiting** and is not used in its absence.

**Figure 4 — EPS treatment algorithm:** dietary and lifestyle advice → **PPI (short term, standard dose) or herbal medicine** → endoscopy/blood/other tests if not yet done → **neuromodulator (TCA for pain and postprandial fullness) and/or brain–gut behavior therapy** → consider other diagnoses; nutritional support as needed. In both PDS and EPS, **nutritional support is recommended in patients with refractory symptoms inducing weight loss or feeding restrictions**.

### B2a. Diagnostic criteria for chronic nausea vomiting syndrome

> Must include **all** of the following:
> 1. **Bothersome** (severe enough to affect usual activities) **nausea occurring at least 2 days per week**, with or without **1 or more vomiting episodes per week**
> 2. Self-induced vomiting, eating disorders and regurgitation are excluded as a cause of vomiting
> 3. No evidence of structural, systemic, or metabolic disease likely to explain the symptoms on routine investigations
> 4. **Symptoms predominantly due to PDS exclude the diagnosis**
> 5. **Predominant nausea or persistent vomiting associated with delayed gastric emptying should prompt a diagnosis of gastroparesis**
>
> Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset ≥6 months before diagnosis.

- **Change from Rome IV:** frequency changed to **≥2 nausea days weekly, with or without ≥1 weekly vomiting episode**.
- Evaluation is dictated by the clinical presentation. **Bilious vomiting, abdominal tenderness, abnormal neurologic findings, or worsening vomiting warrants evaluation.** Blood work excludes electrolyte abnormalities and metabolic disease; endoscopy or imaging evaluates for organic disease.
- **Treatment:** scant investigation. Antagonists of histamine-1, dopamine-2 and 5-HT₃ receptors, and agonists of cannabinoid CB₁ receptors, exhibit antiemetic action. TCAs and psychological measures benefit some patients. Limited data support ginger, herbal remedies and electroacupuncture.

### B2b. Diagnostic criteria for cyclic vomiting syndrome

> Must include **all** of the following:
> 1. **Stereotypical episodes of repetitive vomiting** regarding onset (acute) and duration (**up to 10 days**)
> 2. **At least 3 discrete episodes in the prior year and 2 episodes in the past 6 months**, occurring **at least 1 week apart**
> 3. Milder symptoms including nausea and isolated vomiting can be present between cycles
>
> **Supportive remark:** history or family history of migraine headaches can be present.

- **Change from Rome IV:** minor changes acknowledging that some patients experience **minor vomiting during interepisodic phases**.
- **Four phases.** A **prodrome** of nausea, abdominal pain, fatigue and weakness, stereotypical in timing → within minutes to hours, a **3- to 6-day emetic phase** with unrelenting vomiting **>8 times hourly on average**; nearly two-thirds report abdominal pain and diarrhea → a **recovery phase lasting up to 1 week** with persistent nausea, lightheadedness, and meal intolerance in some but not all → an **interepisodic phase** lasting weeks to months, in which **up to 63%** still have milder nausea, dyspepsia and intermittent vomiting.
- Acute complications: fluid loss, electrolyte abnormalities, hematemesis; **dental enamel damage with repeated attacks**. **Compulsive hot bathing is reported by 48% of CVS patients who do not use cannabis.**
- Prevalence 1.4% in a Rome IV survey, greater in the United States than Canada or the United Kingdom; mean age of onset 32 years; more common in women. **78% screen positive for depression and 84% for anxiety.**
- **Treatment:** abortive treatment may not be feasible if the prodrome is short; after emesis starts, **nonoral** treatments are most likely to be beneficial. **Ondansetron** is commonly used for attacks but without CVS-specific trials. **Neurokinin-1 antagonists (aprepitant, fosaprepitant)** reduce emesis frequency and hospitalizations in refractory patients. Sedating agents — **benzodiazepines and histamine-1 antagonists** — are often a beneficial adjunct. **Intranasal or injectable triptans** may reduce vomiting and abdominal pain, especially with a family history of migraine.
- **Prevention:** TCAs produced improvement in **69%**; poor responses occurred with migraines, psychological illness, and cannabis or opioid use. **Topiramate** reduced attacks and health care use in **65%**. Patients refractory to TCA had **50% fewer attacks on zonisamide or levetiracetam**. Mitochondrial supplements (coenzyme Q10, L-carnitine, riboflavin) show some prophylactic efficacy. CBT is advocated for some patients.

### B2c. Diagnostic criteria for cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome

> Must include **all** of the following:
> 1. **Stereotypical episodic vomiting resembling cyclic vomiting syndrome** in onset, duration and frequency
> 2. **Presentation after prolonged (1 year) and excessive (≥4 days per week or ≥15 doses per week, or both) cannabis use**
> 3. **Relief of vomiting episodes by sustained cessation of cannabis use — at least 6 months or 3 typical emetic cycles**
>
> **Supportive remark:** may be associated with pathologic bathing behavior (prolonged hot baths or showers).

- **Change from Rome IV:** CHS criteria were **updated to quantify cannabis-use profiles and durations of abstinence** for diagnosis.
- Prevalence 0.12% overall, **0.4% in United States populations**. More common in men; median age at diagnosis 28 years; **a >4-year delay in diagnosis in 40% of patients**.
- **Acute emetic phases last a few hours to 1 week, with vomiting >20 times daily**, abdominal pain, diarrhea and autonomic symptoms. Up to **18%** of cases are not episodic, raising the possibility that not all CHS mimics CVS. **Extreme compulsive bathing is prevalent and is reported by 92% of patients** to relieve vomiting and pain.
- Diagnostic testing is rarely indicated except for complications (e.g. hemorrhage). **Urine drug testing for tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) metabolites is considered for patients who do not admit to cannabis use, but no metabolite profile is specific for CHS.** Cannabis metabolites persist ≥1 month and cannabis-induced brain impairments may endure longer.
- **Confirming symptom resolution after cannabis cessation is a roadblock to diagnosis** — one analysis found **only 19% of patients were able to abstain from cannabis for >4 weeks**.
- Factors associated with the diagnosis: cannabis use >1 year, daily use, at least weekly use, symptom resolution with cessation, compulsive bathing to relieve symptoms, male sex, and age <50 years.
- **59% screen positive for anxiety and 68% for depression.**
- **Treatment:** cannabis cessation leads to resolution, and resuming cannabis promotes resurgence; **CBT may help reduce cannabis dependence**. Abortive: **cutaneous capsaicin** (a transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 1 [TRPV1] agonist) shortens episodes and decreases therapy requirements; hot showers plausibly work through the same skin TRPV1 thermoreceptors. **Parenteral dopamine-2 antagonists haloperidol and droperidol** show efficacy in uncontrolled series and a controlled trial. **Benzodiazepines (lorazepam, alprazolam)** provide relief in small series. **CHS responds poorly to preventive TCA therapy**; one small series described effective prophylaxis with **olanzapine**.

### B3. Diagnostic criteria for belching disorders

> **Bothersome** (severe enough to impact usual activities) **belching from the esophagus or stomach >3 days a week.**
>
> **B3a. Supragastric belching** (from esophagus) · **B3b. Gastric belching** (from stomach)
>
> Supportive criteria:
> 1. Supragastric belching is strongly supported by observing frequent, repetitive belching
> 2. Supragastric belching can be interrupted by maneuvers or distraction
> 3. **Excessive gastric belching has no defining clinical correlate**
> 4. Intraluminal esophageal impedance measurement (with or without concomitant manometry) allows objective differentiation of supragastric from gastric belches
>
> Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset ≥6 months before diagnosis.

- **Upper limit of normal:** there is no solid data for gastric belches, but for **excessive supragastric belches the upper limit was defined as >13 per 24 hours**.
- Patients presenting with excessive belching are more likely affected by **supragastric** than gastric belching, especially when the frequency is very high (e.g. **10–20 times per minute**). Distraction reduces the frequency; drawing attention to the behavior increases it.
- Confirmation when uncertain: **esophageal manometry-impedance testing with prolonged registration, often after a meal challenge, or 24-hour ambulatory pH-impedance monitoring**. In gastric belching, a plain abdominal radiograph may show gaseous distension of stomach and bowel loops, but sensitivity and specificity are uncertain.
- **GERD overlap:** in patients meeting Rome IV criteria for excessive belching compared with patients with GERD, **supragastric was the most common type in both groups (93% vs 87% of belches)**. Acid-suppressive treatment diminishes the incidence of these belches; supragastric belches can also provoke reflux, and **treating supragastric belching with CBT reduces acid reflux**.
- Excessive supragastric belching can be **mistaken for hiccups** when the patient is not observed during an episode — **a smartphone video recording can provide clarity**. Hiccups are not associated with abnormal air transports in the esophagus.
- **Treatment.** Reassurance and explanation of the mechanism matter; some physicians demonstrate that they can belch intentionally, or interrupt the patient's belching by **biting on a pencil between the teeth**, to convince the patient the behavior is learned. **Gastric belching:** dietary modification (avoid sucking candies, chewing gum, eating slowly, encouraging small swallows, avoiding carbonated beverages) — commonly recommended but not rigorously tested; **contrary to popular belief, gum chewing had no effect on supragastric or gastric belching**. There is **no evidence to support surface tension-reducing drugs such as dimethicone and simethicone**. **Supragastric belching:** speech therapy by a dedicated, well-informed therapist led to an **83% positive symptom response rate** — the first step is making the patient aware of the behavior that causes the air to be brought into the esophagus, accompanied by abdominal breathing and open-mouth breathing exercises. **Dedicated CBT** improves subjective symptoms and reduces objectively measured supragastric belches. Diaphragmatic breathing training may also be used.

### B4. Diagnostic criteria for inability to belch syndrome (new in Rome V)

> 1. **Bothersome inability, or impaired ability, to belch at least 3 days per week**
> 2. **No evidence of underlying major esophageal disease/dysfunction** that explains the symptom (e.g. achalasia, fundoplication, esophagogastric junction outflow obstruction, GERD)
>
> Supportive criteria:
> 1. Associated symptoms that can be present: chest pain · gurgling noises in the chest · bloating · flatulence · epigastric pain
> 2. Esophageal impedance manometry findings are supportive (air entrapment/oscillations, gas reflux episodes not followed by adequate upper esophageal sphincter [UES] relaxation)
>
> Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset ≥6 months before diagnosis.

- Also known as **retrograde cricopharyngeal dysfunction**. Mechanism: **ineffective relaxation of the UES in response to gastroesophageal gas reflux**. In health, injecting boluses of air into the mid-esophagus relaxes the UES; in this syndrome gas injection **fails to elicit UES relaxation** and instead produces common-cavity pressurization, oscillations of air load in the esophagus, and secondary peristalsis clearing the air into the stomach — the same events observable during high-resolution impedance manometry using **carbonated water swallows as a provocative test**.
- Diagnosis is **clinical**, from patient-reported inability to belch with the associated symptoms. High-resolution impedance manometry with belch provocation by carbonated water confirmed high prevalence and specificity of manometric abnormalities in retrograde cricopharyngeal dysfunction patients, and those abnormalities were **largely eliminated or improved by botulinum toxin injection**; its value in the presence of typical suggestive symptoms remains to be determined.
- **Treatment: injection of 50 to 100 IU of botulinum toxin in the UES**, mainly performed by ear-nose-throat specialists targeting by anatomical landmarks or confirmatory electromyography. **No sham-controlled trial exists**, so the precise responder rate and long-term outcome are unclear, but **clinical effects seem to last ≥6 months in most patients, and sustained remission also seems to occur**. Occasional reports of hypnosis and of surgery (cricopharyngeal myotomy).
- Probably **under-diagnosed** due to its recent emergence and lack of clinician awareness; it may also be **over-diagnosed**, because most patients are self-referred and objective confirmation of UES dysfunction is rarely performed.

### B5. Diagnostic criteria for rumination syndrome

> Must include **all** of the following:
> 1. **Recurrent, seemingly effortless regurgitation of recently ingested food into the mouth, with subsequent oral expulsion or swallowing**
> 2. **Regurgitation is not preceded by retching or nausea**
>
> Supportive criteria:
> 1. The regurgitated material contains recognizable food that might have a pleasant taste
> 2. The process tends to cease when the regurgitated material becomes acidic
> 3. **The presence of heartburn may help to differentiate regurgitation in the context of GERD from rumination**
> 4. Combined manometry and impedance monitoring can be used to establish an objective diagnosis of rumination
>
> Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset ≥6 months before diagnosis.

- **Changes from Rome IV:** a new supportive remark underlines that **objective diagnosis by esophageal impedance monitoring with concurrent manometry is available** to distinguish rumination from other conditions such as GERD. The observation that rumination events are usually **not preceded by nausea was moved from the supportive criteria into the second diagnostic criterion**, because the committee felt that characteristic is of diagnostic relevance. A supportive criterion was added to help differentiate reflux disease from rumination.
- Mechanism: most rumination events begin with an **intra-abdominal pressure elevation from voluntary abdominal contraction** moving gastric contents into the esophagus. A subset has **secondary rumination** — gastric contents enter the esophagus *before* abdominal straining begins, so the patient perceives a spontaneous reflux episode and strains in response. A third type is **immediately preceded by a supragastric belch**.
- Prevalence **3.1%** overall, more common in women (54.5% vs 45.5%), mean age 44.5 years; **up to 12.8%** in patients presenting for evaluation of gastric symptoms. Occurs in males and females of all ages and levels of cognitive function.
- Commonly **misdiagnosed as GERD, gastroparesis, unexplained vomiting, or an eating disorder**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **PDS vs EPS assignment:** Rome V moves postprandial epigastric pain into PDS; Rome IV placed it in EPS. Pages written against Rome IV will assign some patients to the wrong subtype.
- **Gastroparesis vs PDS with delayed gastric emptying:** Rome V reserves "gastroparesis" for predominant nausea or recurrent vomiting, so a patient with postprandial fullness and delayed emptying is **not** labelled gastroparetic here. US gastroparesis guidelines define the entity by delayed emptying with compatible symptoms.
- **Celiac serology in dyspepsia:** Rome V does not recommend routine testing, citing low yield.
- **CNVS frequency threshold** changed from Rome IV; pages quoting the older threshold are stale.
- **CHS as a distinct entity is debated** — whether it is a subtype of CVS or distinct; up to 80% of CVS patients use cannabis to relieve symptoms and likely do not have CHS.
