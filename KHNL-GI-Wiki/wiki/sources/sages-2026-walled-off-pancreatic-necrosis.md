## Summary

Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons (SAGES) guideline, developed jointly with a panel drawn from the SAGES Guidelines and Hepato-pancreato-biliary/Solid Organ Committees and the Americas Hepato-Pancreato-Biliary Association (AHPBA), addressing **which modality to use for the initial management of symptomatic walled-off pancreatic necrosis (WOPN)** in adults. Four key questions compare image-guided intervention, endoscopic intervention, minimally invasive surgery (MIS), open surgery, and the step-up approach head-to-head. The panel was multidisciplinary (surgeons, gastroenterologists, interventional radiologists) and included three patient partners who voted on outcomes, utility values and the evidence-to-decision value/acceptability domains.

Methodology was a prospectively registered systematic review (PROSPERO CRD420250654995; PubMed, CINAHL, Embase, Cochrane searched through 31 December 2024; studies before 2000 excluded) feeding GRADE certainty assessment and GRADE Evidence-to-Decision (EtD) tables in GRADEpro GDT. Population was adults with WOPN — an **encapsulated collection of pancreatic or peripancreatic necrosis at least 4 weeks after acute necrotizing pancreatitis** per the 2012 revised Atlanta classification, confirmed by imaging or by onset of symptoms. Mixed-population studies were admitted only when >50% of patients had presumed WOPN. Critical outcomes were 90-day mortality, repeat intervention, quality of life, return to the operating room for complications, surgery for insufficient source control, organ failure (Clavien–Dindo IV), and complications (bleeding/transfusion, fistula, perforation, stent dislodgement); length of stay was rated important. Consensus required ≥80% of votes.

**Every recommendation carries very low certainty of evidence and is conditional** — the panel issued no strong recommendation. The recommendations are not transitive (see *Contradictions / Open Questions*): endoscopic intervention is suggested over image-guided, image-guided is suggested over open surgery and judged interchangeable with MIS, and MIS is suggested over endoscopic. The panel's narrative resolves this by making anatomy, local expertise and resources the operative determinants rather than a fixed modality hierarchy, and by reserving open surgery for the unstable patient in septic shock. A separate emphasis runs through the document: **optimal treatment should include debridement, not drainage alone.**

Background figures the guideline gives: acute necrotizing pancreatitis occurs in approximately 20% of pancreatitis cases with a mortality of 8–40%; WOPN occurs in about 1–9% of patients with acute pancreatitis, though the panel judges the true incidence likely between 3% and 20%, rising with the severity of the index episode. Presentation ranges from asymptomatic to florid sepsis; symptoms include abdominal pain, nausea, emesis, early satiety, weight loss and jaundice, and complications include infection, bleeding, persistent sepsis and obstruction. Non-operative management is first-line because a large proportion of collections resolve spontaneously; the symptomatic patient who fails to improve on non-operative management is the one who needs source control and debridement.

### KQ1 — image-guided vs endoscopic intervention

- Three non-randomized studies pooled (six considered; three excluded as heterogeneity sources after sensitivity analysis, one of those retained for quality of life alone). About 64% of patients had WOPN in each arm.
- **No outcome favored image-guided intervention.** Image-guided was worse across every available outcome; combined magnitude judged large. Harms of image-guided vs endoscopic: mortality odds ratio (OR) 4.80 (95% CI 1.03–22.39; n = 467); combined reintervention OR 3.94 (2.33–6.65; n = 288); surgical reintervention OR 12.76 (2.38–68.49); organ failure (Clavien–Dindo IV) OR 5.42 (1.65–17.79; n = 163); length of stay mean difference 15.75 days higher (3.66–27.84). Return to the operating room (OR 4.44, 0.42–47.50) and complications (OR 5.23, 0.92–29.70) crossed the line of null effect; quality of life (social-functioning domain only) 2.5 lower, not significant.
- Certainty very low throughout: high risk of bias from selection bias and no adjustment for confounders, plus imprecision and heterogeneity.
- **Practice guidance:** WOPN with favorable anatomy — a collection abutting the gastrointestinal tract — and available local endoscopic expertise should undergo endoscopic intervention first, with interventional radiology (IR) reserved as an adjunct if endoscopy fails. For **collections without enteric contact, poorly organized collections, or where advanced endoscopic expertise is unavailable**, an IR approach may be indicated initially, **with early transfer to a tertiary center if unsuccessful**.
- Endoscopic feasibility depends on endoscopic access to the collection, availability of endoscopic ultrasound, and gastrointestinal expertise; IR is more widely available but may lack pancreatic specialization. Both require a safe access window free of intervening vessels or critical structures.
- Subgroups where endoscopic intervention may be less effective or feasible: **patients presenting with sepsis, and unorganized collections.**
- Some panelists were concerned that combining endoscopic and percutaneous intervention risks an **enterocutaneous fistula**; a **percutaneous transgastric route** can often avert it.
- All included studies were performed at tertiary referral centers — a health-equity caveat the panel raises explicitly.

### KQ2 — image-guided vs surgery

- Four non-randomized studies; one at low risk of bias, the rest high. Image-guided was compared with open necrosectomy and, separately, with MIS.
- **Image-guided vs open surgery — benefits of image-guided (large combined magnitude):** mortality OR 0.40 (0.20–0.82; n = 222); IR reintervention OR 0.14 (0.02–0.83); quality of life (social functioning only) mean difference 35 higher (24.45–45.55). Return to the operating room (OR 0.38, 0.02–8.20), complications (OR 0.90, 0.25–3.26), organ failure (OR 0.50, 0.19–1.34) and length of stay (5.27 days lower) all crossed the line of null effect.
- **Image-guided vs open surgery — harm of image-guided:** **surgical reintervention OR 4.28 (2.00–9.17; n = 185)**, magnitude large.
- **Image-guided vs MIS:** only mortality (OR 0.46, 0.02–12.45), organ failure (OR 0.38, 0.07–2.03) and complications (OR 2.00, 0.32–12.33) were evaluable, all from a single study and all crossing the line of null effect — hence the recommendation that either is acceptable.
- **Practice guidance:** open surgery remains the gold standard for the **unstable patient in septic shock**. Patients with a **"hostile abdomen" or multiple prior abdominal operations** may not be suitable MIS candidates. Image-guided management may require more procedures but allows recovery between them.

### KQ3 — endoscopic vs surgery

- Two low-risk-of-bias randomized controlled trials (RCTs) supplied the data presented to the panel, plus five high-risk-of-bias observational studies. A third RCT (Bakker) was excluded because its surgical arm was open necrosectomy rather than cystenterostomy. Total RCT n = 100.
- **Benefits of endoscopic over MIS:** complications OR 0.47 (0.15–1.42; 2 RCTs) and quality of life mean difference 19.3 higher (10.73–27.87) — the latter from a single observational study mixing open and MIS arms, which the panel weighted down. Combined magnitude moderate.
- **Equivalent:** mortality (one death in the endoscopic arm, none surgical, across 50 patients per arm — absolute effect not calculable), return to the operating room OR 1.00 (0.06–17.18), organ failure OR 1.00 (0.13–7.60), length of stay 0.68 days higher (−4.13 to 5.5; **low** certainty, the only outcome in the guideline above very low).
- **Harms of endoscopic vs MIS — reintervention, combined magnitude large:** IR reintervention OR 3.57 (0.53–24.09), endoscopic reintervention OR 3.20 (0.37–27.48), surgical reintervention OR 1.68 (0.30–9.36). All crossed the line of null effect.
- Certainty very low despite RCT data, downgraded for imprecision from low event rates and small samples.
- **Practice guidance:** the panel suggests MIS drainage and debridement rather than endoscopic management "based on patient factors, surgeon experience, and local resources," acknowledging that complications and quality of life favored endoscopy while reintervention rates favored MIS. Open surgery remains the standard of care in septic shock. Patients with severe comorbidities who are not operative candidates are a subgroup with limited evidence — the included RCTs evaluated only MIS, leaving no RCT evidence on open surgery for them.

### KQ4 — pure endoscopic vs step-up approach

- Two low-risk-of-bias RCTs (van Brunschot and Onnekink — the **same population** reported at 6 months and 7 years, not pooled, informing different outcomes) plus two observational studies. An RCT comparing direct endoscopic necrosectomy with an endoscopic step-up approach was excluded as not addressing the question.
- **The step-up arm in the underlying trials used video-assisted retroperitoneal debridement (VARD) — a minimally invasive approach, not open surgery.** No outcome from an open approach was included.
- **Indirectness flagged:** the trial population had infected pancreatic necrosis, and 36% (pure endoscopic) and 43% (step-up) of patients were intervened on within the 4-week window (no significant difference); median time from symptom onset to intervention was 39 days, which met the >50%-presumed-WOPN inclusion rule.
- **Benefits of pure endoscopic vs step-up (combined magnitude large):** surgical reintervention OR 0.28 (0.05–1.46), complications OR 0.67 (0.30–1.51), **organ failure OR 0.36 (0.14–0.95)**, length of stay 16 days lower (−32.86 to +0.86). Long-term (7-year) quality of life equivalent (EQ-5D mean difference 0.06 lower; minimal important difference 0.04 — **low** certainty, ~40% dropout).
- **Harms of pure endoscopic vs step-up (combined magnitude large):** mortality OR 1.46 (0.48–4.48, not significant); **IR reintervention OR 4.48 (1.36–14.70)** and **endoscopic reintervention OR 2.96 (1.18–7.39)** — both of which do **not** cross the line of null effect.
- **Practice guidance:** if the collection is anatomically favorable and accessible for endoscopy, endoscopic intervention is the preferred approach; otherwise either approach is reasonable. **Extent of necrosis is the key patient factor** — a step-up approach to VARD typically allows greater access and more debridement tools in severe necrosis. The step-up algorithm should be standardized, as in the PANTER trial. The enterocutaneous-fistula concern about combined endoscopic plus percutaneous intervention, and the percutaneous transgastric workaround, are repeated here.

### Cross-cutting points

- **Debridement, not drainage alone,** is what the panel emphasizes as optimal treatment.
- Cost was discussed by the panel but did not influence decision-making; no formal cost analysis was performed.
- Health equity was a formal EtD domain: both advanced endoscopy and HPB-capable MIS require workforce and infrastructure that rural and community hospitals may lack.
- Limitations the guideline states: every recommendation rests on very low certainty evidence; WOPN collections are highly variable; selection bias and confounding are expected and were largely uncontrolled in the included studies; "not all collections can be treated the same way."
- Scheduled for reevaluation in three years, earlier if new data require it.
- **What others are saying:** the AGA 2020 clinical practice update on pancreatic necrosis ([[aga-2020-cpu-pancreatic-necrosis]]) called both transmural endoscopic and percutaneous drainage appropriate first-line treatments, with percutaneous particularly preferred in those too ill for endoscopic or surgical drainage or as an adjunct for deep extension of disease; SAGES notes that update was not based on a meta-analysis and did not primarily focus on WOPN. A 2016 eight-country Asian consensus produced 27 statements on WOPN but used neither a systematic review nor GRADE and made no head-to-head comparisons.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **The recommendations are non-transitive, and the guideline does not reconcile them.** Endoscopic is suggested over image-guided (Rec 1), image-guided is suggested over open surgery and judged equivalent to MIS (Recs 2, 4), yet MIS is suggested over endoscopic (Rec 5). Each comparison rests on a different, very-low-certainty body of evidence — KQ1 on three retrospective studies favouring endoscopy on every outcome, KQ3 on two small RCTs where reintervention rates favoured MIS and complications and quality of life favoured endoscopy. The panel's own narrative makes anatomy, local expertise and resources decisive rather than modality rank, and in KQ4 states that an endoscopically accessible, anatomically favorable collection should be managed endoscopically.
- **Conflict with [[aga-2020-cpu-pancreatic-necrosis]] on first-line modality.** AGA 2020 Best Practice Advice 7 treats transmural endoscopic and percutaneous drainage as both appropriate first-line, with endoscopic possibly preferred; SAGES 2026 grades endoscopic as superior to image-guided across every outcome measured when expertise is available. Same tier, SAGES newer and systematic-review based, so SAGES governs the page; AGA's preference for percutaneous in the patient too ill for endoscopy or surgery, and as an adjunct for deep extension, is not contradicted and stands.
- **Conflict with the wiki's step-up framing.** [[pancreatic-fluid-collection-drainage]] presents surgery as step 3 after drainage and direct endoscopic necrosectomy. SAGES Rec 5 suggests MIS *over* endoscopic treatment as initial management, and Rec 7 makes a pure endoscopic approach and a step-up approach interchangeable. The step-up sequence is not withdrawn — the KQ4 trials' step-up arm was VARD, and SAGES endorses a standardized PANTER-style algorithm — but initial MIS is now an explicitly graded alternative to endoscopy rather than a salvage step.
- **Unresolved by the guideline:** whether open surgery benefits patients with severe comorbidity who are not MIS candidates (no RCT evidence); outcomes in septic shock and in unorganized collections (expert opinion only); the true comparative effect sizes, given that nearly every confidence interval crossed at least one decision threshold.
- **Terminology:** this guideline writes **WOPN**; the revised Atlanta classification and the rest of the wiki use **walled-off necrosis (WON)** for the same entity.
