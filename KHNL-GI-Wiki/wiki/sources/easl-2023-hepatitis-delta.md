## Summary

The first international clinical practice guideline (CPG) commissioned by the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) dedicated to hepatitis delta virus (HDV). HDV is a defective RNA virus that borrows all three hepatitis B virus (HBV) envelope proteins to enter and egress from the hepatocyte; it cannot replicate without hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg). Chronic hepatitis D (CHD) is considered the most aggressive chronic viral hepatitis, with rapid and frequent progression to [[cirrhosis]], decompensation and [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)]].

The panel of 29 Delphi experts addressed six topics — screening; diagnosis and stratification by virologic and liver-disease profile; clinical aspects, natural history and cofactors; monitoring and selection for treatment; therapeutic approaches by viral target (HDV, HBV or both) plus liver transplantation; and treatment endpoints. Questions were framed in PICO format, graded by the Oxford Centre for Evidence-based Medicine (OCEBM) levels, and required ≥75% Delphi agreement for approval.

The document's two central practical shifts are: **universal, repeatable anti-HDV screening of every HBsAg-positive individual** (rather than risk-based testing), and the position that **every patient with CHD is a candidate for antiviral therapy** — EASL sets no HDV RNA or alanine aminotransferase (ALT) threshold that must be crossed before treatment is considered. Treatment is framed as a choice between a **finite** strategy (pegylated interferon-α [pegIFNα] ± bulevirtide [BLV], aiming to cure) and a **prolonged** strategy (BLV, aiming to control), with the stage of liver disease the major determinant.

Nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) are explicitly **not** anti-HDV therapy — they are given only when control of HBV replication is independently warranted, and the guideline prints the three HBV-based triggers. The transplant section gives the hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG) + high-genetic-barrier NA regimen and the anti-HBs target. The monitoring section gives a full calendar of virologic, biochemical, imaging and histologic testing intervals that differ by treatment strategy.

## Grading System

The document grades each recommendation on **two axes**, and gives the **Delphi consensus level** alongside:

- **Strength of recommendation** (Table 1) — **Strong** ("shall, should, is recommended" / "shall not, should not, is not recommended") vs **Weak or open** ("can, may, is suggested" / "may not, is not suggested").
- **Level of evidence (LoE)** (Table 2, adapted from the Oxford 2011 Levels of Evidence) — **1** systematic reviews of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) with homogeneity · **2** RCTs or observational studies with dramatic effects; systematic reviews of lower-quality studies · **3** non-randomised controlled cohort / follow-up study / control arm of an RCT · **4** case-series, case-control, or historically controlled studies · **5** expert opinion (mechanism-based reasoning).
- **Delphi consensus** — reported as *consensus* (75–90% approval) or *strong consensus* (≥90%). `n.a.` where the panel did not vote a strength.

⚠ **The document does not number its recommendations.** The numbering below is the wiki's, added for reference only; the grades and consensus labels are exactly as printed.

## Key Recommendations

### Screening

*Question: How and which HBsAg-positive individuals should be screened for HDV infection?*

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Screen for **anti-HDV antibodies with a validated assay at least once in all HBsAg-positive individuals** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 2 | **Re-test** for anti-HDV antibodies in HBsAg-positive individuals **whenever clinically indicated** — e.g. aminotransferase flares, or acute decompensation of chronic liver disease | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 2b | Re-testing **may** be performed **yearly in those remaining at risk** of infection | LoE 5, weak recommendation, strong consensus |

Supporting points:

- EASL and the Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL) HBV CPGs already recommend HDV testing in all HBsAg-positive patients; the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) hepatitis B guidance recommends only **risk-based** screening.
- **Reflex testing** (anti-HDV automatically on every new HBsAg-positive result) produced a **5-fold increase** in HDV diagnoses in one programme; 60% of those found had **no common risk factor** and 60% had **advanced fibrosis**.
- Anti-HDV prevalence by group: ~15% in [[hiv-aids-related-diarrhea|human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)]] co-infection, 30% in institutionalised persons, up to 67% in people who inject drugs (PWID); pooled odds ratios 3.4 (haemodialysis), 16.0 (men who have sex with men), 10.0 (anti-hepatitis C virus [HCV]-positive).

### Diagnosis and Stratification

*Question: Which diagnostic test should be used to diagnose ongoing HDV infection?*

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | **HDV RNA should be tested in all anti-HDV-positive individuals**, using a standardised and sensitive reverse-transcription PCR assay, to diagnose active HDV infection | LoE 2, strong recommendation, strong consensus |

*Question: Which HBV markers should be tested in patients with acute or chronic HDV infection?*

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | In **acute hepatitis**, use **anti-HBc IgM** to distinguish HBV/HDV **coinfection** from an HBsAg-positive individual newly **superinfected** with HDV | LoE 3, strong recommendation, consensus |
| 5 | Test **HBeAg/anti-HBe status and HBV DNA level**, because active HBV infection may worsen the outcome of hepatitis D | LoE 3, strong recommendation, consensus |

*Question: When should liver biopsy and non-invasive tests (NITs) be used in the clinical management of hepatitis D?*

> **Statement:** Fully published data on the use of NITs in patients with CHD are currently limited, and the correlation with liver histology is missing in a significant proportion of cases **(LoE 4, strong consensus).**

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | **Liver biopsy is recommended** whenever it may contribute to management, or **for grading and staging** when clinical signs or indirect imaging evidence of cirrhosis are **absent** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, consensus |
| 7 | **NITs may be used to assess advanced liver disease, but specific cut-off values are not well established** | LoE 5, weak recommendation, strong consensus |

Supporting detail on NIT performance in CHD (why the cut-offs are unsettled):

- Transient elastography (TE) **performs better at ruling out than ruling in** cirrhosis in CHD.
- Reported TE thresholds for cirrhosis: **10, 12.5 and 14 kPa** — sensitivity/specificity 95/75%, 77.8/82.5% and 75/81.5% respectively; the 14 kPa cut-off gave 78% sensitivity, 86% specificity, **negative predictive value (NPV) 93%, positive predictive value (PPV) 64%**.
- In the largest histology-controlled report (n = 235) fibrosis-score performance was poor — sensitivity 13% (aspartate aminotransferase-to-alanine aminotransferase ratio [AAR]), 27% (aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index [APRI]), 29% (Fibrosis-4 index [FIB-4]), 31% (FibroTest) — vs TE sensitivity 47%, NPV 77%.
- **53% of 230 patients** with cirrhosis on biopsy were **misclassified** by fibrosis scores (only 29% by TE) — necro-inflammation, which is prominent in CHD, inflates stiffness.
- A **CHD-specific score, the delta-4 fibrosis score (D4FS)**, combines TE with gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), platelet count and ALT: AUROC 0.94 for cirrhosis in the validation cohort.
- EASL's position: use the **Baveno VII TE thresholds** to identify compensated advanced chronic liver disease (cACLD) and clinically significant [[portal-hypertension|portal hypertension]] (CSPH) — see [[baveno-viii-2026-portal-hypertension]] for the current version.

### Clinical Aspects, Natural History and Cofactors

*Question: Which factors identify patients with CHD at higher risk of liver disease progression?*

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | Factors to consider: **elevated aminotransferases and GGT, advanced stage of liver disease, persistence of HDV viraemia, high serum HBV DNA, and viral coinfections.** Cofactors of chronic liver injury — **alcohol abuse, obesity and diabetes** — should also be considered | LoE 4, strong recommendation, strong consensus |

*Question: How and when should HCC surveillance be performed in patients with CHD?*

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | **HCC surveillance with abdominal ultrasound every 6 months** in patients with CHD and **advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis**, regardless of anti-HDV therapy | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |

- The guideline defines what it means by the stratum: **advanced fibrosis = bridging fibrosis, METAVIR F3 or Ishak stage 4–5**; **cirrhosis = METAVIR F4, Ishak stage 6**.
- Because **no NIT threshold is validated** to define advanced fibrosis in CHD, the decision to start surveillance **must rest on imaging, clinical or bio-humoral signs** of advanced liver disease rather than on a stiffness number.
- Non-cirrhotic patients with **bridging (F3) fibrosis** may reasonably be surveilled, especially with other HCC risk factors (alcohol, tobacco, obesity, family history, aflatoxin exposure, sub-Saharan African origin, HIV or HCV coinfection).
- **Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) is optional**, as in the EASL and AASLD HCC guidelines; computed tomography (CT) or contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may be substituted where ultrasound is technically inadequate (severe obesity, hepatic steatosis).

### Patient Monitoring and Selection for Treatment

*Question: How should untreated patients with CHD be monitored?*

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | Patients with CHD should receive **regular work-up for liver disease at least every 6–12 months** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 11 | Virological parameters in that work-up should **ideally include quantitative assays for HBsAg, HBV DNA and HDV RNA** | LoE 5, strong recommendation, consensus |

- **HDV RNA should be monitored at least yearly in untreated patients**, with repeat tests at **3 or 6 months** to distinguish true HDV clearance from spontaneous fluctuation in replication.
- Where advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis is present, **clinical, biochemical and imaging follow-up continues irrespective of HDV RNA clearance** — liver disease may still progress, and HBV can relapse when HDV is suppressed.
- **HBV DNA and HBsAg should be monitored yearly**, or when major fluctuations in HDV RNA or ALT flares are observed.

*Question: Which patients with CHD should be considered for antiviral treatment?*

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | **All patients with CHD should be considered for antiviral treatment** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, consensus |
| 13 | Patients with **decompensated cirrhosis should be evaluated for [[liver-transplantation\|liver transplantation]]** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 14 | Patients with **HCC may be considered for antiviral treatment on an individualised basis** | LoE 5, weak recommendation, strong consensus |

- ⚠ **No HDV RNA or ALT threshold gates treatment.** The driving factor for progression is persistence of viral replication, and cirrhosis can develop subclinically in patients with mild but long-lasting necro-inflammation, so every patient is a potential candidate; the decision is then individualised.
- There is **no licensed treatment for CHD-related decompensated cirrhosis** — those patients are evaluated for transplantation, which has an excellent outcome in CHD.
- In CHD **with HCC**, treatment of the HCC (including transplantation) **takes priority**; antiviral therapy is case-by-case.
- In **mild liver disease (F0–F1)**, interferon-α (IFNα) use should be weighed carefully against the new agents in development, given its toxicity.

### Therapeutic Approaches

*Question: Which patients with CHD can be treated with pegIFNα?*

> **Statement:** IFNα has been used since the 1990s for CHD. Mono- and multicentre studies have been conducted, with **only two randomised phase II studies published**; nevertheless, long-term data on clinical benefit and safety are available **(LoE 2, strong consensus).**

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 15 | **All patients with CHD and compensated liver disease — irrespective of whether they have cirrhosis — should be considered for pegIFNα** | LoE 2, strong recommendation, consensus |
| 16 | **PegIFNα for 48 weeks should be the preferred treatment schedule** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, consensus |
| 17 | **Personalised treatment durations may be considered**, based on HDV RNA and HBsAg kinetics and treatment tolerability | LoE 3, weak recommendation, strong consensus |

Supporting detail:

- **PegIFNα is contraindicated in decompensated cirrhosis.** In compensated cirrhosis, specifically look for **CSPH and oesophageal [[variceal-upper-gi-bleeding|varices]]** — the risk of major adverse events is higher in this subset and the cost/benefit must be weighed carefully.
- **Virologic response (undetectable HDV RNA 24 weeks post-treatment): 29% (17–47%)** in a meta-analysis of 13 studies; pegIFNα outperformed standard IFNα (**25% vs 17%**).
- **More than 50% of responders at 24 weeks post-treatment relapse later, up to 10 years after the end of treatment** — yet patients undetectable at 6 months post-treatment, or at any point in follow-up, still had **fewer liver-related events** than non-responders.
- **On-treatment prediction:** week-24 HDV RNA is the strongest predictor — undetectable HDV RNA at week 24 had a **PPV of 100%** for virologic response at 24 weeks post-treatment. A **<1 log decline at the end of therapy** identified non-response with 67% sensitivity, 85% specificity, 67% PPV, **91% NPV**. HBsAg **<1,000 IU/mL at week 24**, and a **1.61 log HDV RNA decline at week 24** (AUROC 0.791, sensitivity 86%, specificity 68%), separate responders from non-responders.
- **Prolongation beyond 48 weeks** (to 2 years) increased virologic response in some studies but **did not improve liver histology in most patients**; it may be considered in good-compliance patients with a slow virologic response or progressive HBsAg decline.
- Combining pegIFNα with **NAs or ribavirin does not improve the virologic response.**
- **Watch for autoimmune hepatitis triggered by treatment** — anti-liver-kidney microsomal (LKM)-3 antibodies are frequently detected in CHD (up to 13% in Italian cohorts), and [[autoimmune-hepatitis]] can be triggered in a minority of patients.

*Question: Which patients with CHD can be treated with bulevirtide (BLV)?*

> **Statement:** Despite the lack of data on long-term efficacy and safety, or on the optimal duration of treatment, preliminary results from phase II studies (BLV as monotherapy or with pegIFNα), on-treatment data from a phase III trial of BLV monotherapy, and real-life studies suggest consideration of BLV as a treatment option for CHD **whenever available (LoE 3, consensus).**

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 18 | **All patients with CHD and compensated liver disease should be considered for BLV** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, consensus |
| 19 | **The optimal dose and duration have not yet been defined** (LoE 5, consensus). Until further data are available, **long-term treatment with BLV 2 mg once daily may be considered** | LoE 5, weak recommendation, consensus |
| 20 | The **combination of pegIFNα and BLV may be considered** in patients without pegIFNα intolerance or contraindications | LoE 5, weak recommendation, strong consensus |

Supporting detail:

- **BLV is a synthetic myristoylated lipopeptide of 47 amino acids** from the preS1 domain of the HBV large surface protein; it blocks HBsAg attachment to the cell-entry receptor **sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP)**. It does not stop **cell-division-mediated intrahepatic HDV spread**, which is NTCP-independent.
- **Dose:** 2 mg daily subcutaneously was granted conditional EMA marketing authorisation in July 2020, **with the recommendation to maintain treatment until clinical benefit is observed.** The lower 2 mg dose is preferred because it **does not saturate the bile acid transporter**; phase III interim analysis (MYR301) showed **10 mg gave no efficacy advantage over 2 mg**.
- **Efficacy:** ≥2 log HDV RNA decline at week 48 in **71% (2 mg)** and **76% (10 mg)**; at 1 year, undetectable HDV RNA and combined (virologic + biochemical) response in **12% and 45%** on 2 mg, **20% and 48%** on 10 mg. Real-world German cohort (mean 38 ± 17.6 weeks): ≥2 log decline in 74%, undetectable in 22%, **<1 log decline in 9%**, and **viral breakthrough (≥1 log rise) in 12% of virologic responders**.
- **Relapse is the reason for prolonged therapy:** 49/55 (89%) with a virologic response relapsed after discontinuation, with **aminotransferase flares in 22%**.
- **Safety:** well tolerated, no drug-related serious adverse events or treatment discontinuations across studies. Mild fatigue, nausea, headache, dizziness, platelet/white-cell reduction; injection-site reactions mild and transient.
- ⚠ **Bile acids rise in everyone on BLV** — a transient, dose-related increase in total bile acids was reported in all studies (median values up to **20 µmol/L on 2 mg and 80 µmol/L on 10 mg**), and is **symptomless, including no pruritus**.
- ⚠ **Drug interactions:** BLV partially inhibits **OATP1B1 and OATP1B3** at higher doses (not usually reached at the approved 2 mg subcutaneous dose) — avoid co-administering NTCP substrates where possible, including **fluvastatin, atorvastatin, pravastatin, rosuvastatin and thyroid hormones**. Co-administration of tenofovir and BLV **decreased clearance of the CYP3A substrate midazolam**.
- Where available, **BLV plus pegIFNα should be offered to compliant patients and preferably within clinical protocols.**
- **NAs must be considered at the time of BLV discontinuation** and initiated if HBV replication relapses, because blocking entry of both viruses exposes the patient to HBV reactivation on stopping.

*Question: When should NAs be used in patients with CHD?*

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 21 | NAs **should be given in decompensated cirrhosis, irrespective of whether HBV DNA is detectable** | LoE 5, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 22 | NAs **should be given in compensated cirrhosis with detectable HBV DNA** | LoE 5, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 23 | NAs **should be given in patients without cirrhosis if HBV DNA is higher than 2,000 IU/mL** | LoE 5, strong recommendation, strong consensus |

- ⚠ **NAs are not anti-HDV therapy.** Famciclovir, lamivudine, clevudine, entecavir, adefovir and tenofovir have all been given for 6–12 months and found **ineffective against HDV**; combining pegIFNα with an NA did not raise virologic response rates. **NAs are not recommended as part of antiviral therapy aimed at controlling CHD.**
- They are indicated only when **control of HBV replication is independently appropriate** — active HBV replication (**HBV DNA >2,000 IU/mL**) or when prevention of HBV reactivation is clinically mandated.

### Post-Transplant Prophylaxis

*Question: Which is the best prophylactic strategy for prevention of post-transplant hepatitis D recurrence?*

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 24 | Patients transplanted for CHD should receive **hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG) combined with a high-genetic-barrier NA** after transplantation | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 25 | **After the early post-transplant period (6 months), HBIG should be dosed to maintain anti-HBs serum levels >100 mIU/mL** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |

> **Statement (early period dose):** The optimal HBIG dose in the early post-transplant period **has not been defined and varies among centres.** Most experienced centres give **HBIG 10,000 IU intravenously in the anhepatic phase, then 600–1,000 IU intramuscularly or intravenously daily for 7 days, then weekly for 3 weeks, then monthly until month 3–6 (LoE 3, n.a.).**

> **Statement (duration):** Indefinite combination HBIG + NA is currently **considered the gold standard**, but evidence on **HBIG discontinuation after 1–2 years is gradually accumulating (LoE 4, n.a.).** Further studies, particularly within clinical trials, are warranted to assess the safety of this approach.

- **Liver failure, rather than HCC, is the most frequent reason for transplantation in CHD** — the opposite of chronic hepatitis B.
- **Intrahepatic HDV recurrence occurs in over 70% of cases** after transplant, but without HBV re-infection it causes only mild histological change and no progressive liver disease.
- Monoprophylaxis with an NA alone is **not considered acceptable** for HBV/HDV co-infected recipients: a small risk of HBsAg recurrence that is tolerable in HBV monoinfection is not tolerable when HDV is present.
- Among NAs, **entecavir and tenofovir alafenamide should be preferred**; tenofovir disoproxil fumarate is better avoided in the transplant setting because its nephrotoxicity risk may be exacerbated by concomitant calcineurin inhibitors.

### Treatment Endpoints and Monitoring

*Question: Which parameters should be monitored during and after treatment?*

**Virologic markers**

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 26 | Virological response should be **determined during and after therapy** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 27 | **HDV RNA quantified every 6 months during treatment**, and whenever there is a clinical indication | LoE 5, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 28 | **PegIFNα-based finite therapy:** test HDV RNA **at the end of treatment, at 6 and 12 months, and yearly thereafter** | LoE 4, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 29 | **On BLV discontinuation:** test HDV RNA **at discontinuation, then 1, 3, 6 and 12 months, and yearly thereafter**, to detect relapse of viral replication | LoE 4, strong recommendation, consensus |
| 30 | **HBsAg testing every year** during and after therapy | LoE 3, strong recommendation, consensus |
| 31 | **PegIFNα-based therapy:** quantitative HBsAg **may** be measured **every 6 months during and every 12 months after** treatment | LoE 3, weak recommendation, strong consensus |
| 32 | **HBV DNA every 6 months in all treated patients not on NA therapy** (LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus); on BLV discontinuation, **more frequent HBV DNA testing may be required** | LoE 5, weak recommendation, strong consensus |

**Biochemical markers**

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 33 | Test **biochemical markers of liver disease activity — aminotransferases, full blood count, and liver function tests whenever clinically indicated** — during antiviral treatment | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 34 | Frequency **at least every 3–6 months**, timing modulated by stage of liver disease and type of treatment | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 35 | **PegIFNα-based finite therapy:** test at the end of treatment, at **least at months 6 and 12** after the end of treatment, and yearly thereafter | LoE 4, strong recommendation, strong consensus |
| 36 | **On BLV discontinuation:** test at discontinuation and **at least at 1, 3, 6 and 12 months**, or more frequently as clinically needed | LoE 4, strong recommendation, strong consensus |

**Liver imaging, histology and clinical events**

| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 37 | **Liver stiffness may be determined yearly** during and after antiviral treatment of CHD | LoE 5, weak recommendation, strong consensus |
| 38 | **Liver biopsy** should be performed during and/or after antiviral treatment **where histological diagnosis would aid clinical management** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, consensus |
| 39 | Patients should be **monitored during and after treatment for liver-related clinical events** | LoE 3, strong recommendation, strong consensus |

Operational detail given in the text (not graded):

- **Aminotransferases on pegIFNα:** every **4 weeks for the first 12 weeks**, then every **6–8 weeks**; after the end of treatment at **weeks 24 and 48**, with additional checks at **weeks 4, 8 and 12** according to disease stage.
- **Aminotransferases on BLV monotherapy:** **every 12 weeks** seems reasonable; on **BLV discontinuation**, monitoring may be required in the **first 6 months** to catch an ALT flare from recurrent HDV replication.
- **Liver function tests:** every **3–6 months**, more often with cirrhosis or aminotransferase flares.
- **Complete blood count:** standard pegIFNα schedule; **at least every 3 months on BLV**.
- **Liver stiffness during pegIFNα may paradoxically rise** (immune-cell activation); on BLV it declines by weeks 24–48, probably reflecting reduced intrahepatic necro-inflammation — the clinical significance of both is uncertain.
- **Routine follow-up liver biopsies are not recommended** outside clinical trials/protocols; biopsy is useful to investigate unexplained ALT flares during IFNα-based treatment, since these may be autoimmune events requiring corticosteroids.
- **HBcrAg and HBV RNA testing is not currently recommended** in clinical practice for CHD.

## Key Findings / Claims

- **Universal, not risk-based, anti-HDV screening** of every HBsAg-positive person — at least once, repeated when clinically indicated, and optionally yearly in ongoing risk. Reflex testing raises HDV diagnoses 5-fold.
- **Anti-HDV positive → HDV RNA by standardised RT-PCR** is the only way to establish active infection. Serum HDAg and anti-HDV IgM are **not** suitable markers: HDAg is detectable only for about 2 weeks in acute infection, and anti-HDV IgM is a disease-activity surrogate, not a replication marker.
- **HDV RNA status cannot be assigned from a single result** — levels fluctuate and may become transiently undetectable, so the definition requires **repeated tests 3 to 6 months apart**.
- **Assay standardisation is a real clinical problem.** In the first World Health Organization international quality-control assessment, only **46.3% of 28 laboratories** properly quantified the serum panel and **57.1% failed to detect up to 10 samples**; African sub-genotype 1 and genotypes 5–8 are underestimated by several commercial assays. Sequential monitoring must use **the same assay in the same laboratory**, with results in **IU/mL**.
- **Every patient with CHD is a treatment candidate** — EASL sets no viral-load or ALT threshold to cross.
- **Two strategies, chosen by stage of liver disease:** finite (pegIFNα ± BLV, aiming to cure) vs prolonged (BLV, aiming to control).
- **PegIFNα 48 weeks** is the preferred schedule; contraindicated in decompensated cirrhosis; ~29% virologic response, with >50% late relapse but durable reduction in liver-related events in responders.
- **BLV 2 mg daily, long-term**, where available; optimal dose and duration undefined; **≥2 log HDV RNA decline in 71% at week 48**; relapse after stopping is the norm (89%).
- **NAs do not treat HDV.** Three HBV-based triggers only: decompensated cirrhosis (any HBV DNA), compensated cirrhosis with detectable HBV DNA, or no cirrhosis with **HBV DNA >2,000 IU/mL**.
- **Post-transplant:** HBIG + high-genetic-barrier NA, with HBIG titrated to **anti-HBs >100 mIU/mL** after month 6.
- **Monitoring calendars differ by strategy** — pegIFNα finite therapy is followed at EOT, 6 and 12 months then yearly; BLV discontinuation is followed at 1, 3, 6 and 12 months then yearly, because relapse is early and common.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **HCC surveillance population — resolved in favour of AASLD-IDSA 2025.** EASL 2023 restricts 6-monthly ultrasound to CHD with **advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis**; [[aasld-idsa-2025-chb-treatment|AASLD-IDSA 2025]] suggests surveillance in **all HBV-HDV co-infected adults independent of cirrhosis status** (Conditional, Very Low; HCC incidence 18.65 vs 4.84 per 1,000 person-years). Same tier, **AASLD-IDSA 2025 is newer**, so the wiki asserts surveillance for all. EASL's stratum definitions are retained because they are the only ones printed.
- **Threshold for starting treatment.** [[aga-2025-hepatitis-delta|AGA 2025]] reports the AASLD HBV-guidance indication as **elevated HDV RNA *and* elevated ALT** without quantifying either. EASL 2023 does not use a threshold at all — **all CHD patients are considered**. These are different framings rather than different numbers; the practical effect of EASL's position is that the missing thresholds do not gate treatment.
- **PegIFNα duration.** AASLD guidance (via AGA 2025) gives **12 months**; EASL gives **48 weeks** as the preferred schedule, with personalised durations permitted. No document reconciles the four-week difference.
- **Bulevirtide dose and duration remain formally undefined** (LoE 5). EASL suggests **2 mg long-term**; the US expanded-access programme uses **10 mg**, while EASL's own data show 10 mg gives no efficacy advantage.
- **NIT cut-offs in CHD are not established** — the guideline says so explicitly rather than giving a number, and the CHD-specific D4FS is not yet validated for routine use.
- **Does HDV viraemia level carry prognostic weight?** The guideline leaves this open: higher viral load correlates with higher aminotransferases and worse clinical outcomes in some cohorts, but whether viraemia level itself is prognostic "remains to be clarified."
- **HBIG discontinuation after 1–2 years post-transplant** is an open safety question the panel flags for trials.
