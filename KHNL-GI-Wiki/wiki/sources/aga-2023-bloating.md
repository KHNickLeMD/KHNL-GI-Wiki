## Summary

AGA Expert Review covering three highly prevalent, often-overlapping GI symptoms — **belching, abdominal bloating, and abdominal distention** — which, when frequent/severe enough to impair daily activity, are categorized as **disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBIs)**. Because systematic reviews were not performed, the best-practice-advice statements carry **no formal GRADE ratings**; where evidence was insufficient, level-5 (expert opinion) guidance is given.

Belching is separated from bloating/distention given differing location, pathophysiology, and diagnostic/treatment pathways. **Belching** is subdivided into **gastric** (a true transient LES relaxation letting gastric air escape; associated with GERD) and **supragastric** (a learned behavior — air sucked/injected into the esophagus and immediately expelled, independent of peristalsis; associated with anxiety, stops during sleep/distraction/speaking). **Aerophagia** (excess air swallowing) is distinct and presents mainly with bloating/flatulence. High-resolution manometry with impedance/impedance-pH differentiates the belching syndromes. Supragastric belching is a behavioral disorder → treated with brain–gut behavioral therapies (CBT, diaphragmatic breathing), speech therapy, and central neuromodulators, not PPIs.

**Abdominal bloating** (subjective sensation of fullness/gas/tightness) and **distention** (objective, visible increase in girth) have interrelated pathophysiology and are diagnosed by **Rome IV** criteria as primary DGBIs only when IBS, functional constipation/diarrhea, and functional dyspepsia are excluded. Prevalence of primary functional bloating/distention is ~3.5% (higher in women), but bloating/distention are >50% prevalent when overlapping other DGBIs. Diagnostic testing is **algorithmic and symptom-driven** — most patients need no labs/imaging/endoscopy absent alarm features. Management combines dietary (low-FODMAP, fructan/gluten avoidance with dietitian), treatment of underlying constipation/pelvic-floor dyssynergia (biofeedback), and central neuromodulators + brain–gut behavioral therapies for visceral hypersensitivity. **Probiotics are not recommended** for bloating/distention.

## Key Findings / Claims (Best Practice Advice — verbatim)

1. **BPA 1:** Clinical history and physical examination findings and impedance pH monitoring can help to differentiate between gastric and supragastric belching.
2. **BPA 2:** Treatment options for supragastric belching may include brain–gut behavioral therapies, either separately or in combination, such as cognitive behavioral therapy, diaphragmatic breathing, speech therapy, and central neuromodulators.
3. **BPA 3:** Rome IV criteria should be used to diagnose primary abdominal bloating and distention.
4. **BPA 4:** Carbohydrate enzyme deficiencies may be ruled out with dietary restriction and/or breath testing. In a small subset of at-risk patients, small bowel aspiration and glucose- or lactulose-based hydrogen breath testing may be used to evaluate for small intestinal bacterial overgrowth.
5. **BPA 5:** Serologic testing may rule out celiac disease in patients with bloating and, if serologies are positive, a small bowel biopsy should be done to confirm the diagnosis. A gastroenterology dietitian should be part of the multidisciplinary approach to care for patients with celiac disease and nonceliac gluten sensitivity.
6. **BPA 6:** Abdominal imaging and upper endoscopy should be ordered in patients with alarm features, recent worsening symptoms, or an abnormal physical examination only.
7. **BPA 7:** Gastric emptying studies should not be ordered routinely for bloating and distention, but may be considered if nausea and vomiting are present. Whole gut motility and radiopaque transit studies should not be ordered unless other additional and treatment-refractory lower gastrointestinal symptoms exist to warrant testing for neuromyopathic disorders.
8. **BPA 8:** In patients with abdominal bloating and distention thought to be related to constipation or difficult evacuation, anorectal physiology testing is suggested to rule out a pelvic floor disorder.
9. **BPA 9:** When dietary modifications are needed (eg, low-FODMAP diet), a gastroenterology dietitian should preferably monitor treatment.
10. **BPA 10:** Probiotics should not be used to treat abdominal bloating and distention.
11. **BPA 11:** Biofeedback therapy may be effective for bloating and distention when a pelvic floor disorder is identified.
12. **BPA 12:** Central neuromodulators (eg, antidepressants) are used to treat bloating and abdominal distention by reducing visceral hypersensitivity, raising sensation threshold, and improving psychological comorbidities.
13. **BPA 13:** Medications used to treat constipation should be considered for treating bloating if constipation symptoms are present.
14. **BPA 14:** Psychological therapies, such as hypnotherapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, and other brain–gut behavior therapies may be used to treat patients with bloating and distention.
15. **BPA 15:** Diaphragmatic breathing and central neuromodulators are used to treat abdominophrenic dyssynergia.

**Additional decision-relevant detail:**
- Rome IV **belching** = audible escape of air from esophagus/stomach into pharynx; "excessive belching" when bothersome and >3 days/week.
- Rome IV **functional bloating/distention** = recurrent fullness/pressure or visible girth increase ≥1 day/week, active ≥3 months, onset ≥6 months prior, without predominant pain or altered bowel habits, and not meeting IBS/functional constipation/diarrhea/FD criteria.
- Supragastric belching before reflux does **not** respond to PPI; after a reflux episode it may.
- Baclofen not advocated for supragastric belching alone; may be considered for gastric belching due to GERD (reduces LES relaxations).
- SIBO testing **not** advocated routinely for bloating/distention unless clear risk factors or severe symptoms (chronic watery diarrhea, malnutrition/weight loss, small-bowel dysmotility diseases — cystic fibrosis, Parkinson, scleroderma, diabetes).
- Fructose intolerance (60%) more common than lactose (51%) across DGBIs.
- Bloating/fullness can be a presenting symptom of **ovarian cancer** (highest risk women ≥50) — alarm-feature imaging caveat.
