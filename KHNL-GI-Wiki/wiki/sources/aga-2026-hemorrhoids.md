## Summary
American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) Expert Review framing hemorrhoid diagnosis and treatment as core gastroenterology practice. Diagnosis is clinical (history + exam + anoscopy in every new patient before treatment). Conservative care (fiber, avoid straining/prolonged toilet time, sitz baths) is first-line; topical agents have limited evidence and topical steroids should be capped at 2 weeks. Office-based rubber band ligation (RBL) and infrared coagulation (IRC) are both safe and effective for grades 1–3 and should precede surgery; RBL has greater durability for prolapse and recurrent bleeding. The update gives specific guidance for special populations — active inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (defer treatment until remission), pregnancy (conservative first), and cirrhosis (distinguish hemorrhoids from rectal varices; IRC preferred over banding when significant coagulopathy is present). Acute thrombosed hemorrhoids are best treated with incision and drainage; grade 4 requires surgical hemorrhoidectomy.

## Methods and Grading

- Expert review commissioned and approved by the AGA Institute Clinical Practice Updates Committee (CPUC) and Governing Board; internal CPUC peer review + external peer review by the journal.
- Best Practice Advice (BPA) statements drawn from a review of the published literature and from expert opinion.
- **No systematic reviews were performed, so the BPA statements carry no formal rating of evidence quality or of the strength of the considerations presented — every statement below is ungraded.**
- The document numbers its own statements **BPA 1–11**; the numbering below is the document's, not the wiki's.

## Key Findings / Claims — Best Practice Advice (verbatim)
1. **BPA 1:** The diagnosis and treatment of hemorrhoids is within the purview of the gastroenterologist. The diagnosis and grading of hemorrhoids is easily made by taking a history from the patient and examining the patient. Symptoms caused by hemorrhoids include bleeding, itching, discomfort, and/or prolapse. Hemorrhoids only cause significant pain when acutely thrombosed. Sharp pain on defecation is most likely anal fissure.
2. **BPA 2:** Dietary and lifestyle modifications, including increasing fiber intake and avoiding straining or prolonged time on the toilet, are reasonable first-line therapies for symptomatic hemorrhoids. The use of sitz baths for symptom improvement in symptomatic hemorrhoids is often advised, but scientific data is limited.
3. **BPA 3:** Topical treatments, including anesthetics, astringents (witch hazel), corticosteroids, and vasoactive agents, can be considered for treatment of symptomatic hemorrhoids, but there is little data to support efficacy. Topical steroids should not be used for more than 2 weeks at a time.
4. **BPA 4:** Anoscopy should be performed, whenever possible, on every new patient with suspected hemorrhoids, prior to treatment, to ensure accurate diagnosis.
5. **BPA 5:** Both hemorrhoid banding and infrared coagulation are safe, effective, and easy to perform in the office setting. Infrared coagulation and rubber band ligation have similar benefits in the short term. Rubber band ligation has longer-term benefits for treatment of prolapsing hemorrhoids and recurrent bleeding. Hemorrhoid banding or infrared coagulation should be employed prior to surgical hemorrhoidectomy for grades 1 to 3 hemorrhoids.
6. **BPA 6:** As part of informed consent for hemorrhoid therapies, the patient must be made aware of the small possibility of pelvic sepsis as a complication. Patients should be counseled about the risk and instructed to present to the emergency department immediately for evaluation, if indicated.
7. **BPA 7:** In patients with active Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, hemorrhoid disease management should be delayed until complete remission is achieved.
8. **BPA 8:** Hemorrhoids occur in up to two-thirds of women during pregnancy. Treatment should generally involve conservative management, including fiber, treatment of constipation, and topical ointments. If symptoms persist postpartum, or if a woman is planning further pregnancies, standard treatment such as banding or infrared coagulation can be considered.
9. **BPA 9:** Acute thrombosed hemorrhoids are often extremely painful. They are best treated surgically with incision and drainage.
10. **BPA 10:** Consultation with a surgeon should be offered to patients with grade 3 internal hemorrhoids who fail banding procedures or have associated external hemorrhoids. Large skin tags can be removed without a hemorrhoidectomy if they are not associated with significant hemorrhoids. Grade 4 internal hemorrhoids require surgical hemorrhoidectomy.
11. **BPA 11:** Patients with cirrhosis and hemorrhoids should be carefully examined so as not to confuse hemorrhoids with rectal varices. Hemorrhoids in patients with cirrhosis can be treated with banding or infrared coagulation. In patients with significant coagulopathy, infrared coagulation is preferred to banding. Concomitant portal hypertension should not alter this approach. For most clinicians, significant coagulopathy means a platelet count of less than 50,000 per microliter or international normalized ratio greater than 2.0.

## Supporting Detail (condensed)

**Grading (BPA 1).** Internal hemorrhoids graded by **degree of prolapse** — Goligher's classification, the document's Table 1. Graded mostly **from the history**; exam can confirm, but a good history classifies the grade. **No standard classification exists for external hemorrhoids**; description is frequently subjective. Grade-by-grade criteria: see the Goligher table on [[hemorrhoids]].

**Symptom attribution (BPA 1).**
- Bleeding more likely internal; itching more likely external.
- Significant pain only when acutely thrombosed. Sharp pain on defecation, or sharp pain on digital rectal examination (DRE) → most likely [[anal-fissure|anal fissure]]; fissure and hemorrhoid disease coexist in **up to 20%** — treat the fissure first.
- Redundant perianal skin tags = vestiges of prior thrombosed hemorrhoids; harmless, best left alone.
- Distinguish prolapsing hemorrhoids (**radial** folds, engorged vessels) from [[rectal-prolapse|rectal prolapse]] (intussusception through the anal canal, **circular** folds of pink rectal mucosa).

**Diet and lifestyle (BPA 2).** Targets from the cited advanced-hemorrhoid study: limit time on the toilet to **3 minutes**, defecate **once daily**, avoid straining, consume **at least 20–30 g fiber/day**. Fiber evidence = 2 systematic reviews of 7 trials, moderate evidence for improved overall symptoms and bleeding. Sitz baths are not addressed in several major colorectal surgery society guidelines; a review of 36 articles found little evidence for symptom improvement. Bidets may limit pruritus by improving hygiene and avoiding repeated friction from toilet paper.

**Topicals (BPA 3).** Over-the-counter agents fall into anesthetics, astringents/protectants, corticosteroids, vasoactive agents; efficacy not well studied.
- Burning and soreness → lidocaine, pramoxine, or benzocaine ointment.
- Inflammation and swelling with intense itching → **brief** course of hydrocortisone cream or suppository.
- Minor itching and irritation → witch hazel.
- Some Preparation H products contain phenylephrine, a vasoconstrictor that temporarily shrinks vessels and reduces swelling or bleeding.
- Zinc oxide, mineral oil, petrolatum = protective barrier.
- **Why the 2-week cap:** prolonged topical steroid use thins the skin and makes it more sensitive.

**Anoscopy (BPA 4).** Superior to flexible endoscopy for the anal canal and the preferred method to examine it.
- Left lateral decubitus, after the DRE, no sedation; easy to learn and perform.
- **Beveled, disposable, slotted scopes with a built-in light are best.** A beveled scope can be rotated without risk of trauma to the hemorrhoid column; a **slotted scope cannot be rotated** inside the anal canal without damaging the column.
- Tense or nervous patient → ask the patient to bear down during insertion to relax the pelvic floor.
- Not to be confused with **high-resolution anoscopy**, which uses a colposcope to detect anal intraepithelial neoplasia or early anal cancer.
- Full [[colonoscopy|colonoscopy]] should be considered in patients with rectal bleeding, to rule out concomitant or more serious disease.

**Rubber band ligation — technique and yield (BPA 5).**
- Band placed **above or proximal to the dentate line**, so it is well tolerated; ligated tissue becomes ischemic and necroses, leaving an ulcer that scars and tacks mucosa to the rectal wall. Two aims: prevent mucosal prolapse, and reduce blood flow so the hemorrhoid shrinks.
- **Most effective office-based procedure — success 66%–94%** (success = resolution of symptoms and successful treatment of prolapse).
- **Efficacy varies by grade:** a Cochrane review found RBL **equally effective as surgical excision for grade 2**, but **not as effective as surgical excision for grade 3**.
- Complications in **2%** — urinary retention, bleeding, secondary fissure formation; also pain, vasovagal symptoms, band slippage, priapism, thrombosed hemorrhoids. A prospective series of 512 patients had complications in **7.2%**, of which 4.7% were minor; significant bleeding, severe pain, or perianal fistula in **2.5%**.
- **Band deployed too close to the dentate line causes pain → remove it immediately** by rolling it off the hemorrhoid with a finger or massaging the hemorrhoid in a circular fashion.

**Infrared coagulation — technique and limits (BPA 5).**
- Heat energy induces coagulation and fibrosis in the submucosal layer around the origin/pedicle, reducing blood supply and shrinking the hemorrhoid; performed under direct vision through an anoscope.
- **Dose:** 1- to 1.5-second bursts applied **just proximal to the visible internal hemorrhoid column, in 5 or 6 spots**, repeated for each column, producing a visible white spot. Treatment may need repeating in a few weeks for complete control.
- **Pain or burning during the procedure signals a possible transmural burn → reduce the applied energy.** Risks are pain, burning, transmural burn, ulceration.
- **Works particularly well for grades 1 and 2, and for many grade 3. Not suitable for large, prolapsing hemorrhoids.**
- **IRC is favored over RBL when bleeding risk is a concern — patients on anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents** (risk of bleeding when the band falls off); also preferred in pregnancy, and in young patients with significant irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) symptoms, who tend to be more sensitive to banding (little supporting literature).
- Post-procedure discomfort after either procedure: over-the-counter analgesia (acetaminophen or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs).
- **Sclerotherapy has been largely abandoned** because of the potential for complications such as a misplaced sclerosing injection, and reduced efficacy compared with safer modalities.

**Pelvic sepsis (BPA 6).** Rare but potentially life-threatening; can follow hemorrhoid surgery and office-based procedures such as RBL. Other infectious complications of RBL: liver abscess, Fournier's gangrene, tetanus, bacterial endocarditis.
- **Window: any time between 3 and 10 days after banding.**
- **Counsel every patient to return to the emergency department immediately for: severe pain, urinary retention, fever, chills, or significant bleeding.** Most frequent presenting symptoms are severe pain and urinary retention; may also present with post-procedural pelvic pain, urinary dysfunction, worsening perineal swelling and induration, muscle aches, or altered mental status. Give written instructions.
- Diagnosis requires immediate and aggressive intervention, which may or may not require surgery.
- Aggressive thermal energy with IRC can cause a transmural burn that results in an abscess.

**Inflammatory bowel disease (BPA 7).** Literature on safety and efficacy of hemorrhoid treatment in IBD is very limited.
- **Consultation with an IBD expert and with a surgeon is frequently required before hemorrhoid treatment.**
- Higher complication risk after surgical hemorrhoidectomy in IBD, though lower in more recent studies, possibly reflecting biologics. One retrospective series of 36 excisional hemorrhoidectomies in Crohn's disease in remission: 4 complications over mean 31-month follow-up (anal stricture, perianal abscess, nonhealing wound, hemorrhoid recurrence).
- Conservative management failing → surgical options only in highly selective cases.
- **Anal skin tags** are frequent after long-standing external hemorrhoid disease and also in IBD, particularly [[crohns-disease|Crohn's disease]] — **surgery is not advised** in either situation.
- Young patient with abdominal pain and/or diarrhea presenting with anal skin tags → work up to exclude Crohn's disease. Thorough anoscopic exam, sometimes examination under anesthesia, to exclude active disease and give an accurate diagnosis.

**Pregnancy (BPA 8).** Hemorrhoids usually develop in the **third trimester**, from the enlarging uterus raising intra-abdominal pressure with venous stasis and engorgement of the inferior mesenteric veins; extrinsic rectal compression also drives straining, prolonged defecation time, and constipation. Prolonged pushing, especially in the second stage of labor, induces or aggravates hemorrhoids and prolapse.
- Primary treatment is topical, including hydrocortisone suppositories for inflammation, bleeding, and/or pain, **with banding or IRC delayed until postpartum**.
- Systemic absorption from these ointments or creams is minimal, so they are considered **safe in pregnancy**.
- **If a procedure cannot wait until after delivery, IRC is preferred to banding** (less post-procedural discomfort).
- **Involve the obstetrician in treatment decisions.** Most recommendations here rest on expert opinion.

**Thrombosed external hemorrhoids (BPA 9).** Thrombus in the external hemorrhoid causes significant swelling and pain; patients report a mass or nodule, which can bleed. Thorough evaluation is important to make the diagnosis.
- **Early presentation = within 72 hours**; early incision and drainage (I&D) gives the most effective and rapid symptom relief compared with conservative measures.
- **"De-roof" the cavity** so it does not reform with the incision closing over.
- **I&D can be done by a gastroenterologist in the office or in the emergency room. Antibiotics are not required afterward.**
- Distinguish I&D from **thrombectomy** — removal of the entire thrombosed portion of the hemorrhoid, usually with sutures to close, usually performed by surgeons. Immediate-relief outcomes are very similar.
- Most thrombosed external hemorrhoids resolve without any surgical treatment; excision offers early relief. **If excision is offered, complete excision and evacuation of the clot should be performed.**

**Surgical referral and operative options (BPA 10).** Seek surgical opinion for patients who remain symptomatic despite medical therapy. Most symptomatic grade 1, grade 2, and many grade 3 internal hemorrhoids can be treated with office-based procedures. Refer to surgery: **grades 1–3 who cannot tolerate or fail office-based procedures; concomitant external hemorrhoids; grade 3 not responding to banding; all grade 4.**
- **Surgical excision remains the definitive procedure** — the entire internal and external hemorrhoid complex is dissected off the internal anal sphincter and excised. Incision may be left open or closed with suture; the **closed approach has decreased risk of postoperative bleeding and faster wound healing**. Overall complication rate **1%–2%** (urinary retention, bleeding, infection, fistula formation, rare pelvic sepsis).
- **Stapled hemorrhoidopexy** — circular stapler plicates the mucosa via a mucosal anastomosis; **less postoperative pain than excisional hemorrhoidectomy, but increased recurrence on long-term follow-up** and risk of significant complications (rectovaginal fistula, rectal perforation, bleeding, staple-line stricture). **Has fallen out of favor.**
- **Doppler-guided hemorrhoidectomy** — Doppler probe identifies and ligates the hemorrhoid artery without excision; mucosal pexy may be added for symptomatic prolapse. No excision → less postoperative pain, well tolerated, minimal complications.
- Patients should be counseled on bleeding, recurrence, and pelvic sepsis.

**Cirrhosis and rectal varices (BPA 11).** Hemorrhoid disease is commonly found in the cirrhotic patient.
- **Rectal varices** are portosystemic collaterals of [[portal-hypertension|portal hypertension]], present in **30%** of cirrhotic patients with portal hypertension.
- **How they differ:** rectal varices sit in the **mid and more proximal rectum**; hemorrhoids are usually **distal and cross the dentate line**.
- Bleeding from rectal varices is uncommon but can be **life-threatening**. Endoscopic cyanoacrylate injection or coils frequently control bleeding, but **embolism risk and rebleeding rates remain high**.
- **Managing the underlying portal hypertension is essential** — transhepatic portosystemic shunting, or alternatively balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration (BRTO) by interventional radiology.
- **Significant coagulopathy, for most clinicians = platelets <50,000/µL or international normalized ratio (INR) >2.0** → choose IRC over banding. Concomitant portal hypertension by itself does not change the approach.

- Primary update to [[hemorrhoids]] — Goligher grading criteria (Table 1, which lives on that page), modality selection by grade and by bleeding risk, IRC technique and limits, pelvic-sepsis counseling window, and special-population guidance (pregnancy, active IBD, cirrhosis with the rectal-varices distinction).
- Corroborates American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2021 / American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) 2010 on conservative first-line therapy, RBL as the preferred office procedure, early drainage of thrombosed hemorrhoids, and grade 4 → hemorrhoidectomy.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

**Versus [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders|ACG 2021]] (same tier; AGA 2026 is newer by publication date and wins the wiki page, with the difference surfaced here and on [[hemorrhoids]]):**

| Point | AGA 2026 (ungraded BPA) | ACG 2021 (GRADE) |
|---|---|---|
| Grades eligible for office procedures | Banding or IRC for **grades 1–3**, and **required before** surgical hemorrhoidectomy in grades 1–3 (BPA 5) | Graded recommendation covers **grade 1–2** failing medical therapy (Strong/Moderate); grade 3 is addressed separately by Doppler-guided artery ligation (Conditional/Very low) |
| Sclerotherapy | **Largely abandoned** — misplaced sclerosing injection, reduced efficacy vs safer modalities | Listed as an alternative office procedure within the Strong/Moderate recommendation; 75%–90% success in first- to third-degree hemorrhoids |
| Patient on anticoagulants/antiplatelets | **IRC** is the favored modality (bleeding risk when the band falls off) | Sclerotherapy named as effective in acute bleeding on anticoagulants or immunosuppression; banding to be used with caution |
| Acutely thrombosed external hemorrhoid | **I&D** (with de-roofing); early = **within 72 hours** | **Either** surgical excision **or** incision and evacuation of the thrombus; early = **within the first 4 days** (Strong/Low) |

**Open questions acknowledged by the document itself:**
- Sitz baths — scientific data limited; a review of 36 articles found little evidence for symptom improvement, and the authors call for more research.
- Topical agents — efficacy "has not been well-studied"; the advice is permissive, not an endorsement.
- Preferring IRC in pregnancy and in young patients with significant IBS symptoms rests on "very little evidence in the literature"; further studies are needed. Most pregnancy treatment recommendations are based on expert opinion.
- Safety and efficacy of hemorrhoid treatment in IBD rests on small series and expert opinion.
- BPA 10 permits removing "large" skin tags without hemorrhoidectomy but gives no size threshold.
- Because no systematic review was performed, none of the 11 statements carries a strength or evidence rating; where AGA 2026 and ACG 2021 agree, ACG's GRADE rating is the only formal evidence signal available.
