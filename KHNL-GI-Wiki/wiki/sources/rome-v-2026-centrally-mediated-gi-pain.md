## Summary

The Rome V chapter for the **D category — centrally mediated disorders of gastrointestinal (GI) pain** — the group whose pain is driven predominantly by the central nervous system (CNS) rather than by heightened peripheral afferent excitability. It covers three disorders: **D1 centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome (CAPS)**, **D2 abdominal migraine** (newly recognised in adults), and **D3 narcotic bowel syndrome (NBS) / opioid-induced GI hyperalgesia**. Each gets printed diagnostic criteria, the committee's justification for what changed, pathophysiology, clinical evaluation, and treatment.

**CAPS is the chapter's main structural change.** Rome IV required "no or only occasional relationship of pain with physiological events," which was read strictly as *no* association — and the Rome Foundation Global Epidemiology Study found a prevalence of only **0.02%** in 54,127 people across 26 countries, in women only. Yet specialty practices see a large group with severe, nearly constant pain that *is* modified by eating, defecation or menses and that meets neither CAPS nor irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) criteria. Rome V therefore splits CAPS into **category A** (no association with physiological events) and **category B** (variable association), raising the identified prevalence to **0.11%** — five times the Rome IV figure. Separately, "continuous or nearly continuous" is tightened to **"continuous abdominal pain"** (in the awake state); the committee judged that dropping "nearly" removes ambiguity and preserves specificity against IBS.

**Abdominal migraine is a new adult diagnosis.** It was described in adults more than a century ago, then became a pediatric entity from Rome II onward; the committee restored it for adults because it is recognised in practice, is likely underrecognised for want of a diagnostic standard, and is phenotypically distinct from IBS (no constipation or change in bowel habit). The criteria require **paroxysmal, stereotypical episodes of intense abdominal pain lasting 1 hour or more up to several days**, separated by **weeks or months**, occurring **≥3 times per year and 2 times in the previous 6 months**, incapacitating, with **≥2 of 6** associated features. Abdominal migraine, cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS) and migraine headache are drawn as three overlapping episodic syndromes (Figure 4); abdominal migraine is distinguished from CVS because **pain, not vomiting, is dominant and the vomiting is not repetitive**.

**NBS criteria are unchanged in substance from Rome IV** but are printed here in full, which the Rome V process overview does not do. The chapter also supplies the detail the wiki previously lacked on the only published detoxification protocol and its outcomes: inpatient detoxification over roughly 7 days succeeded in **89.7%** of 39 patients, **60%** were responders by predefined criteria, **11%** reported worse pain afterward, and **43.2%** had restarted opioids by 3 months — but those still off opioids at 3 months reported significantly lower pain scores. Treatment throughout the chapter is **ungraded narrative**: there are no numbered recommendations and no GRADE statements anywhere in this document.

## Key Findings / Claims

### D1 — Centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome (criteria as printed)

Must include **all** of the following; criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis:

1. Continuous abdominal pain
2. Relationship of pain with physiological events — **category A:** no association, **or category B:** variable association
3. Pain limits some aspect of daily functioning
4. Pain is not factitious
5. Another GI or non-GI disorder does not explain the continuous abdominal pain

Footnotes as printed: physiological events may include **eating, defecation, menstruation, positioning, or other visceral or somatic activities**; limitations of daily functioning include **work, intimacy, social and leisure, family life, and caregiving for self or others**; CAPS is typically associated with psychosocial comorbidities, but **no specific psychosocial profile characterizes the diagnosis**.

- "Continuous" refers to pain **in the awake state**.
- Pain is typically constant and may wax and wane; colicky, cramping, burning, dull or aching, and can be diffuse. The **constant** nature is what separates it from IBS, functional dyspepsia and biliary pain — though CAPS may overlap with them.
- Prevalence: **0.02%** (95% CI 0.01–0.03) for category A; **0.11%** for category B. Up to approximately **20%** of DGBI patients in some specialty series have chronic abdominal pain. US householder survey: **1.52** physician visits per year for abdominal pain, **5.66** for extra-GI symptoms.
- Up to **two-thirds** of patients with CAPS have been or are being treated with opioids, which do not improve pain control, function or quality of life in chronic visceral pain.

### D1 — physical examination signs the chapter names

- **No dominant autonomic arousal** (no tachycardia, raised blood pressure or diaphoresis) during pain.
- **Multiple surgical scars without clearly understood indications.**
- **Closed eyes sign** — the patient winces with eyes closed on abdominal palpation.
- **Stethoscope sign** — palpating with a stethoscope reduces the behavioural response seen on manual palpation.
- **Carnett's test** separates visceral from abdominal wall pain: identify the point of maximal tenderness, then have the patient tense the abdominal wall by raising head and trunk or legs off the table; **identical or increased tenderness = positive = abdominal wall pain**.
- Examination should include **digital rectal examination and visual inspection of the perineum**.
- **Extensive and repeated testing to exclude alternative diagnoses should not be undertaken routinely.** Minimal workup = routine labs to exclude inflammation and GI blood loss (anemia, fecal blood). Alarm features → investigate for causes other than CAPS.

### D1 — pharmacotherapy (ungraded, with doses as printed)

- **Category A** (no association with physiological events) is expected to respond to **centrally acting** neuromodulators; **category B** may respond to both central and peripheral agents (antispasmodics, laxatives). Gut-directed drugs (probiotics, antibiotics, secretagogues, bile acid sequestrants, serotonin antagonists) **will likely not be effective for category A** and may have a partial effect in category B.
- **Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) — first line**, most extensively studied. Moderate dosages **10–25 mg building up to 75 or 100 mg** depending on the agent. **Secondary amines (desipramine, nortriptyline) are favoured over tertiary amines (amitriptyline, imipramine)** for fewer anticholinergic and antihistaminic effects.
- **Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs)** may also be first line — fewer adverse effects than TCAs and usable in patients with constipation. **Duloxetine 30–60 mg/d or 90 mg/d**; **venlafaxine requires >150 mg/d** to achieve noradrenergic activation. Nausea in approximately **15%–20%**, minimised by taking with meals.
- **Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are typically not helpful for CAPS**, but can improve anxiety-related symptoms to augment low-dose TCA or SNRI, and can help constipation.
- **Atypical antipsychotics — second-line augmenting agents.** **Quetiapine 25–100 mg once daily at bedtime** (vs 400–800 mg for psychiatric indications) augments the pain benefit of a TCA or SNRI; in one study, patients with painful IBS or CAPS unresponsive to 4 weeks of TCA or SNRI showed up to **50% benefit**. **Olanzapine dose is approximately 10% of quetiapine — 2.5–10 mg once daily at bedtime.** Risk of metabolic and/or motor adverse effects.
- **Additional central neuromodulators:** gabapentin, pregabalin and mirogabalin (α2δ ligands); **buspirone 10–20 mg twice daily** or **tandospirone 10–20 mg three times daily** (5-HT1A partial agonists used to augment antidepressants); **mirtazapine 7.5–30 mg at night** (sedative; weight gain is frequent).
- **Adverse effects are usually immediate over the first several days and diminish over a week or so; the analgesic benefit may take several weeks.**
- **Most non-opioid analgesics (acetaminophen, aspirin, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) offer little benefit.** **Narcotic analgesics must be avoided in CAPS** — risk of addiction and of developing NBS.
- **Augmentation** = add a second centrally acting agent, a peripherally acting agent, or a brain–gut behavioural treatment, with the antidepressant dose then possibly lowered. Watch for **serotonin syndrome**.
- **Relapse prevention:** continuing a neuromodulator beyond clinical benefit reduces relapse/recurrence; continuing or starting brain–gut therapies does the same.

### D1 — non-pharmacologic treatment

- Brain–gut behavioural therapies: **cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)**, **psychodynamic interpersonal therapy** (particularly helpful where there is a history of sexual abuse), **gut-focused hypnotherapy**, **mindfulness-based stress reduction** and acceptance-based approaches.
- **Neurolytic celiac plexus blockade should be abandoned in benign diseases.**
- **Spinal cord stimulation** may have a role after failed medical therapy, but **a specific clinical recommendation cannot be made**.
- Transcranial direct current stimulation (1–2 mA) can be tried — inexpensive, no major adverse effects. Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation results are indeterminate.
- Eleven physician behaviours are listed for the relationship itself: listen actively, accept CAPS as a true disorder, offer empathy, use a questioning style and non-verbal messages, validate, set realistic treatment goals, educate, reassure, negotiate, maintain boundaries, be aware of time constraints.
- General treatment principles: **base treatment on symptom severity and degree of disability**; know when to refer to a mental health professional; know when to refer to a multidisciplinary DGBI or pain centre; be aware of sociocultural bias; **recommend face-to-face rather than virtual appointments**.

### D2 — Abdominal migraine (criteria as printed)

Must include **all** of the following; criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at 6 months before diagnosis:

1. Paroxysmal, stereotypical episodes of intense abdominal pain lasting **1 hour or more, up to several days**
2. The episodes are separated by **weeks or months**
3. The episodes occur **at least 3 times per year and 2 times in the previous 6 months**
4. The pain is **incapacitating and interferes with everyday activities**
5. The pain is associated with **2 or more** of: history of migraine headaches · family history of migraine headaches · anorexia, nausea or vomiting with episodes · photophobia · pallor · aura or prodrome preceding the full episode
6. After appropriate evaluation, no other medical condition can fully explain the symptoms

Printed footnote: abdominal migraine is distinguished from CVS because **the pain, not the vomiting, is dominant, and the vomiting is not repetitive**.

- Clinical detail: episodes last **2 to 72 hours, averaging approximately 17 hours**. Pain is usually **noncolicky**, dull, periumbilical, epigastric, midline or hard to localise, and **nonradiating**. **Not related to eating or defecation.**
- **Seventy percent of patients have a history of migraine headaches.** A history of symptom relief with antimigraine therapy further supports the diagnosis.
- Pediatric data: first-degree relative with migraine headache in **34%–90%**; personal history of migraine in **24%–57%**. Pediatric prevalence **1.5%–23%** depending on criteria (the 23% figure used the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 2nd edition). **No adult epidemiologic data existed before the Rome V Global Epidemiology and Validation Study.**
- Workup: screening labs = **complete blood count, complete metabolic profile, C-reactive protein, fecal calprotectin**. **Red flags requiring further evaluation: weight loss, abnormal screening labs, abnormal physical examination, blood in the stool, protracted vomiting.** Cross-sectional imaging (ultrasound, computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging) is usually performed and **is recommended if not already done** — but **repeat evaluation is not recommended unless the clinical features change**.
- Treatment is aimed at **aborting acute episodes and prophylaxis**. Acute: intravenous hydration, antiemetics, **triptans** (empiric benefit), intravenous paracetamol, nefopam, short-term opioids, histamine H1 receptor antagonists. Prophylaxis: **β-blockers (propranolol), calcium channel blockers (flunarizine), serotonin antagonists (cyproheptadine, pizotifen), γ-aminobutyric acid agonists (valproate)** — all borrowed from cephalic migraine; plus neuromodulators (**TCA — desipramine or amitriptyline; SNRI — duloxetine**) and brain–gut therapies including CBT. One double-blind controlled study of **pizotifen in children** showed significant improvement in pain severity and days of pain.
- Triggers: psychological stress, lack of sleep, infection, possibly histamine/tyramine-containing foods.

### D3 — Narcotic bowel syndrome / opioid-induced GI hyperalgesia (criteria as printed)

Must include **all** of the following; criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis:

1. **Chronic or frequently recurring abdominal pain that is treated with narcotics**
2. The nature and intensity of the pain are **not explained by a current or previous GI diagnosis**
3. **Two or more** of:
   - a. The pain **worsens or incompletely resolves with continued or escalating dosages** of narcotics
   - b. **Marked worsening of pain when the narcotic dose wanes and improvement when narcotics are reinstituted** ("soar and crash")
   - c. **Progression of the frequency, duration, and intensity** of pain episodes

Printed footnotes: **pain must occur most days**; a patient may have a structural diagnosis (e.g. inflammatory bowel disease, chronic pancreatitis) provided **the character or activity of that disease is not sufficient to explain the pain**.

- Epidemiology: community prevalence **0.17%**, rising to **4%, 3% or 6%** of those prescribed regular opioids across three studies. **Female preponderance; incidence peaks in the fifth decade**; associated with comorbid chronic pain syndrome and higher educational attainment.
- NBS is **distinct from opioid-induced bowel dysfunction / opioid-induced constipation** — NBS is CNS pain-pathway hyperalgesia; opioid-induced bowel dysfunction is a dose-related peripheral µ-receptor motility and sphincter problem (nausea, vomiting, gastroparesis, ileus, pseudo-obstruction, constipation).
- Drugs most often implicated in opioid-induced hyperalgesia (not confirmed for NBS) are the **fast-acting opioids — fentanyl, remifentanil, sufentanil** — though hyperalgesia also follows morphine and oxycodone. **Methadone (an N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor agonist effect) might prevent opioid-induced hyperalgesia and has been used in treatment; buprenorphine may be antihyperalgesic** and may be less prone to produce NBS, with patch formulations an advantage where GI function is disturbed.
- Imaging in NBS may show **colonic fecal retention and/or signs of "ileus" or "incomplete bowel obstruction"**; cross-sectional imaging is often done to exclude obstruction, pancreatitis, inflammatory or ischemic bowel.
- **A subset reporting abdominal pain on opioids has an opioid use disorder instead — refer to a substance abuse program and do not treat as NBS.** The **Current Opioid Misuse Measure** helps gauge severity of misuse and likelihood of successful detoxification; a **low score predicted responder status and remaining off opioids**.
- Detoxification outcomes (39 inpatients, approximately 7 days): **89.7% successfully detoxified; 60% responders; 11% worse pain; 43.2% had restarted opioids at 3 months; more than two-thirds had restarted by end of follow-up.** Those still off opioids at 3 months reported **significantly lower abdominal pain scores**.
- **Low-dose ketamine** (N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonist) is an effective opioid substitute in chronic pain with positive effects in opioid-induced hyperalgesia, **but has not been systematically studied in NBS**.

### Table 1 — the published opioid detoxification protocol

Adapted from Drossman et al with permission; reproduced here as the chapter prints it.

| Component | What it specifies |
|---|---|
| Before detoxification | Effective communication about pain management; **add a TCA or SNRI before detoxification**; add augmenting agents as needed; clonidine to prevent withdrawal; mirtazapine for nausea; quetiapine for sleep and pain; benzodiazepine for anxiolysis; psychological treatment |
| Narcotic withdrawal | Convert the **total daily dosage of all opioids to a morphine equivalent**; give that daily dose as a **continuous intravenous drip on day 1**, then reduce by a **fixed, predetermined proportion of 10%–33% each day until off narcotics (usually 4–11 days)** |
| Clonidine | **Start at the point of 50% opioid withdrawal; 0.1 mg twice or three times daily, titrate up to 0.6 mg/d as needed; taper off at the end of detoxification** |
| Dose discipline | By prior agreement, **once detoxification starts the narcotic dosing does not change** — augmenting agents may be used instead |
| Constipation | Polyethylene glycol, a chloride channel activator, or a peripherally acting µ-opioid receptor antagonist as needed |
| Anxiety | Benzodiazepine intravenously or by mouth as needed on day 1, adjusted as needed; **an atypical antipsychotic (quetiapine or aripiprazole) may be substituted** |
| Psychological treatment | Mental health provider available during detoxification; augmentation with hypnosis, CBT or medication |

### Pathophysiology (summarised)

- CAPS sits at the far end of the DGBI pain-severity spectrum from IBS: strong central disinhibition, weak afferent excitation, **nociplastic pain** with disinhibited signals, reorganised central pathways, and neuroinflammation. The International Association for the Study of Pain now classifies these as **chronic primary pain**.
- Mechanisms by level: spinal dorsal horn sensitization and wind-up → brainstem loss of descending inhibitory feedback → brain reorganisation, atrophy and neurotransmitter switching; microglia modulate the relevant neuroplasticity.
- NBS: the favoured mechanism is **glial cell activation in the spinal dorsal horn** (astrocytes and microglia releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, nitric oxide and excitatory amino acids), plus **bimodal opioid modulation** (Gi/Go-coupled inhibitory receptor → analgesia; Gs-coupled excitatory receptor → hyperalgesia, depending on opioid concentration and treatment duration), plus pronociceptive descending tracts in the dorsolateral funiculus.

### Psychiatric comorbidity in CAPS

- **Depression and anxiety independently predict pain reporting, particularly in women**, and are associated with greater pain severity and disability. Anxiety is present in up to **40%** of patients with DGBI.
- **Chronic abdominal pain may be an independent risk factor for suicidal ideation, particularly among women**; risk rises with unemployment, poor sleep, a feeling of helplessness, and illicit drug use for pain control.
- Post-traumatic stress disorder is increasingly recognised as a mechanism by which pain is exacerbated and is **critical to probe during the interview**; a history of abuse predicts poorer health status, greater health care use, greater disability and lower quality of life.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **TCA ceiling for CAPS vs the DGBI-wide neuromodulator table.** [[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]] caps TCAs at **30–50 mg at night** across painful DGBI; this chapter gives **10–25 mg building up to 75 or 100 mg** for CAPS and adds that secondary amines "may also be used in higher dosages to manage the pain." Both are tier-1; **Rome V 2026 is the newer publication and governs the CAPS page**, while the AGA 2021 table continues to govern the general DGBI page. Surfaced on [[centrally-mediated-abdominal-pain-syndrome]].
- **NBS opioid-exposure threshold.** [[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]] describes NBS with **>100 mg morphine equivalent per day for >3 months**; the Rome V criteria printed here contain **no dose or duration threshold at all** — only "treated with narcotics," pain on most days, 3-month symptom duration and 6-month onset. These are different instruments rather than contradictory claims (descriptive clinical picture vs diagnostic criteria), and the page now carries both, labelled.
- **Prevalence of NBS among opioid users** is given as 4%, 3% and 6% from three separate studies without the chapter reconciling them.
- **Spinal cord stimulation** — the chapter explicitly declines to make a recommendation.
- **Treatment in this chapter is entirely ungraded.** There are no numbered recommendations, no GRADE ratings and no strength-of-evidence labels anywhere in the document; nothing on the entity pages may be presented as graded.
