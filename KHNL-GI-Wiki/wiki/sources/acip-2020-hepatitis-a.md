## Summary
- Supplants and summarizes all prior ACIP hepatitis A (HepA) recommendations (Summary, p. 1; Methods, p. 2).
- Hepatitis A virus (HAV): fecal-oral transmission (person-to-person, contaminated food/water); acute, self-limited, **no chronic infection**; immunoglobulin G (IgG) anti-HAV after infection is lifelong and protective; vaccine-induced IgG anti-HAV gives long-term immunity (Summary, p. 1).
- Severity rises with older age, immunocompromise, chronic liver disease, other comorbidity (Introduction, p. 1).
- Core recommendations (Summary, p. 1; BOX, p. 17): routine vaccination at 12–23 months; catch-up at 2–18 years; vaccination of adults at risk for HAV infection or for severe disease, and of any adult who requests it; guidance for travel, postexposure prophylaxis (PEP), settings serving adults, and outbreaks.
- **New in 2020** (p. 2): catch-up for all unvaccinated 2–18 years; all persons with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection aged ≥1 year; chronic liver disease list spelled out (incl. fatty liver disease, alcoholic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis, alanine aminotransferase [ALT] or aspartate aminotransferase [AST] persistently >2× the upper limit of normal); pregnant women at risk; outbreak vaccination of persons ≥1 year at risk; vaccination in settings serving adults with a high proportion of risk factors. **No longer recommended:** routine vaccination for recipients of blood products for clotting disorders (e.g., hemophilia).
- **New CDC clinical guidance** (p. 2): infants 6–11 months traveling abroad; persons aged >40 years; immunocompromising conditions; chronic liver disease with travel plans; HIV; pregnancy; PEP and outbreak vaccination.
- PEP (p. 21): one dose of single-antigen HepA vaccine, immune globulin (IG) 0.1 mL/kg, or both, as soon as possible and within 2 weeks of exposure, by age and risk group (Table 4). Twinrix is not used for PEP.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Recommendations — preexposure vaccination (Recommendations for Hepatitis A Vaccine and Immune Globulin for Preexposure Prophylaxis, pp. 17–21)
- Framing (p. 18): HepA vaccination is recommended routinely for children, persons at increased risk for infection, persons at increased risk for severe disease from HAV infection, and any person who requests vaccination. IG gives short-term protection and is recommended in certain situations (Tables 2 and 4).

**Children (p. 18)**
- **R1.** ACIP recommends HepA vaccination for **all children aged 12–23 months**.
- **R2.** ACIP recommends that **all children and adolescents aged 2–18 years** who have not previously received HepA vaccine be vaccinated (catch-up). *New recommendation.*

**Travelers — persons traveling to or working in countries with high or intermediate HAV endemicity (p. 18)**
- **R3.** All susceptible persons (unvaccinated, partially vaccinated, or never infected) traveling to or working in high/intermediate-endemicity countries (Figure 3) are at increased risk → **should be vaccinated, or receive IG if too young or vaccine is contraindicated, before departure**. Partially vaccinated travelers → a dose before travel if needed according to the vaccine schedule.
- **R4. Infants aged <6 months** → **should receive IG before travel** when protection against HAV is recommended (Tables 2 and 4):
  - travel up to 1 month → 1 dose IG 0.1 mL/kg
  - travel up to 2 months → 1 dose IG 0.2 mL/kg
  - travel ≥2 months → IG 0.2 mL/kg **repeated every 2 months** for the duration of travel or until the infant receives HepA vaccine (i.e., at age ≥6 months)
- **R5. Infants aged 6–11 months** → HepA vaccine **should be administered** when traveling outside the United States and protection is recommended (Table 4).
  - The travel dose **does not count** toward the routine 2-dose series → start the 2-dose series at age 1 year with the appropriate dose and schedule.
  - *CDC clinical guidance:* IG (GamaSTAN) cannot be given simultaneously with measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine — antibody-containing products can inhibit the measles and rubella response for up to 6 months. MMR is recommended for all infants 6–11 months traveling internationally, and measles in infancy is more severe than HAV infection in infancy → **prioritize measles prevention; do not give IG**. HepA vaccine (**off-label indication**) and MMR can be given simultaneously at 6–11 months.
- **R6. Healthy persons aged 12 months through 40 years** who have not received HepA vaccine and plan travel to a high/intermediate-endemicity area → **single dose of HepA vaccine as soon as travel is considered**, then complete the series with the appropriate dose and schedule (Table 4).
- **R7. Persons aged >40 years, persons with immunocompromising conditions, and persons with chronic liver disease** planning such travel → **single dose of HepA vaccine as soon as travel is considered**.
  - Traveling in **<2 weeks** → give the initial vaccine dose and **may simultaneously give IG** at a different anatomic site (e.g., separate limbs).
  - Complete the series on the routine schedule (Table 4).
  - *CDC clinical guidance:* in addition to vaccine, **IG should be considered** before travel for persons with special risk factors or increased risk for severe disease (Table 3; Appendix A).
- **R8. Travelers for whom vaccine is contraindicated or who choose not to be vaccinated** → **should receive IG before travel** when protection is recommended: up to 1 month 0.1 mL/kg; up to 2 months 0.2 mL/kg; ≥2 months 0.2 mL/kg repeated every 2 months for the duration of travel (Tables 2 and 4).

**Other risk groups for HAV infection (p. 19)**
- **R9. Men who have sex with men (MSM):** vaccination is recommended. Providers in primary care and specialty settings where MSM receive care (e.g., sexually transmitted disease clinics) **should offer HepA vaccine to all MSM**.
- **R10. Persons who use injection or noninjection drugs** (i.e., all who use illegal drugs): vaccination is recommended. Providers **should conduct a thorough risk factor assessment** to identify persons who use or are at risk for using such drugs; settings where they receive care (e.g., opioid treatment programs providing medication-assisted treatment) **should offer HepA vaccine to all** of them.
- **R11. Occupational risk:** persons who work with HAV-infected nonhuman primates or with clinical/nonclinical material containing HAV in a research laboratory **should be vaccinated**. No other occupational group (e.g., health care providers, food handlers) has been shown to be at increased occupational risk.
- **R12. Close personal contact with an international adoptee** from a high/intermediate-endemicity country: all persons who anticipate close personal contact (e.g., household contact, caretaker, regular babysitter) **during the first 60 days after the adoptee's arrival** in the United States **should be vaccinated**. First dose **as soon as adoption is planned, ideally ≥2 weeks before arrival**.
- **R13. Persons experiencing homelessness:** all persons **aged ≥1 year** experiencing homelessness **should be routinely vaccinated**; integrate HepA vaccine into routine preventive services.

**Increased risk for severe disease (pp. 19–20)**
- **R14. Chronic liver disease** — including but not limited to hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, cirrhosis, fatty liver disease, alcoholic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis, or an ALT or AST level **persistently >2× the upper limit of normal** → **should be routinely vaccinated**. *(New per p. 2.)*
- **R15. HIV infection:** ACIP recommends **all persons with HIV infection aged ≥1 year** be routinely vaccinated with HepA vaccine. *New recommendation.*
  - *CDC clinical guidance (p. 20):*
    - **Postvaccination serologic testing for all persons with HIV ≥1 month after completing the series**; some studies show ≥6 months may be needed to seroconvert (≥10 mIU/mL).
    - **Do not delay vaccination until the CD4 count exceeds a threshold** (missed opportunities prolong HAV exposure risk).
    - Nonresponders → consider susceptible; counsel on prevention and on the need for **IG PEP for any known or likely exposure**.
    - Providers **should consider revaccination** (repeat series) for persons with HIV without an adequate response (≥10 mIU/mL) after the initial series, particularly if immune status later improves (higher CD4, lower HIV viral load). Test **≥1 month after** the repeat series; still **<10 mIU/mL → no further vaccination**; counsel on prevention incl. IG after exposure.
    - **All** vaccinated persons with HIV, regardless of serology, should be counseled that the vaccine might not give long-term protection → they might need IG after a high-risk exposure (e.g., sexual or household contact).

**Other persons recommended for vaccination (p. 20)**
- **R16. Pregnant women:** ACIP recommends that pregnant women identified to be at risk for HAV infection during pregnancy (e.g., international travelers, persons who use injection or noninjection drugs, occupational risk, anticipated close contact with an international adoptee, homelessness) or for a severe outcome (e.g., chronic liver disease, HIV infection) **should be vaccinated during pregnancy if not previously vaccinated**. *New recommendation.*
  - *CDC clinical guidance:* vaccinate pregnant women for the same indications as nonpregnant women; unvaccinated or partially vaccinated pregnant adolescents should receive catch-up vaccination; counsel at-risk pregnant women on other prevention (e.g., hand hygiene).
- **R17. Any person who requests vaccination:** disclosure of a risk factor is not necessary; ACIP recommends that any person who has not previously completed the HepA series **may** receive HepA vaccine.

**Implementation strategies for settings providing services to adults (pp. 20–21)**
- **R18.** Settings with a high proportion of persons with risk factors: health care settings focused on persons who use injection or noninjection drugs; group homes and nonresidential day care facilities for persons with developmental disabilities. Providers **may assume** unvaccinated adults **aged ≥19 years** in these settings are at risk and offer vaccination to those who have not completed it.
  - HepA vaccination **may be offered** in outreach and other settings serving persons at risk (e.g., homeless shelters, syringe services programs).
  - Providers **should consider** standing orders to identify and vaccinate adults recommended for HepA vaccination as part of routine services.
  - HepA vaccination **should be considered** for persons (e.g., residents and staff) in facilities where hygiene is difficult to maintain (e.g., group homes for persons with developmental disabilities, homeless shelters).

**Persons who are incarcerated (p. 9)** — no stand-alone ACIP recommendation; offering HepA vaccine in jails and prisons is described as an effective way to reach high-risk persons (>90% first-dose Twinrix uptake with staff partnership and education; accelerated Twinrix schedules used before release from short-term incarceration). Correctional facilities appear as outbreak settings (Table 3; outbreak CDC guidance, p. 22; Appendix B congregate-setting PEP).

**Groups no longer or not routinely recommended (Groups and Settings with Low Risk, pp. 9–10; BOX)**
- **R19. Blood products for clotting disorders** (e.g., hemophilia): **no longer** specifically recommended (first recommended 1996). Reason: secondary virus-reduction steps; >80% of US patients receive recombinant factor concentrates; source plasma now screened for HAV; risk now equal to the general population (p. 9).
- Food handlers: HepA vaccination **not specifically recommended** absent other risk factors (p. 9).
- Health care personnel: **not routinely recommended**; should adhere to standard precautions and contact precautions for incontinent patients, incl. hand hygiene (p. 10).
- Workers handling untreated sewage: **not routinely recommended** (p. 10).
- Schools: HepA vaccination is recommended for all children; multiple cases among children at a school → investigate a common source (p. 10).

### Recommendations — postexposure prophylaxis (ACIP Recommendations for Hepatitis A Vaccine and Immune Globulin for Postexposure Prophylaxis, p. 21)
- Without PEP, household secondary attack rates are 20%–50%, higher from infected young children than from adolescents/adults (p. 21).
- **R20.** Persons exposed to HAV **within the last 2 weeks** (e.g., sexual or household contact, known contaminated food source) and not previously vaccinated → **1 dose of single-antigen HepA vaccine, IG (0.1 mL/kg), or both** (both = persons aged ≥12 months who are immunocompromised or have chronic liver disease) **as soon as possible**.
  - Vaccine advantages over IG: active immunity, longer protection, ease of administration, greater acceptability and availability.
  - Efficacy of IG or vaccine given **>2 weeks after exposure has not been established**.
- **R21.** Severely immunocompromised persons — including those receiving routine IG for immunocompromising conditions — who **were vaccinated in the past** may benefit from PEP → **assess individually** for HAV risk.
- **R22.** HepA vaccine **should be given as soon as possible, within 2 weeks of exposure, to all unvaccinated persons aged ≥12 months** recently exposed. Coadministration of GamaSTAN IG (0.1 mL/kg) is recommended under certain circumstances (Table 4) and **for persons aged >40 years based on the provider's risk assessment**.
- **R23. Infants aged <12 months** → **IG 0.1 mL/kg instead of vaccine**, as soon as possible within 2 weeks of exposure. MMR not to be given <6 months after IG.
- **R24. Healthy persons aged ≥12 months** exposed within the past 2 weeks who have not completed the series → **1 dose of single-antigen HepA vaccine** as soon as possible. IG (0.1 mL/kg) **may** be added for persons **aged >40 years** depending on the provider's risk assessment (Appendix B). If the PEP dose is the first dose → second dose **6 months after the first** for long-term immunity (not needed for PEP).
- **R25. Persons aged ≥12 months who are immunocompromised or have chronic liver disease**, exposed within the past 2 weeks, not completed the series → **both IG (0.1 mL/kg) and HepA vaccine, simultaneously at different anatomic sites** (e.g., separate limbs), as soon as possible (Table 4). If first dose → second dose 6 months later for long-term immunity (not needed for PEP).
- **R26. Vaccine contraindicated** → **IG 0.1 mL/kg instead of vaccine**, as soon as possible within 2 weeks. MMR and varicella vaccines not to be given <6 months after IG.
- *CDC clinical guidance for PEP (p. 21):* in addition to vaccine, **IG should be considered** for persons with special risk factors for HAV infection or severe disease (Tables 3 and 4; Appendix B). **Twinrix is not recommended for PEP** — it contains 720 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) units of HAV antigen (half the single-antigen adult dose) and no PEP data exist.

### Recommendations — outbreaks (Vaccination During Outbreaks, p. 22)
- Outbreak control should focus on preexposure vaccination of at-risk populations plus postexposure strategies. **A single dose of HepA vaccine is adequate for outbreak control**; complete the series when feasible, but completion is **not required for PEP or outbreak response**.
- Hard-to-reach groups (persons who use drugs, homelessness) may need mobile field vaccination, stationary points of dispensary, vaccination foot teams.
- **R27.** ACIP recommends **1 dose of HepA vaccine during a hepatitis A outbreak for all unvaccinated persons aged ≥1 year at risk for HAV infection** (e.g., persons who use injection or noninjection drugs, persons experiencing homelessness, MSM) **or at risk for severe disease** (e.g., chronic liver disease, HIV infection). *(New per p. 2.)*
- *CDC clinical guidance:* in a community outbreak propagated person to person, public health officials **should consider** recommending preexposure vaccination in close congregate settings serving high-risk persons in the vicinity of the outbreak (e.g., correctional facilities, health care settings for persons who use drugs, homeless shelters, syringe services programs).

### Recommendations — serologic testing and revaccination (pp. 22–23)
- **R28. Prevaccination testing** (p. 22): **not routinely recommended**; may be considered in specific settings to reduce cost by not vaccinating immune persons.
  - Should **not be a barrier** to vaccinating susceptible persons, especially hard-to-reach populations.
  - If done → use commercially available **total anti-HAV or IgG anti-HAV**.
  - Most cost-effective in adults born in or who lived for extensive periods in high/intermediate-endemicity areas (Figure 3). **Not indicated in children** (low prevalence).
  - Obtain vaccination history when feasible; do not postpone vaccination if history, records or testing are unavailable. Vaccinating persons already immune, or giving extra doses, carries no known risk.
- **R29. Postvaccination testing** (p. 22): **not necessary after routine vaccination** of infants, children, or adults. **Recommended** for persons whose management depends on immune status and for whom revaccination might be indicated — persons with HIV and other immunocompromised persons (e.g., hematopoietic cell transplant [HCT] and solid organ transplant recipients, persons receiving chemotherapy).
- **R30. Revaccination** (pp. 22–23): booster, challenge dose, or repeat series **not recommended for healthy persons** who completed a series as infants, children, or adults. Limited data for immunocompromised persons.
  - Clinicians **may consider** revaccinating persons with HIV and persons vaccinated while immunosuppressed from chemotherapy who did not reach ≥10 mIU/mL after the initial series → test **≥1 month after** the repeat series; still **<10 mIU/mL → no additional vaccination**; counsel on prevention incl. IG after exposure. After seroconversion, limited data on repeat testing or booster frequency.
- HCT recipients (p. 14, citing ACIP best practice guidance): recipients vaccinated before HCT should be vaccinated or revaccinated routinely after HCT, regardless of stem-cell source; **revaccination doses of HepA vaccine are recommended after HCT**.
- Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidance cited by ACIP (p. 14): solid organ transplant candidates — particularly liver transplant candidates — who are unvaccinated, undervaccinated, or seronegative for hepatitis A, aged 12–23 months (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence) and ≥2 years (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence) should receive a HepA vaccine series. *(IDSA's grading as quoted by ACIP; not an ACIP recommendation.)*

### Recommendations — schedules and vaccine management (pp. 23–24)
- **Interrupted schedule:** for all ages, **do not restart** the series. Limited data: a second Havrix dose delayed 20–31 months (age >2 years) or 4–8 years (adults) gave responses similar to the licensed schedules; in adults, a second Vaqta dose delayed 12–18 months gave a response similar to a booster dose given after 6 months.
- **Doses <6 months apart:** if dose 1 and dose 2 of single-antigen vaccine were given **<6 months apart → dose 2 is invalid**; repeat it 6 months after the invalid dose. If that repeat (third) dose is given any time **≥6 months after the first** and at least the age-appropriate dose was used → series complete.
- **Minimum interval/age:** doses given **≤4 days** before the minimum interval or age are valid; the 4-day rule **does not apply** to the first 3 doses of the accelerated Twinrix schedule (0, 7, 21–30 days, 12 months).
- **Age-appropriate dose only:** giving persons **aged ≥19 years** 2 pediatric doses (2 × 25 units Vaqta or 2 × 720 ELISA units Havrix), together or sequentially, instead of 1 adult dose is **not recommended**.
- **Twinrix and PEP:** Twinrix is **not recommended for PEP**.
- **Interchangeability:** ACIP **prefers** doses in a series from the same manufacturer; if not possible or unknown → give the vaccine available; the dose is valid and need not be repeated.
- **Mixing single-antigen vaccine with Twinrix** (Twinrix HepB component = standard adult hepatitis B [HepB] dose; HepA component = pediatric HepA dose):
  - single-antigen HepA and HepB vaccines may be used with Twinrix to complete either series; because the HepB component equals a standard adult HepB dose, the HepB schedule is the same whichever vaccine is used for which dose
  - age >19 years, 1 dose Twinrix → complete with **2 doses of adult HepA vaccine separated by ≥5 months**
  - 2 doses Twinrix → complete with **1 dose of adult HepA vaccine or Twinrix 5 months after the second dose**
  - age ≥19 years starting with single-antigen HepA vaccine → may complete with **2 doses of Twinrix or 1 dose of adult HepA vaccine**
  - age 18 years → same schedule as adults, using **pediatric** HepA formulations
  - accept only **dated records** as evidence of vaccination
- **Susceptible persons** to vaccinate: unvaccinated, partially vaccinated, nonresponders, or never infected.
- **Records:** try to locate missing records, but do not postpone vaccination; not found within a reasonable time → treat as susceptible and vaccinate.
- **Series initiation:** concern about loss to follow-up should **not** deter starting the series; 1 dose gives personal protection and contributes to herd immunity, though long-term protection might be suboptimal.
- **Live vaccines and IG:** give live vaccines (e.g., MMR, varicella) **≥2 weeks before or ≥6 months after IG**. IG given <2 weeks after MMR/varicella → revaccinate no sooner than 6 months after IG, unless serology shows a response.
- **Vaccine and IG together:** if both recommended → give simultaneously at different sites. Only one available → give it as soon as possible; the person may return for the other, **within 2 weeks of exposure** if given as PEP.
- **Standing orders** should be considered to raise coverage. Report adverse events to the Vaccine Adverse Event Reporting System (VAERS).

### Appendix A — CDC provider guidance: preexposure protection for travelers aged ≥12 months (p. 35)
- HepA vaccination at the appropriate dose and schedule is **preferred to IG** for travelers aged ≥12 months.
- One dose of single-antigen vaccine **at any time before departure** might give adequate protection for most healthy persons; single-dose long-term data are limited, and data are limited for other populations (e.g., immunocompromised) and for Twinrix.
- Unvaccinated → first dose as soon as travel is considered; second dose on the licensed schedule.
- One prior single-antigen dose **≥6 months ago** → can receive the second dose; prior dose **within the previous 6 months** → no additional dose needed for travel.
- Twinrix before travel: 0, 1, 6 months; or accelerated 0, 7, 21–30 days plus a booster at 12 months.
- Aged >40 years, immunocompromised, or chronic liver disease → decision to add IG weighs the person's ability to make protective antibody, magnitude of exposure risk (e.g., endemicity at destination), and availability of IG and vaccine.
- Because global endemicity is hard to determine, preexposure protection **can be considered for persons aged ≥12 months traveling to any area**.

### Appendix B — CDC provider guidance: risk assessment for PEP (pp. 36–37)
- Healthy persons who completed the series **at any time** need **no additional PEP** after exposure.
- PEP works better the sooner it is given; only one product available → give it as soon as possible, return for the other if it becomes available within 2 weeks of exposure; simultaneous → separate limbs. Complete the series with a second dose **≥6 months after the first** for long-term protection.
- **PEP should be given** to all persons who have not completed the series and were exposed, or are at risk of exposure, through close personal contact with a person with **serologically confirmed** HAV infection (e.g., household and sexual contacts, persons using injection or noninjection drugs with the infected person, caregivers not using appropriate personal protective equipment).
- Decision factors for IG, vaccine, or both: ability to make protective antibody; magnitude of transmission risk; availability of IG and vaccine. Severity and time to protective antibody **increase with age**. Household/sexual exposure generally riskier than common-source exposure (contaminated food, restaurant).
- **Child care centers:** PEP **should be given** to all previously unvaccinated staff and attendees if (1) ≥1 case is recognized in children, or (2) cases are recognized in ≥2 households of attendees. Case(s) among employees → **consider** PEP for other staff/attendees based on duties, hygiene, and symptoms at work. Centers with no diapered children → PEP **can be considered only** for center contacts of the index patient.
- **Food handlers / common-source food:** PEP for patrons **typically not indicated**. If, while likely infectious, the food handler **both** handled uncooked or cooked food without gloves **and** had diarrhea or poor hygiene → patron risk still low, but PEP **may be considered** for patrons or co-workers exposed while the handler was symptomatic and working. PEP here = generally vaccine for persons ≥12 months; IG may be considered in addition for immunocompromised persons or those with chronic liver disease. Possible repeated exposures (e.g., institutional cafeterias) → consider vaccine, IG, or both.
- **Schools, offices, other work settings:** single case with an outside source → PEP **not routinely indicated**. Epidemiologic evidence of transmission in the setting (e.g., among students) → PEP **should be given** to close contacts of index patients.
- **Congregate settings with regular close contact and hard-to-maintain hygiene** (correctional facility, homeless shelter, psychiatric facility, group home or residential facility for persons with developmental disabilities): confirmed case → PEP **should be considered** for all previously unvaccinated residents and employees; in multi-unit settings (e.g., prison ward), limit to persons in the area at risk.
- **Health care institutions:** HepA vaccination not routinely recommended for health care personnel. Patient with HAV admitted → staff **not routinely** given PEP; emphasize infection control (contact precautions for diapered or incontinent patients). PEP **may be considered case by case** when exposure risk is high.
  - Host risk factors: personnel with hepatitis A working while infectious or with diarrhea; patients with hepatitis A who are diapered or incontinent and symptomatic.
  - Infection-control risk factors: poor hand hygiene; poor adherence to standard and transmission-based precautions; suboptimal environmental cleaning.
  - In multi-unit facilities, PEP may be limited to areas of potential exposure (e.g., cardiology ward, intensive care unit). Sharing food or beverages among patients, families and staff has been associated with transmission.

### Epidemiology (pp. 1, 3–4)
- Vaccination history: 1996 ACIP routine vaccination of children ≥2 years in high-rate communities plus risk groups; 1999 expanded to 11 high-incidence states (consider in 6 more); 2006 routine vaccination of all children 12–23 months → 95.5% fall in reported cases 1996–2011 (p. 1).
- Since 2016: widespread person-to-person outbreaks, mainly among persons who use drugs and persons experiencing homelessness, also MSM; low adult coverage and high adult susceptibility sustain them (pp. 1, 4).
- 2017: 3,366 reported cases (1 per 100,000); estimated 6,700 after adjustment (95% confidence interval [CI] 4,700–7,400). 2018 preliminary: ~12,500 reported (p. 3).
- Highest 2017 rate in adults 30–39 years (2.1 per 100,000); lowest in children ≤9 years (0.1 per 100,000) (p. 4).
- US-born adults ≥20 years: 74.1% susceptible (total anti-HAV negative) in 2007–2016 (p. 4).
- Coverage: children born 2015–2016 — ≥2 doses by 35 months 76.6%, ≥1 dose by 24 months 84.7%; adolescents 13–17 years (2017) — ≥2 doses 68.4%; adults ≥19 years (2017) — ≥2 doses 10.9% overall, 17.7% travelers, 20.8% chronic liver disease (p. 3). MSM coverage estimated 25%–45% (p. 6).

### Virology and transmission (pp. 4–5)
- Single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus (~7.5 kb), Picornaviridae, genus Hepatovirus; genotypes I–III infect humans; one human serotype; vaccine response similar across genotypes (p. 4).
- Fecal-oral spread (person to person, food, water); children historically a key source because most infections are asymptomatic and they shed for months; transmission now mainly among susceptible adults (p. 4).
- Environmental stability for months; stable frozen. **Inactivation:** heating food >185°F (>85°C) for 1 minute, or surface disinfection with 1:100 dilution of household bleach (sodium hypochlorite) in tap water (p. 4).
- Rare transmission by blood products; nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) screening of source plasma since 2002 (p. 4).

### Clinical features and natural history (p. 5)
- **Incubation:** average 28 days (range 15–50 days).
- Clinically indistinguishable from other acute viral hepatitis; abrupt fever, malaise, anorexia, nausea, abdominal discomfort, then within days to a week dark urine, pale stools, jaundice.
- **Age dependence:** children <6 years — 70% asymptomatic, illness usually without jaundice; older children and adults — usually symptomatic, jaundice in >70%.
- Resolution within 2–3 months; complete recovery within 6 months.
- **Prolonged or relapsing hepatitis A:** 10%–15% of symptomatic persons, lasting up to 6 months — **consider infectious**; recovery from relapse is universal; no chronic infection.
- **Fulminant hepatic failure:** rare, <1% of cases; higher proportion seen in the outbreaks that began in 2016.
- Peak infectivity: the **2 weeks before jaundice onset or enzyme elevation** (highest stool viral load); most persons non-infectious **1 week after jaundice onset**. Children may shed up to 10 weeks after illness onset; neonatally infected infants up to 6 months.
- Prolonged viremia/fecal shedding: >1 month in immunocompetent and >1 year in immunocompromised persons reported; prolonged viremia after organ transplantation described.
- Severity increases with age; adults >40 years more likely to be hospitalized (p. 9).
- Chronic liver disease does not raise the risk of acquiring HAV, but HBV, HCV, cirrhosis, fatty liver disease, alcoholic liver disease and autoimmune hepatitis are associated with **increased risk of fulminant hepatitis** with HAV infection; 38.3% of US HAV hospitalizations in 2010–2011 had some liver disease (p. 8).
- HIV: up to 87% susceptible; HAV infection prolonged, may increase HIV replication and lengthen HAV transmission; underlying liver disease raises severe-disease risk (p. 8).

### Diagnosis and serology (pp. 5–6, 14)
- Cannot be diagnosed on clinical or epidemiologic features alone. **Diagnosis requires IgM anti-HAV in serum, or HAV RNA in serum or stool** (p. 5).
- **IgM anti-HAV:** detectable within 5–10 days of symptom onset in most persons, peaks within 1 month, undetectable within 6 months; positives >1 year after infection and false positives reported → **test for IgM anti-HAV only if symptomatic and suspected of HAV infection** (p. 5).
- **Total or IgG anti-HAV:** for prevalence studies or to check immunity in a person with a preexposure indication. IgG anti-HAV (infection or vaccine) appears early and lasts for life. Total anti-HAV positive in a symptomatic person with no IgG assay available → test IgM to establish acute infection. **Total anti-HAV positive + IgM negative = immune** (past infection or vaccination) (p. 5).
- Liver tests: AST/ALT rise 5–10 days before symptoms; bilirubin and aminotransferases usually normal by 2–3 months (p. 5).
- **After vaccination:** IgM anti-HAV detectable 2–3 weeks after 1 dose in 8%–20% of adults → same rule, test IgM only if symptomatic and suspected (p. 12).
- Correlate of protection: seroconversion = IgG anti-HAV **≥10 mIU/mL** (the minimum protective level used in postvaccination studies); 10–33 mIU/mL proposed as protective across assays; no absolute protective level defined. IG gives ~90% efficacy despite very low anti-HAV levels; vaccine/IG levels are 10- to 100-fold lower than after natural infection (pp. 11–12).

### Vaccines — products, administration, performance (pp. 11–17)
- **Products** (p. 11): single-antigen Havrix (licensed 1995) and Vaqta (1996); combination Twinrix (HAV + HBV antigens, 2001). All inactivated, aluminum-adjuvanted, thimerosal-free; Havrix and Twinrix contain 2-phenoxyethanol; Vaqta preservative-free. Doses and schedules: Table 1.
  - Twinrix: licensed ≥18 years; 720 ELISA units HAV (half the Havrix adult dose) + 20 µg hepatitis B surface antigen (= Engerix-B adult dose); primary 0, 1, 6 months; accelerated 0, 7, 21–30 days + booster at 12 months, may be used before travel or other potential exposure (p. 11).
  - Old 3-dose 360-ELISA-unit pediatric Havrix formulation no longer available or recommended since 1999 (p. 11 footnote).
- **Storage:** 36°F–46°F (2°C–8°C); do not freeze (p. 11).
- **Route:** intramuscular, anterolateral thigh or deltoid by age, age-appropriate needle length; different anatomic site from other vaccines or IG; a dose given subcutaneously (inadvertently or for clinical reason) is **valid**, no repeat (p. 11).
- **Efficacy** (p. 13): Havrix — 94% (95% CI 79%–99%) against clinical hepatitis A after 2 doses (360 ELISA units, 1 month apart) in ~40,000 Thai children 1–16 years; Vaqta — 100% (lower bound 87%) after 1 dose (25 units) in ~1,000 New York children 2–16 years. Twinrix efficacy expected similar to its components.
- **Immunogenicity** (p. 13):
  - Ages 2–18 years: 97%–100% protective 1 month after dose 1; 100% after dose 2.
  - Infants <12 months with passively acquired maternal antibody: lower geometric mean concentrations (GMCs) — about one-third to one-tenth — though all become protected; most have an anamnestic booster response 1–6 years later; maternal antibody mostly undetectable by age 1 year; vaccine highly immunogenic when started at age >1 year regardless of maternal status (pp. 13–14).
  - Adults: highly immunogenic; adults ≥40 years vs 20–30 years similar at 1 month after dose 1 (91%–99.7%) and dose 2 (95.3%–100%), but **lower at 15 days** (79.7% vs 92.3%) (p. 13).
  - Twinrix seroconversion for HAV: 93.8% after 1 dose, 98.8% after 2, 99.9% 1 month after 3 (standard schedule) (p. 13).
- **Duration** (pp. 13–14):
  - Exact duration after vaccination unknown; lifelong after natural infection.
  - Vaccinated at <24 months (Alaska Native children, series started 6–21 months): seropositive to at least 15–16 years; modeling ≥30 years.
  - Vaccinated as children (3–6 years, 3-dose): protective levels ≥22 years in most.
  - Vaccinated as adults (Havrix 1,440, 0–6 or 0–12 months): >97% seropositive at 20 years; modeling ≥95% at 30 years, ≥90% at 40 years; 6 of 7 who lost antibody had strong booster response.
  - Single dose: protective antibody for almost 11 years (up to 10.67 years studied); controls outbreaks; Argentina single-dose program >97% protected up to 9 years, ~87.6% up to 11 years; more data needed.
- **Reduced immunogenicity** (pp. 14–16):
  - Immunocompromise (HCT, chemotherapy, HIV): humoral response may be reduced; limited data suggest doubled antigen dose or extra doses might raise response.
  - HIV: seroconversion 50%–94%; CD4 >200–300 cells/mm³ respond nearly as well as immunocompetent persons, lower CD4 less likely to respond; 85% of 130 seropositive 6–10 years after 2 doses; seroreversion linked to overweight/obesity, lower CD4 or viremia at vaccination, lower/delayed response. Vaccine does not raise HIV viral load, affect CD4 or speed progression (pp. 8, 15).
  - Chronic liver disease: seroprotection similar to healthy adults. Liver transplant recipients: highly variable (0 of 8; 6 of 23 [26%]; 38 [97%] responded to standard dose in another study) (p. 15).
  - Age >40 years: lower seroprotection especially within 15 days of dose 1, similar after 2 doses despite lower final levels (pp. 15–16).
  - Overweight: slower response; smoking not evaluated (p. 16).
  - IG given with vaccine: lower GMCs but same proportion protected (p. 14).
- **Safety** (p. 16):
  - Common: injection-site reactions (pain, erythema), mild systemic reactions (fever, irritability, loss of appetite, drowsiness, headache); Twinrix rates similar to separate vaccines.
  - VAERS 2006–2018: 25,079 HepA reports, 5.7% serious; 2,117 Twinrix reports, 8.6% serious; findings similar to other inactivated vaccines.
  - **Pregnancy:** limited data; 139 VAERS reports (1996–2013) showed no concerning pattern; Vaccine Safety Datalink study found no increased risk of adverse events examined, but an association with small-for-gestational-age infants, believed likely due to unmeasured confounding.
- **Contraindication/precaution** (p. 16): severe allergic reaction (e.g., anaphylaxis) to a previous dose or severe allergy to a vaccine component → **should not receive** HepA vaccine. Moderate or severe acute illness with or without fever → **might want to defer** while ill. (Table 4 footnote \*\* defines contraindicated as life-threatening allergic reaction to a previous dose or allergy to any vaccine component.)
- **Simultaneous administration** (pp. 16–17): no reduced response with other vaccines in children or adults (incl. diphtheria, polio, tetanus, typhoid, cholera, Japanese encephalitis, rabies, yellow fever, HepB in adults; diphtheria-tetanus-acellular pertussis [DTaP], Haemophilus influenzae type b [Hib], HepB, MMR, inactivated polio in children <18 months).
- **Cost-effectiveness** (p. 17): routine vaccination at age 1 year — $28,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) vs no vaccination; catch-up likely more favorable now than the 2015 estimate.

### Immune globulin (pp. 12–13)
- Passive antibody transfer; **GamaSTAN** is the only US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved IG for hepatitis A prophylaxis (intramuscular, preservative-free). Doses: Table 2 (changed 2017).
- Can be given simultaneously with inactivated vaccines or toxoids at a different site, or at any interval.
- May interfere with live vaccines (MMR, varicella): give those ≥2 weeks before or ≥6 months after IG; IG needed <2 weeks after MMR/varicella → revaccinate no sooner than 6 months after IG (pp. 12–13).

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Elevated-aminotransferase criterion differs within the document:** Recommendations text (p. 2; Persons with Chronic Liver Disease, pp. 19–20) says ALT or AST "persistently greater than twice the upper limit of normal"; Table 3 (p. 15) says "more than twice the upper limit of normal **or** persistently elevated for 6 months". The text sets the routine-vaccination indication; Table 3 is the risk-assessment table.
- **Age floor for immunocompromised / chronic liver disease travelers:** Table 4 (p. 19) preexposure rows read ">6 months", while the narrative travel guidance for these groups sits under "Persons Aged ≥12 Months" (p. 18) and Appendix A covers travelers aged ≥12 months (p. 35); infants 6–11 months are addressed separately (vaccine, no IG — measles prioritized, p. 18), which conflicts with an IG option for 6–11-month-olds implied by the ">6 months" rows.
- **Twinrix mixing ages (p. 23):** "A person aged **>19** years who receives 1 dose of Twinrix…" vs the next sentence "A person aged **≥19** years who begins… with single-antigen HepA vaccine"; Twinrix itself is licensed ≥18 years and 18-year-olds are told to follow the adult schedule with pediatric formulations.
- **Second-dose timing wording:** PEP text says the second dose "should be administered 6 months after the first" (p. 21); Table 4 footnote † and Appendix B say "at least 6 months after the first" (pp. 19, 36); Table 1 gives 0, 6–12 months (Havrix) and 0, 6–18 months (Vaqta).
- **Contraindication wording:** Vaccine Safety (p. 16) — "severe allergic reaction (e.g., anaphylaxis) after a previous dose … or severe allergy to a HepA vaccine component"; Table 4 footnote \*\* (p. 19) — "life-threatening allergic reaction to a previous dose … or allergy to any vaccine component" (no "severe" qualifier on component allergy).
- **Healthy adults >40 years, PEP IG:** p. 21 opening text says coadministration of IG "is recommended … for persons aged >40 years based on the provider's risk assessment", while the Healthy Persons subsection says IG "may be administered" to persons >40 years depending on risk assessment; Table 4 footnote § ties it to the provider's risk assessment. Force is "may"/risk-assessment-based, not routine.
- **Wiki comparison:** [[cirrhosis]] states (from ACG 2026) "test for immunity, vaccinate if nonimmune". ACIP calls prevaccination testing "not routinely recommended" but allows it to reduce cost, and says it must not be a barrier (p. 22). Not a direct conflict (ACIP routinely recommends vaccination in chronic liver disease either way), but the testing step differs in emphasis.
- No GRADE evidence types are printed for any recommendation; they are on the ACIP website only (p. 2).
