## Summary

This 2025 update addresses six treatment and surveillance questions not resolved in the 2018 American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guidance, and issues **9 numbered recommendations** (one per Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome [PICO] question except PICO 6, which carries four). Evidence searches ran to February 2024 (questions 1–3) and December 2024 (question 4); four de novo systematic reviews were performed and two existing reviews reused. Eight of the nine recommendations are **Conditional / Very Low** — only the pregnancy recommendation reaches Strong / Moderate — so shared decision-making is the operating mode throughout.

The guideline retains the 2018 framework of five chronic hepatitis B (CHB) phases (immune-tolerant, hepatitis B e antigen [HBeAg]+ immune active, HBeAg− immune active, inactive, hepatitis B surface antigen [HBsAg]-negative) and formally treats the **indeterminate / grey-zone** phases as a distinct group: up to 40% of adults with CHB fail one or more criteria for a defined phase. Preferred nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) remain entecavir (ETV), tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), and tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) — high efficacy, low resistance, broadly applicable including in decompensated [[cirrhosis]] and immunocompromise.

Changes from 2018 that the document itself describes:
- **Pregnancy (p.11):** 2018 = TDF (preferred) at gestational weeks 28–32, continued until delivery or up to 3 months postpartum → 2025 = TDF or TAF at week 28; may stop at delivery when prevention of mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) is the only indication
- **HBeAg-negative indeterminate phase (p.18):** 2018 = not treated; monitor and treat on transition to immune active, or for ≥F2 fibrosis, ≥A3 inflammation, or persistently elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT) above the upper limit of normal (ULN) with hepatitis B virus (HBV) DNA ≥2000 IU/mL, especially if over age 40 → 2025 = antivirals offered via shared decision-making

This is a focused update — the 2018 guidance remains applicable for children, immune-active CHB, low-level viremia and virologic breakthrough, cirrhosis, and special populations (transplant recipients, acute HBV infection, co-infections).

## Key Findings / Claims

**The two approved drug classes, and why one of them is barely used:**

- Two classes are approved for CHB: **peginterferon** and **nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs)**. **Peginterferon is rarely used, given its limitations of applicability, safety and tolerability** compared with NA therapy.
- Three NAs are **preferred**: **ETV, TDF, TAF** — all with high HBV DNA suppression, low resistance, good tolerability, and broad applicability including **decompensated cirrhosis and immunocompromised states**.
- **Choosing among the three** turns on availability/cost, age, renal or bone disease (**avoid TDF**), pregnancy (**avoid ETV**), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) coinfection (**avoid ETV unless HIV RNA is suppressed on antiretroviral therapy**) and prior lamivudine exposure (**avoid ETV**).
- **Renal rules:** all three require attention to renal function and/or dialysis. **TAF is not recommended at creatinine clearance <15 mL/min when not yet on dialysis.** ETV and TDF need dose adjustment for renal dysfunction or dialysis — though for a patient on TDF with declining renal function, **changing the NA is preferred to adjusting the dose**.
- **Functional cure (HBsAg loss) is increasingly the desired endpoint** because it is associated with reduced clinical events and is durable — but it is **infrequent with every approved therapy, albeit higher with peginterferon than with NAs.** That tension is the whole reason peginterferon has not disappeared.
- **Treatment goals** are prevention of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and liver-related death; because those take years to decades, **surrogate endpoints** are used — undetectable HBV DNA by PCR, HBeAg loss/seroconversion, HBsAg loss/seroconversion, normal ALT, and infrequently histology (necroinflammation improved ≥2 points with no fibrosis worsening). Secondary goals: reduced vertical and horizontal transmission, better patient-reported outcomes and quality of life, less stigma.
- **Named research gap:** the role of **peginterferon as add-on or switch therapy to an NA** as a strategy to achieve functional cure is listed among the guideline's open questions — i.e. not a recommended strategy today.

**PICO 1 — Prevention of MTCT:**

- Initiate TDF or TAF at gestational **week 28** for HBV DNA **>200,000 IU/mL at any time point in pregnancy, regardless of HBeAg status** (Rec 1 — Strong / Moderate)
- Past week 28 at presentation → start immediately anyway
- TAF is co-recommended; TDF has the more extensive pregnancy safety record. TDF/TAF preferred over lamivudine/telbivudine (lower resistance)
- Start at **week 16** if hepatitis B immune globulin (HBIG) will be unavailable for the infant (randomized controlled trial [RCT]: noninferior MTCT, 0.76% vs 0%)
- Consider **earlier** initiation if amniocentesis is anticipated (raises perinatal transmission when HBV DNA >2,000,000 IU/mL) or if preterm labour risk is high
- Can stop TDF/TAF **at delivery** if MTCT prevention was the only indication; flare risk is the same as stopping at postpartum weeks 4–12
- After stopping: HBV DNA + ALT every 1–3 months for up to 6 months; reinitiate for a significant withdrawal flare (**ALT ≥5× ULN**)
- Breastfeeding safe on TDF or TAF
- Already on TDF or TAF when pregnant → continue; on ETV or other antivirals → switch to TDF or TAF (p.9; ETV has insufficient pregnancy safety data, p.11)
- Untreated MTCT despite HBIG + birth-dose vaccine within 24 h: ~8–10% when maternal HBV DNA >200,000 IU/mL; 16–25% in HBeAg-positive persons with no birth-dose HBIG

**PICO 2 — Horizontal transmission prevention:**

- For viremic HBsAg+ persons not meeting disease-specific treatment indications but in high-risk transmission scenarios (unprotected sex or multiple partners, injection drug use with inconsistent harm reduction, susceptible household contacts, healthcare workers performing Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America (SHEA) category III exposure-prone procedures): shared decision-making about antivirals (Rec 2 — Conditional / Very Low)
- Favour treatment when the exposed contacts are unvaccinated, have inadequate vaccine response, are immunocompromised, or have unknown vaccine status; a person who requests treatment for this reason can be prescribed it
- Groups with inadequate vaccine response (Table 2 footnote): immunocompromising conditions, diabetes, advanced liver disease, advanced kidney disease, smokers, obesity
- Not necessary for average-risk transmission scenarios (Table 2)
- Goal of treatment: HBV DNA below the lower limit of quantification (Table 2)
- Continue until the high transmission risk is no longer present — e.g. healthcare worker no longer doing category III/exposure-prone procedures, or risk reduced by harm reduction or immunization of susceptible contacts (p.13; Table 2)
- Counsel everyone starting therapy about the risks of stopping, including severe liver flares (p.13; Table 2)
- Healthcare workers doing SHEA category III procedures follow SHEA and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidance; **SHEA suggests maintaining HBV DNA <1000 IU/mL** (Table 2 footnote). SHEA and other committees worldwide allow variable HBV DNA thresholds (p.13)
- No activity restrictions: routine household contact, shared meals, hugging, contact sports, school and professional training are not routes of transmission — but avoid sharing toothbrushes and razors. Disclosure by healthcare workers is not required (CDC and SHEA), and CHB is protected under the Americans with Disabilities Act

**PICO 3 — Immune-tolerant phase treatment:**

- Definition: HBeAg+, HBV DNA >10⁷ IU/mL, persistently normal ALT (M: <35 U/L; F: <25 U/L) on **≥2 measurements ≥6 months apart**
- Suggest treatment for **age over 40 years**, OR significant liver inflammation (**grade ≥2**), OR fibrosis (**≥F2**) on biopsy or non-invasive testing (Rec 3 — Conditional / Very Low)
- For age <40 without significant inflammation or fibrosis: shared decision-making weighing age (child/adolescent vs adult), HCC family history, likelihood of long-term adherence to treatment and monitoring, and prevention of transmission
- If untreated: HBV DNA + ALT at least every 6 months to catch transition to immune-active CHB
- Vibration-controlled transient elastography cutoff **7 kPa** for ≥F2 (sensitivity 69%, specificity 83%)
- ETV/TDF/TAF have low resistance rates but DNA may not fully suppress given very high viral replication levels
- **NA plus peginterferon for 1–1.5 years** in this phase gave a significant fall in HBV DNA but had **limited effect on HBeAg and HBsAg loss/seroconversion** (systematic review, p.16)
- No direct evidence NA therapy reduces HCC in immune-tolerant phase; benefits inferred

**PICO 4 — HBeAg-negative indeterminate phase treatment:**

- Definition: HBsAg+, HBeAg−, no cirrhosis, with ALT/HBV DNA outside inactive or immune-active thresholds
- Two subgroups: (I) normal/mildly elevated ALT + HBV DNA ≥2000 IU/mL; (II) elevated ALT + HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL
- AASLD now suggests antivirals via shared decision-making, reassessing at every follow-up visit (Conditional/Very Low)
- **Advanced fibrosis (F3/F4) → recommended for treatment** (p.17). High-sensitivity non-invasive thresholds for advanced fibrosis: Fibrosis-4 Index (FIB-4) **>1.45** or vibration-controlled elastography **≥8 kPa**
- More likely to benefit: older (>40 years), male, low platelets (<180 k/mm³) (p.17)
- Other shared-decision factors: pill burden, cost (ETV and TDF are generic), need for regular monitoring
- Phase determination: ALT and HBV DNA on ≥2 occasions over 6–12 months (p.18)
- Progression to advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis, or transition to HBeAg-negative immune active phase → start treatment (p.18)
- Meta-analysis (14,691 patients): antiviral treatment associated with lower HCC incidence (adjusted incidence rate ratio [aIRR] 0.36, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.16–0.81)
- If not treating: ALT + HBV DNA every 3–6 months year 1, every 6 months thereafter
- Elevated ALT + HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL → evaluate for other liver injury etiologies

**PICO 5 — NA discontinuation:**

- Population: HBeAg−, no cirrhosis, HBV DNA undetectable ≥2 years, on NA ≥3 years
- AASLD suggests NOT withdrawing NA therapy until HBsAg loss (Conditional/Very Low)
- Rationale: HBsAg loss only 11% at 2 years off therapy; ALT flares 27% at 2 years; re-treatment 42% at 5 years; decompensation and death reported in cohort studies
- Diverges from European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) and Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL), which recommend considering NA withdrawal to enhance HBsAg loss after sustained HBV DNA suppression for 2–4 years; World Health Organization (WHO) allows discontinuation only in exceptional circumstances
- After withdrawal: ALT + HBV DNA every 1–3 months for the first 6 months, then every 3 months for the next 6–12 months, then every 3–6 months
- If patient wishes to stop, **all** of the following required (p.20; Table 3):
  - No history of advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation (variceal bleed, ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome), HCC, or extrahepatic complications of HBV
  - HBeAg-positive at start → seroconverted to HBeAg-negative/antibody to hepatitis B e antigen (anti-HBe)-positive for ≥1 year, with HBV DNA undetectable ≥2 years
  - HBeAg-negative at start → HBV DNA undetectable ≥2 years
  - No hepatitis D virus (HDV) or HIV co-infection
  - Quantitative HBsAg (qHBsAg) <100 IU/mL
  - Willing to adhere to frequent monitoring
- Restart immediately — need only ONE (p.21): HBV DNA ≥10,000 IU/mL regardless of ALT; ALT ≥5× ULN regardless of HBV DNA; total bilirubin >2.5 mg/dL; any symptoms of hepatic decompensation
- May also restart any time for: personal wish, extrahepatic complications of HBV, or treatment-initiation criteria met (HBV DNA >2000 IU/mL **and** ALT >2× ULN) (p.21; Table 3)

**PICO 6 — [[hcc-surveillance|HCC surveillance]] post-HBsAg loss and in co-infections:**

- Post-HBsAg loss: continue surveillance for cirrhosis, family history of HCC, men >40 at loss, women >50 at loss (Rec 6 — Conditional/Very Low)
  - Timing of HBsAg loss unknown → individualize, considering cirrhosis and family history of HCC (p.23)
- HBV/HDV co-infection: all adults regardless of cirrhosis (HCC incidence 18.65 vs. 4.84 per 1000 person-years vs. mono-infection) (Rec 7 — Conditional/Very Low)
  - Children/adolescents with HDV → individualize, considering age, family history and fibrosis stage (p.23)
- HBV/HIV: men ≥18 years, women ≥40 years (Rec 8 — Conditional/Very Low)
- HBV/hepatitis C virus (HCV): treat HCV, and HCC surveillance per HBV mono-infection criteria (Rec 9 — Conditional/Very Low)
- Prior HCV (anti-HCV+, HCV RNA−) or prior HDV (anti-HDV+, HDV RNA−) → surveillance same as HBV alone (p.23–24)
- Triple or quadruple infection (HBV plus HIV, HDV and/or HCV) → follow the co-infection with the highest risk; e.g. HBV-HDV-HCV follows HBV-HDV (p.24)
- All surveillance: ultrasound + alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) about every 6 months per 2023 AASLD HCC guideline

**Table 4 — target groups for HCC surveillance (p.45):**

| Group | Who |
|---|---|
| Chronic HBV with cirrhosis | Child-Pugh A–B, and transplant-eligible Child-Pugh C |
| Chronic HBV without cirrhosis, increased HCC risk | Man from endemic country, age >40 y · Woman from endemic country, age >50 y · Person from Africa at earlier age · Family history of HCC · PAGE-B score ≥10 |
| Co-infection without cirrhosis | HBV/HDV: all persons · HBV/HIV: men ≥18 years and women ≥40 years |
| HBsAg loss with increased risk | All persons with cirrhosis · Man >40 y at HBsAg loss · Woman >50 y at HBsAg loss · Family history of HCC |

- Rows for chronic HBV with/without cirrhosis are per the 2023 AASLD HCC guidance
- Endemic country = ≥2% prevalence of chronic HBV (most of Africa, Asia, the Middle East and Eastern Europe)
- Person from Africa: surveillance can start as early as the third decade (median age 46 years at HCC diagnosis)
- Other risk calculators (REACH-B, REAL-B) can be considered; PAGE-B was validated in people on antiviral treatment, REACH-B in untreated people

**Definitions (Table 1, p.41):**

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Immune-tolerant | HBeAg+, HBV DNA >10,000,000 IU/mL, normal ALT (<25 IU/mL women, <35 IU/mL men) |
| Immune active | HBeAg+, ALT >2× ULN, HBV DNA >20,000 IU/mL · or HBeAg−, ALT >2× ULN, HBV DNA >2000 IU/mL |
| Inactive | HBeAg−, HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL, normal ALT |
| Indeterminate ("grey zone") | HBeAg+ or −, HBV DNA and/or ALT outside immune-tolerant, immune-active or inactive definitions |
| HBsAg loss | Loss of HBsAg on antiviral therapy or spontaneously |
| Functional cure | HBsAg-negative and HBV DNA undetectable off treatment ≥6 months (± anti-HBs) |
| Partial cure | HBsAg <100 IU/mL with HBV DNA undetectable off treatment ≥6 months |

**Figures 2–4 — management by phase and cirrhosis (p.37–39):**

- ALT ULN of 35 U/L (males) and 25 U/L (females) recommended to guide management (Figures 2, 3)
- HBeAg-positive, no cirrhosis (Figure 2): immune active (HBV DNA ≥20,000 IU/mL, ALT >2× ULN) → treat; immune-tolerant or indeterminate → monitor ALT + HBV DNA, assess fibrosis, reassess each visit; suggest considering treatment if age ≥40 years or ≥F2 fibrosis on biopsy or imaging-based non-invasive tests
- HBeAg-negative, no cirrhosis (Figure 3): HBV DNA >2000 IU/mL + ALT >2× ULN → treat; HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL + ALT >ULN → evaluate other liver disease; HBV DNA >2000 IU/mL + ALT <2× ULN → consider treatment by shared decision-making
- Cirrhosis — **any one of**: abdominal imaging consistent with cirrhosis (nodular liver with splenomegaly or varices), elastography with liver stiffness measurement (LSM) **≥11 kPa**, FIB-4 **≥3.25**, liver biopsy F4 (Figures 3, 4)
- HBsAg+ with cirrhosis, any HBV DNA level (image printed under the Figure 4 heading):
  - Compensated → ETV, TDF or TAF **until HBsAg loss**; assess for clinically significant portal hypertension; HCC surveillance every 6 months
  - Decompensated (variceal hemorrhage, ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome) → ETV, TDF or TAF **indefinitely**; monitor for lactic acidosis; refer for consideration of liver transplantation; HCC surveillance every 6 months

**Suboptimal response to NAs (image printed under the Figure 5 heading, p.40; legend under Figure 4, p.39):**

- Plateau: HBV DNA ≤1 log decline in the past 6 months · Breakthrough: HBV DNA rise ≥1 log or >100 IU/mL after undetectability · Slow decline: HBV DNA quantifiable after 96 weeks of therapy and <5-log decline from baseline
- First steps (all types): confirm adherence and correct dosing (tenofovir with food, entecavir on an empty stomach); more frequent HBV DNA testing to define the pattern; consider resistance testing (may not be possible at HBV DNA <1000 IU/mL; not available for tenofovir)
- Plateau or breakthrough after adherence confirmed → change antiviral; switch preferred (lower pill burden), add-on an alternative. Slow decline → keep monitoring, no change

| Current drug | Switch | Add-on |
|---|---|---|
| Entecavir | TDF or TAF | TDF or TAF |
| TDF | TAF or ETV | ETV |
| TAF | ETV | ETV |
| Lamivudine or telbivudine | TDF or TAF | — |
| Adefovir | ETV, TDF or TAF | — |

- After a change: HBV DNA every 3 months until undetectable, then every 6 months

## Recommendations

The document numbers **9 recommendations**, each with a Strength (Strong or Conditional) and a Certainty of evidence. Everything else in the document — the "Implementation Considerations" bullets under each PICO question, and the Background/Evidence prose — is **ungraded**; those points appear in *Key Findings* above rather than here.

| # | PICO | Recommendation (as written) | Strength | Certainty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | For pregnant persons with HBV DNA levels greater than 200,000 IU/mL at any time point during pregnancy, regardless of HBeAg status, AASLD recommends initiating tenofovir disoproxil fumarate or tenofovir alafenamide at gestational week 28 to prevent mother-to-child transmission. Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate has a more extensive safety record in pregnancy than tenofovir alafenamide. | Strong | Moderate |
| 2 | 2 | For persons who are HBsAg-positive with viremia not meeting disease-specific treatment indications and who are in high-risk scenarios for transmission to others, AASLD suggests a shared decision-making approach regarding antiviral treatment. | Conditional | Very Low |
| 3 | 3 | For persons in the immune-tolerant phase (defined as HBeAg-positive, HBV DNA >10⁷ IU/mL, and normal ALT levels), the AASLD suggests antiviral therapy for those over age 40 years or with significant liver inflammation (grade 2 or higher) or fibrosis (F2 or greater) on liver biopsy or noninvasive tests. For persons under age 40 years who are interested in starting treatment earlier, the AASLD suggests shared decision-making with consideration of risk factors as well as the benefits and risks of treatment. | Conditional | Very low |
| 4 | 4 | In adults with HBsAg-positive, HBeAg-negative chronic HBV infection without cirrhosis and in the indeterminate phase, AASLD suggests antiviral therapy using a shared decision-making approach by assessing risks and benefits and to reevaluate that decision at each follow-up visit if treatment has not been initiated. | Conditional | Very low |
| 5 | 5 | In adults with chronic hepatitis B, who are HBeAg-negative without cirrhosis, with sustained undetectable HBV DNA on nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy, AASLD suggests not withdrawing nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy until HBsAg loss. | Conditional | Very Low |
| 6 | 6 | In persons who achieved HBsAg loss, AASLD suggests continued HCC surveillance for those with cirrhosis, with family history of HCC, men who experienced HBsAg loss after age 40, and women who experienced HBsAg loss after age 50. | Conditional | Very Low |
| 7 | 6 | In persons with HBV-HDV co-infection, AASLD suggests HCC surveillance of adults independent of cirrhosis status. The decision to undertake surveillance in children should be individualized due to the risk of HCC being unknown in this population. | Conditional | Very Low |
| 8 | 6 | In persons with HBV-HIV co-infection, AASLD suggests HCC surveillance for men ≥18 years of age and women ≥40 years of age. | Conditional | Very Low |
| 9 | 6 | In persons with HBV-HCV co-infection, AASLD recommends treatment for HCV and suggests HCC surveillance as per criteria for HBV mono-infection. | Conditional | Very Low |

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- AASLD diverges from EASL and APASL on NA discontinuation: AASLD suggests not stopping until functional cure; EASL/APASL support considering withdrawal after 2–4 years of sustained suppression
- WHO uses single HBV DNA threshold (>2000 IU/mL) without HBeAg stratification; AASLD maintains HBeAg + DNA thresholds reflecting US resource context
- Role of qHBsAg, HBV RNA, and hepatitis B core-related antigen (HBcrAg) not addressed — future guidance anticipated
- Benefit of treating inactive carriers with high HBsAg levels (>1000 IU/mL) not addressed
- Evidence quality predominantly Very Low — most recommendations require individualized shared decision-making
- **Internal inconsistencies in the document:**
  - NA stopping criteria: text (p.20) excludes a history of **advanced fibrosis**/cirrhosis; Table 3 (p.44) lists only cirrhosis (both list decompensation, HCC and extrahepatic complications) — the stricter text criterion is used above
  - Immune-tolerant age cut-off: Rec 3 says "over age 40 years"; Figure 2 says "≥40 years"
  - Immediate restart after stopping NA: text (p.21) ALT **≥5×** ULN; Table 3 (p.44) ALT **>5×** ULN
  - HBeAg-negative indeterminate (HBV DNA >2000 IU/mL, ALT <2× ULN), untreated: text (p.18) ALT + HBV DNA every **3–6 months** in year 1; Figure 3 every **3 months** in year 1 (both every 6 months thereafter)
  - Immune-tolerant, untreated: text (p.15) at least every 6 months; Figure 2 every 3–6 months in year 1, then every 6 months
  - Figure 4 and Figure 5 legends (p.39–40) are printed above each other's images
