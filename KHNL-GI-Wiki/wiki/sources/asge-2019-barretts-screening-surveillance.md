# ASGE 2019 — Screening and Surveillance of Barrett's Esophagus

## Summary

This American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) guideline addresses **screening and surveillance** of [[barretts-esophagus|Barrett's esophagus]] (BE). It uses Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methodology across 6 PICO (population, intervention, comparison, outcome) questions covering surveillance of nondysplastic BE (NDBE), screening of at-risk populations, and advanced imaging/sampling adjuncts (chromoendoscopy, confocal laser endomicroscopy [CLE], endoscopic ultrasound [EUS], wide-area transepithelial sampling with computer-assisted 3-dimensional analysis [WATS-3D], volumetric laser endomicroscopy [VLE]). It repeatedly defers to the 2018 ASGE guideline on endoscopic eradication therapy (EET) for treatment questions.

Key positions: surveillance endoscopy is suggested for NDBE (conditional, very low quality — no randomized controlled trial (RCT) exists; benefit may be inflated by lead- and length-time bias). There is insufficient evidence on the effectiveness of screening, but if screening endoscopy is performed it should target an **at-risk population** — high risk = family history of esophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) or BE; moderate risk = gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) plus at least 1 other risk factor (age >50, obesity/central adiposity, history of smoking, male gender). The panel did **not** recommend screening in low-risk patients, and declined to broaden the at-risk group to people with multiple risk factors *independent of GERD*. For surveillance imaging, **chromoendoscopy (dye-based or virtual) added to white-light endoscopy with Seattle-protocol biopsies is strongly recommended over white-light + Seattle alone** (moderate quality; ~9% absolute / ~30% relative increase in dysplasia detection) — as an **adjunct to**, never a replacement for, the Seattle protocol. Routine **CLE is suggested against** (conditional, low). **EUS is strongly recommended against** for routinely differentiating mucosal (T1a) from submucosal (T1b) disease (strong, moderate; sensitivity only 41%, high false-positive rate). **WATS-3D added to Seattle-protocol biopsies is suggested** (conditional, low). There is **no recommendation** for or against **VLE** (insufficient evidence).

Two scope limits matter. The surveillance PICO question "does not address the frequency of surveillance intervals in patients with NDBE or the ideal approach to surveillance biopsy sampling": the panel simply **endorsed, without re-grading, the existing 3-to-5-year interval** of previous clinical practice guidelines, based on the low NDBE progression rate (.1%-.3% per year), "until more evidence is available." Likewise the **Seattle protocol** is carried over from prior documents as the criterion standard against which every PICO was judged — in Methods, white-light endoscopy (WLE) with targeted biopsy sampling of all visible abnormalities plus random 4-quadrant biopsy sampling every 1 to 2 cm from the top of the gastric folds to the most proximal extent of BE; in the Question 1 discussion, 4-quadrant sampling at **2-cm intervals without dysplasia** and **1-cm intervals with prior dysplasia**, plus targeted sampling of any mucosal abnormality.

## Key Findings / Claims (Recommendations — near-verbatim, with strength and quality)

**Summary of recommendations — all 7 statements, numbered and graded exactly as in Table 4 of the document** (the document's only strength/quality vocabulary is Strong / Conditional / No recommendation and High / Moderate / Low / Very low / NA):

| # | Statement (Table 4 wording) | Strength | Quality of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | In patients with nondysplastic BE, we suggest performing surveillance endoscopy compared with no surveillance. | Conditional | Very low |
| 2 | There is insufficient evidence on the effectiveness of screening for BE. However, if screening endoscopy for BE is performed, we suggest a screening strategy that identifies an at-risk population. An at-risk population is defined as individuals with a family history of EAC or BE (high risk) or patients with GERD plus at least 1 other risk factor (moderate risk). | NA | NA |
| 3 | In patients with BE undergoing surveillance, we recommend using chromoendoscopy, including virtual chromoendoscopy and Seattle protocol biopsy sampling, compared with white-light endoscopy with Seattle protocol biopsy sampling. | Strong | Moderate |
| 4 | In patients with BE undergoing surveillance, we suggest against routine use of confocal laser endomicroscopy compared with white-light endoscopy with Seattle protocol biopsy sampling. | Conditional | Low |
| 5 | In BE patients with high-grade dysplasia (HGD)/IMC [intramucosal cancer] or nodules, we recommend against routine use of EUS to differentiate mucosal vs submucosal disease. | Strong | Moderate |
| 6a | In patients with known or suspected BE, we suggest using WATS-3D in addition to Seattle protocol biopsy sampling compared with white-light endoscopy with Seattle protocol biopsy sampling. | Conditional | Low |
| 6b | In patients with BE undergoing surveillance, there is insufficient evidence to recommend for or against routine use of VLE. | No recommendation | NA |

Points where the in-text recommendation box differs from Table 4 — both wordings are in the document:

- **Statement 3 (in text):** "we recommend using CE or VC **in addition to** WLE and biopsy specimens obtained using the Seattle protocol compared with WLE and biopsy specimens obtained using the Seattle protocol alone." Chromoendoscopy (CE) / virtual chromoendoscopy (VC) is an addition to the protocol, not a substitute for it — Table 4's compressed wording can read otherwise.
- **Statement 5 (in text):** the population is "BE patients **with dysplasia or early EAC**"; Table 4 says "high-grade dysplasia/IMC or nodules."
- **Statement 6b (in text):** "There is insufficient evidence to make a recommendation for or against routine use of VLE in surveillance of patients with BE (no recommendation)."
- Additional risk factors for Statement 2 are given in the Question 2 text, not in Table 4: age >50, obesity/central adiposity, history of smoking, male gender.
- The panel made **no specific recommendation** on which type of VC or which dye to use for CE, and **did not recommend CE as a replacement for the Seattle protocol** — only as an adjunct.

**Supporting evidence by question:**

- **Q1 — NDBE surveillance vs none** (built on the Codipilly systematic review (SR) and meta-analysis (MA); all evidence observational, certainty **very low** for every outcome in the evidence profile):
  - EAC-related mortality, unadjusted: surveillance vs no/incomplete surveillance, 4 studies, relative risk (RR) .60 (.50-.71); EAC patients with a prior BE diagnosis, 4 studies, RR .73 (.57-.94).
  - EAC-related mortality **adjusted for lead-time bias, stage and treatment**: 1 study (El-Serag) — the association with surveillance "was no longer found." *The document gives two different point estimates for this study: hazard ratio (HR) 1.72 (.78-2.07) in the text and HR 1.27 (.78-2.07) in the evidence profile; the confidence interval is the same and crosses 1 either way.* A second lead-time–adjusted study (Tramontano) gave HR .89 (.78-1.01).
  - All-cause mortality: unadjusted MA of 3 studies HR .75 (.59-.94) (a 25% reduction); 12 studies in EAC patients with a prior BE diagnosis RR .48 (.37-.63). Pooling the 3 studies with lead-time–adjusted survival gave HR **.85 (.75-.95)** — the document calls this an **attenuation** of the mortality benefit, not its disappearance.
  - Stage at diagnosis: surveillance → earlier stage, 4 studies RR 2.11 (1.08-4.11); prior BE diagnosis → earlier stage, 9 studies RR 5.52 (3.70-8.24).
  - Adverse events (AEs) of diagnostic esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) are infrequent: overall 1/200 to 1/10,000; in the evidence profile, cardiopulmonary 1/170 to 1/10,000 and perforation 1/2500 to 1/11,000. AE rate is higher if EET is performed.
  - Cost-effectiveness: 9 Markov-simulation studies; at a willingness-to-pay threshold of <$100,000 per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY), all found endoscopic screening and surveillance of GERD patients cost-effective (5 studies: $4000-$15,000 per QALY).
  - NDBE progression to EAC .1%-.3% per year → basis for endorsing the pre-existing **3-to-5-year** interval. Risk-prediction models are developing (one scoring system on male sex, smoking, BE length, baseline low-grade dysplasia (LGD) stratifies HGD/EAC risk) but need external validation.
  - The awaited RCT is the Barrett's Oesophagus Surveillance Study (BOSS) in the United Kingdom: intensive surveillance every 2 years vs "at need" surveillance.
- **Q2 — Screening / at-risk population:** No studies compared screening with no screening. The panel set a **cut-off prevalence of 10% BE in any given group** as the bar for recommending screening.
  - Risk factors for *having* BE (Table 6): GERD odds ratio (OR) 2.9 (1.86-4.54), rising to 4.5 in 12 studies requiring GERD for at least 2 weeks; family history of BE or EAC OR 12 (adjusted for age, sex, obesity); male-to-female ratio 2.13; obesity OR 1.15 (.89-1.47) adjusting for GERD — **not significant**; central adiposity OR 1.44 (1.2-1.74); smoking OR 1.42 (1.15-1.76) ever-smoked vs population control, OR 1.96 (1.41-2.73) adjusted for confounders.
  - Risk of BE rises **linearly with the number of risk factors — ~1.2% per additional factor**; in that analysis all patients with multiple risk factors had either GERD or age >50.
  - Risk factors for *progression* (separate list): increasing age OR 1.03 (1.01-1.05), male sex OR 2.16 (1.84-2.53), ever smoking OR 1.47 (1.09-1.98), increasing BE length OR 1.25 (1.16-1.36), LGD OR 4.25 (2.58-7.0). Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs; OR .55, .32-.96) and statins (OR .48, .31-.73) are linked to decreased progression.
  - A randomized factorial trial of high-dose PPI with aspirin in BE improved a composite endpoint of all-cause mortality, HGD and EAC (time ratio 1.59; 1.14-2.23; P = .0068).
  - Why GERD-triggered screening misses people: <10% of EAC patients have a prior BE diagnosis; only **7%-10%** of individuals with chronic GERD have BE; **nearly 40%** of EAC patients report no prior GERD history; **nearly 50%** of short-segment BE patients have no GERD symptoms. Pooled BE prevalence at screening-colonoscopy EGD exceeded 10%. The panel deliberated broadening the at-risk group to multiple risk factors independent of GERD but judged the resulting volume, cost and harm unacceptable.
  - Risk-prediction tools using demographic/historical data have only modest accuracy (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) **.61 to .75**) and need refinement and validation before widespread use.
  - Non-endoscopic modalities (transnasal endoscopy, esophageal capsule endoscopy, tethered capsule endomicroscopy, electronic nose): the guideline **did not conduct an SR/MA of them** — out of scope. It notes standard upper endoscopy was the most common screening tool in the literature and that an American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) Clinical Practice Update reviewed these technologies and recommended against the use of any alternative test to screen for BE at that time.
- **Q3 — CE/VC added to WLE + Seattle, vs WLE + Seattle alone** (12 RCTs, n = 2433, mostly crossover/tandem; evidence profile RR 1.31 [1.21-1.41], certainty **Moderate**, rated down for indirectness because the trials came from tertiary expert centers):
  - Absolute increase in dysplasia detection **9% (4.1%-14%; P < .001)**; relative increase **30.3% (16.2%-44.3%; P < .0001)**.
  - Subanalysis: **no significant difference** between VC (risk difference 28.5%, 12.3%-45.5%) and dye-based CE (30.7%, 5.2%-56.3%; P = .98).
  - Performance (ASGE Technology Committee SR/MA, per-lesion not per-patient): conventional dye CE sensitivity 91.9%, negative predictive value (NPV) 95.5%, specificity 89.9%; acetic acid sensitivity 96.6%, NPV 98.3%, specificity 84.6%; narrow-band imaging (NBI)/VC sensitivity 94.2%, NPV 97.5%, specificity 94.4%. **Acetic acid is the only dye-based CE technique meeting the ASGE Preservation and Incorporation of Valuable Endoscopic Innovations (PIVI) thresholds**; NBI/VC also exceeds them.
  - Considerations: VC is essentially cost-equivalent (built into most endoscopes) and risk-free, so VC is the first choice; dye-based CE is cost-effective and usable when VC is unavailable, but some dyes carry potential risks including DNA damage. Number of biopsy specimens correlated strongly with yield — hence CE as adjunct, not replacement.
- **Q4 — CLE vs WLE + Seattle** (4 RCTs; evidence profile RR 1.50 [.93-2.08], certainty **Low**):
  - Absolute increase in diagnostic yield 10.2% (1.4%-19.1%; P = .024) — confidence interval very close to zero. Relative increase **not significant**: 36% (-5.4% to 77.5%; P = .088).
  - Performance: Xiong SR/MA (7 studies, 473 patients) pooled sensitivity 89% (82%-94%), specificity 83% (78%-86%). ASGE Technology Committee pooled estimate combining endoscope-based and probe-based CLE: sensitivity 90.4%, NPV 96.2%, specificity 89.9% — **did not meet the ASGE PIVI thresholds**. Endoscope-based CLE alone did meet them, but is no longer available clinically.
  - False-positive rate stays high across plausible prevalences: 16.3% (13.4%-21.1%) at 5% prevalence, still 12% at 30%.
  - The 4 RCTs had dysplasia prevalence 19%-35%, far above the prevalence most gastroenterologists see at nonspecialized centers → lower real-world yield. AEs minor only (1.4% in 2272 procedures: epigastric pain, nausea/vomiting, rash, injection-site erythema, transient hypotension); not cost-effective vs WLE + Seattle in a Markov cost-utility analysis.
  - Thresholds: the panel assumed a minimum **20% relative increase** in diagnostic yield would warrant recommending CLE; an international expert survey put the minimum incremental diagnostic yield for CLE at **27% (interquartile range 20%-50%)**. Neither was met. The guideline still acknowledges CLE may help in centers with high dysplasia prevalence and significant local expertise.
- **Q5 — EUS for mucosal (T1a) vs submucosal (T1b) disease** (2 MAs, 11 studies; evidence profile certainty **Moderate**, rated down for inconsistency):
  - Pooled sensitivity **41% (35%-48%)**, specificity 89% (86%-91%), accuracy 75% (59%-86%).
  - Prevalence of advanced disease in the included studies ranged 5%-45%; false-positive rate 6% at high prevalence to 10.5% at low prevalence. **About one third of patients who test EUS-positive for advanced disease will be false positives.**
  - The panel's reasoning: possible AEs of EUS (cervical esophageal perforation .03%-.06%, mortality .002%, bleeding after fine-needle aspiration (FNA) .13%, rare tumor seeding), cost of procedure and sedation, plus that high false-positive rate.
  - Explicitly in line with the 2018 ASGE EET guideline, which strongly recommends endoscopic resection of all visible lesions as first-line for both diagnosis and therapy. **EUS should not be used for T staging of early disease instead of endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) or endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD).** The guideline states this should not be confused with the role of EUS in EAC: EUS *is* indicated for accurate staging of advanced cancer (≥T1b) and to evaluate for nodal disease.
- **Q6a — WATS-3D added to WLE + Seattle** (6 studies — 5 trials and 1 meeting abstract; 6271 BE patients; evidence profile RR 2.25 [1.79-2.83], certainty **Low**, rated down for risk of bias, inconsistency and indirectness; all studies manufacturer-funded and the dysplasia definition varied):
  - WLE with random biopsy sampling detected 125 cases of dysplasia; WATS identified **137 additional cases** missed by it.
  - Overall relative increase in dysplasia detection **48% (34%-60%)**.
  - Patients **with a history of dysplasia**: relative increase 47% (32%-61%); absolute increase 10.6% (1.5%-19.8%); relative increase in LGD detection 21%.
  - **All-comers** (with or without a history of dysplasia): relative increase 52% (21%-82%); absolute increase **2% (1.5%-2.5%)**. Absolute increase in LGD detection 1.8% (1.4%-2.3%).
  - Much of the added detection is therefore LGD, and the document states the open question plainly: what LGD on cytology means compared with LGD on histology.
  - Threshold met: an international expert survey set the minimum incremental diagnostic yield for WATS-3D at **27% (20%-50%)**; the pooled results exceed it.
  - Safety/cost: **no cost-effectiveness studies** and no patient-preference data. One abstract surveying 33 physicians using WATS-3D in >4881 cases reported a serious adverse-outcome rate of **.06%**.
  - Process note: the panel initially made **no recommendation** for WATS-3D at the face-to-face meeting; after reviewing additional published literature and AE data on a later phone conference it issued the conditional recommendation.
- **Q6b — VLE:** No recommendation; overall quality of evidence **very low**.
  - An existing SR/MA available only in abstract form (4 abstracts) gave a pooled absolute increase in dysplasia detection of 2.3% (1.5%-3.4%) with a **44% false-positive rate** — the panel declined to rely on it (abstract only, overlapping databases).
  - Only 1 study of the newer VLE-laser device (historical comparison, 386 patients): overall dysplasia detection 33.7% with VLE laser vs 19.6% with the Seattle protocol (Table 12: random biopsies 5.7%, Seattle protocol 19.6%, VLE 24.8%, VLE laser 33.7%); in post-treatment surveillance 32.7% vs 8.3% (P = .02); in treatment-naive patients undergoing surveillance the document reports the rate was **not different** between VLE laser and the Seattle protocol group (16.7% vs 35.3%).
  - Expert threshold not demonstrated: international BE experts set a minimum incremental diagnostic yield of **30% (18%-50%)** for VLE in routine practice; validation in large prospective trials is required.
  - AEs not detailed, but the procedure involves balloon inflation with potential for pain or perforation, and adds device, inflation and interpretation time; cost unclear.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Concordant with [[acg-2022-barretts]] and [[aga-2025-barretts-surveillance]] on the Seattle protocol, chromoendoscopy, and risk-factor-based screening.
- **Non-endoscopic screening devices:** ASGE 2019 took no position of its own — it did not review these modalities (out of scope) and cites the AGA Clinical Practice Update's recommendation against them at the time. American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2022 subsequently endorses swallowable cell-collection devices as an acceptable alternative. Not a true contradiction: one society declined to review the question, the other answered it.
- **Internal discrepancy in the document:** the lead-time–adjusted EAC-mortality HR from El-Serag is reported as 1.72 (.78-2.07) in the text and 1.27 (.78-2.07) in the evidence profile.
- **Internal discrepancy in the document:** the Statement 5 population is "high-grade dysplasia/IMC or nodules" in Table 4 and "dysplasia or early EAC" in the in-text recommendation.
- Surveillance mortality benefit is **attenuated, not abolished**, after lead-time adjustment (pooled all-cause HR .85, .75-.95); single-study lead-time–adjusted EAC-mortality estimates lost significance. No RCT exists; the BOSS result was awaited at publication.
- ASGE 2019 does **not** define BE or state whether intestinal metaplasia is required for the diagnosis — the question is outside its scope. Do not read a position into its silence.
- The guideline does **not** grade surveillance intervals or the biopsy protocol; it carries both over from earlier documents. Any wiki claim that ASGE 2019 "recommends" a 3-to-5-year interval overstates it — the panel *endorsed* the existing recommendation pending better evidence.
