## Summary

The current **surgical** guideline for hiatal hernia, complementing — not replacing — the society's **2013** document (Kohn GP et al., *Surg Endosc* 2013;27:4409–28 — in `raw/` but not yet ingested), which it describes as last revised in 2013 with new publications since accumulated. Scope is deliberately narrow: **the surgical management of type II, III and IV hiatal hernias** in adults, addressed through **four key questions**. Type I (sliding) hernia is described but is not the subject of the recommendations.

What the panel actually produced is unusually modest, and the modesty is the finding: **one conditional, low-certainty recommendation** (perform a fundoplication during repair), **one explicit decision not to make a recommendation at all** (mesh), and **two expert opinions** where the literature was too weak to support anything else (repair of an asymptomatic hernia; conversion to Roux-en-Y gastric bypass for recurrence). Certainty of evidence was **low for every key question**.

The most clinically useful content is therefore in the expert-opinion sections, which give **criteria rather than thresholds** — how to decide whether a patient is genuinely asymptomatic, and which recurrent-hernia patients should be converted to gastric bypass rather than redone.

## Key Findings / Claims

### The four types

- **Type I** — *sliding*: the gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) sits above the hiatus.
- **Type II** — the **gastric fundus** herniates above the hiatus with the **GEJ in normal position**.
- **Type III** — a combination: **both the stomach and the GEJ** lie above the hiatus.
- **Type IV** — **any additional organ** is herniated alongside the stomach.

Hiatal hernia is closely associated with [[gerd|gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)]] and its long-term complications, [[barretts-esophagus|Barrett's esophagus]] and [[esophageal-adenocarcinoma|esophageal adenocarcinoma]]; **loss of the "pinch" effect of the crural diaphragm** is a significant contributor to the reflux seen in hiatal hernia.

### The four recommendations, exactly as graded

| KQ | Question (adults, type II/III/IV) | What the panel said | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Mesh versus no mesh during repair | **The panel decided not to make an evidence-based recommendation for or against mesh.** | Evidence equivocal; deferred |
| **2** | Repair versus continued surveillance for an **asymptomatic** hernia | **No evidence-based recommendation possible — expert opinion only:** select asymptomatic patients **may be offered** repair, against stated criteria | Expert opinion (no literature on truly asymptomatic patients managed either way) |
| **3** | Fundoplication versus no fundoplication during repair | **Suggests** patients undergoing repair **may benefit from surgical fundoplication** compared with no fundoplication | **Conditional recommendation, low certainty** |
| **4** | Conversion to Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) versus redo repair for **recurrent** hernia in patients **without obesity** | **No evidence-based recommendation possible — expert opinion only:** conversion **may be appropriate in certain patients**, against stated criteria | Expert opinion (2 retrospective cohorts + 5 single-arm observational studies, judged too weak) |

> **Note the force of KQ1.** This is a **decision not to recommend**, not a recommendation against. The panel considered conditionally recommending against routine mesh — the benefits and harms appeared closely balanced, and "if it truly makes no difference, why add an extra step?" — but the confidence intervals were wide enough that mesh could be anywhere from quite beneficial to harmful, and **the decision must remain a joint one between surgeon and patient**.

### KQ1 — mesh: what the data showed

Ten RCTs informed the question; the main limitation was that **studies were often underpowered**, so confidence intervals frequently crossed significance.

- **Six outcomes favoured mesh; only one reached statistical significance** — **late total dysphagia**, relative risk (RR) **0.49**, translating to **108 fewer patients with late dysphagia per 1000** treated with mesh (95% CI 153 fewer to 30 fewer).
- Favouring mesh but **not** significant: hiatal hernia recurrence (symptomatic or large) RR **0.98** (5 RCTs, 410 patients) — 4 fewer recurrences per 1000; return to the operating room RR **0.75** (6 RCTs, 587 patients) — 14 fewer per 1000; early unresolved dysphagia RR **0.86** (2 RCTs, 38 patients) — 74 fewer per 1000; and postoperative quality of life (4 RCTs; 2 of 4 instruments favoured mesh, 2 showed no difference).
- **Four outcomes favoured repair without mesh, none significant:** Clavien–Dindo grade ≥3 complications RR **1.36** (4 RCTs, 462 patients) — 19 more per 1000; **early new** dysphagia RR **4.75** (1 RCT, 37 patients — 2/19 with mesh vs 0/18 without, and **it resolved in all patients on longer follow-up**); early total dysphagia RR **3.00** (1 RCT, 100 patients) — 80 more per 1000; late unresolved dysphagia RR **1.51** (2 RCTs, 38 patients).
- **Across 3 RCTs and 270 patients there were no episodes of mesh erosion.**
- **The panel judged the magnitude of benefit moderate and the magnitude of harm small**, and still declined to recommend.
- **What the recommendation explicitly does not address:** the difference between cases where the **crura could be reapproximated** and those where mesh was used to **bridge a crura that could not be closed**. Also not objectively evaluable: the **tension on the crural repair**, the **use and need for a relaxing incision**, and the **tissue quality of the crura** — all named as subgroups warranting additional consideration.

### KQ2 — repairing an asymptomatic hernia (expert opinion)

- **Step one is to establish the patient is truly asymptomatic.** Many patients referred for an "asymptomatic" hiatal hernia are in fact suffering **non-gastrointestinal symptoms secondary to the hernia** — the panel names **shortness of breath, exercise intolerance, and abnormal echocardiogram findings**. Where these cannot be reasonably attributed to a comorbid process, **the patient can be considered symptomatic and offered repair if medically fit**.
- Presenting symptoms in a 270-patient series undergoing repair: **anemia 24%–57%**, **dyspnea 21%–67%**, **chest pain 40%–60%**.
- **Symptom report and objective reflux often disagree.** The panel cites the Lyon consensus: a **history taken by a gastroenterologist has a sensitivity of 70% and a specificity of 67%** for GERD against pH monitoring or endoscopy. Surgical repair can prevent progression and the complications of reflux disease and/or the hernia itself. **Micro-aspiration is another potential sequela of an untreated hiatal hernia, and endoscopy is a good diagnostic test for both.**
- **Patients with objective evidence of reflux or findings of micro-aspiration can be offered repair if medically fit.** Certain populations are at such high risk of micro-aspiration complications that **repair can be offered even without objective findings — lung transplant patients are the named example**.
- **Where there is no objective evidence of reflux or micro-aspiration**, the management is controversial and **there are no strong data**. Required: a thorough discussion of the potential to develop an **acute gastric volvulus**, and a frank discussion of the **inability to predict who is at high risk** for it — though the panel does consider **pre-existing organo-axial rotation a concerning feature**.
- **If the workup is unrevealing and the patient understands and accepts the volvulus risk, watchful waiting is reasonable.** Three modelling studies exist: two found overall benefit favouring watchful waiting (acknowledging a proportion will cross over to surgery), the third found quality of life favoured operative intervention. The panel discounted all three — they are models, built on **symptomatic** populations, so **if anything the theoretical benefit of watchful waiting is greater still in an asymptomatic one**.
- **Age alone must not disqualify a patient from operative management** (health-equity consideration); decide on **operative risk and the individual's values**.

### KQ3 — fundoplication during repair (conditional, low certainty)

- Evidence: 2 RCTs and 6 observational studies. **All the directly comparative data used a complete, not partial, fundoplication.**
- **Benefits, all favouring fundoplication:** objective reflux RR **0.31** (2 RCTs, 147 patients) — **321 fewer patients per 1000 with objective evidence of reflux** (95% CI 386 fewer to 205 fewer); hiatal hernia recurrence (symptomatic or large) RR **0.55** (2 RCTs, 148 patients) — 51 fewer recurrences per 1000; postoperative **leak** rate RR **0.33** (1 RCT, 40 patients) — 33 fewer leaks per 1000; quality of life improved by **1.5 points on the GERD Health Related Quality of Life scale** (1 observational study, 58 patients, **not statistically significant**). **Combined magnitude judged large.**
- **The one harm: late total dysphagia**, RR **2.94** (2 RCTs, 157 patients) — **25 more patients with dysphagia per 1000** (95% CI 7 fewer to 225 more). **Magnitude judged small.**
- **Partial fundoplication may be the better option for postoperative dysphagia** — the panel had **no high-level evidence in the hernia-repair population** and applied indirect evidence from GERD patients without hiatal hernia, where dysphagia rates are lower with partial than with complete fundoplication. **Additional considerations: the relative severity of the patient's heartburn versus their dysphagia, and the type of fundoplication to be performed.**

### KQ4 — conversion to RYGB for recurrent hernia (expert opinion)

In a patient **without obesity** with a recurrent type II/III/IV hernia:

- **After a previous *uncomplicated* hiatal hernia repair with fundoplication, redo repair and fundoplication is appropriate.**
- **Greater consideration for RYGB** in these circumstances, as the panel lists them:
  - **Diabetes mellitus**
  - **Severe esophageal dysmotility**
  - **Short esophagus**
  - **[[gastroparesis|Gastroparesis]]**
  - **Previous complicated hiatal hernia repair with fundoplication leaving poor quality tissue of the fundus**
  - **Multiple recurrent hiatal hernia failures by an expert** — i.e. failure of a prior attempt by an expert surgeon
- **Such patients should be evaluated in a multidisciplinary fashion before proceeding with RYGB.**

### Definitions the guideline sets for its own outcomes

- **"Recurrence (symptomatic or large)"** = recurrence measuring **>2 cm in any direction**, **or** symptomatic. Papers varied — some used radiologic or endoscopic size criteria, others the presence or absence of symptoms — and the panel harmonized them this way.
- **"Unresolved dysphagia"** = patients who had **preoperative** dysphagia. **"New dysphagia"** = patients **without** preoperative dysphagia. **"Total dysphagia"** includes both.
- **Early dysphagia = 3 months to 1 year postoperatively; late = greater than 1 year.**

> ⚠ The panel flags a risk of bias here: not all studies identified which patients had dysphagia at baseline, and even those that did were not designed to investigate it, so **baseline dysphagia may have been unbalanced between arms** — frequently introducing greater risk of bias for the new, unresolved and total dysphagia outcomes.

### Other stated points

- **Obesity is a common comorbidity in hiatal hernia and is known to increase recurrence after repair.** With new medical weight-loss therapies proliferating, the panel calls for study of **the body mass index thresholds at which patients may benefit from lifestyle, medical or surgical weight loss before hiatal hernia repair** — it sets none.
- **Panel composition is a stated limitation:** predominantly academic surgeons and endoscopists, so the members may not represent the practices of other societies and physicians; the importance weighting of patient-centred outcomes was decided by panel members rather than patient advocates, and **no patients participated** in creating the guideline.
- SAGES plans repeat literature searches **every three years**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Mesh is unresolved by design.** Anyone looking for a position will not find one here; the document is explicit that the necessary evidence is simply lacking. **Do not convert this into a recommendation against mesh on a later pass** — the panel considered and rejected exactly that.
- **No size threshold anywhere.** The guideline gives **no hernia size at which repair becomes indicated**, and names no body mass index threshold for pre-repair weight loss. These are stated research gaps, not omissions to be filled from elsewhere.
- **Partial versus complete fundoplication rests on indirect evidence** from GERD patients without hiatal hernia. The panel calls it reasonable to apply; it is not tested in this population.
- **No way to predict who will develop gastric volvulus.** The panel says so plainly and names only organo-axial rotation as a concerning feature. Any risk estimate on the wiki must come from elsewhere.
- **Relationship to the SAGES 2013 guideline:** this is explicitly a **complement** to it, not a replacement — it answers four questions, where the 2013 guideline is broader. Where both speak, 2024 is newer and governs; where only 2013 speaks, 2013 still stands.
