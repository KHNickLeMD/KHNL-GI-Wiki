## Summary

The acute hepatic porphyrias (AHP) are rare inborn errors of heme metabolism comprising **acute intermittent porphyria (AIP)**, **hereditary coproporphyria (HCP)**, **variegate porphyria (VP)**, and **5-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase deficiency porphyria (ALAD)**. Partially defective activity of heme-synthesis enzymes causes accumulation of the porphyrin precursors δ-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) and porphobilinogen (PBG), producing acute neurovisceral attacks — severe abdominal pain without peritoneal signs or cross-sectional imaging abnormalities, plus nausea, vomiting, constipation, muscle weakness, neuropathy, tachycardia, and hypertension. AIP is the most common form; symptomatic AHP has an estimated prevalence of ~1 in 100,000, though population genetic data suggest AIP pathogenic-variant prevalence of 1 in 1300–1785. About 90% of symptomatic patients are women aged 15–50 years; attacks are rare before menses and after menopause. Diagnosis is frequently delayed (~15 years on average).

Because systematic reviews were not performed, the 12 **Best Practice Advice (BPA)** statements carry no formal ratings of evidence quality or strength; they derive from published literature and expert opinion. The CPU addresses when to screen (women 15–50 y with unexplained recurrent severe abdominal pain), how to diagnose biochemically (random urine ALA, PBG, creatinine — elevated ≥5-fold ULN during attacks), how to confirm the specific type genetically (sequencing ALAD, HMBS, CPOX, PPOX), and how to manage acute attacks and provide long-term care.

Acute-attack cornerstones: discontinuation of porphyrinogenic drugs/chemicals, IV or oral dextrose, IV hemin (3–4 mg/kg/day, high-flow central vein), and analgesics/antiemetics with correction of hypertension, tachycardia, hyponatremia, and hypomagnesemia. Patients with recurrent attacks (≥4/year) should be considered for prophylaxis with IV hemin or subcutaneous givosiran (an ALAS1-targeting siRNA). Liver transplantation is curative but reserved for intractable, refractory disease. AHP carries long-term risks of systemic arterial hypertension, chronic kidney disease, chronic liver disease, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), mandating ongoing surveillance.

## Key Findings / Claims

- **Four AHP types** (Table 1): AIP (AD, *HMBS*), HCP (AD, *CPOX*), VP (AD, *PPOX*), ALAD (autosomal recessive, *ALAD*; <12 reported cases worldwide). AIP/HCP/VP autosomal dominant; AHP pathogenic variants cause ≥50% reduction in the corresponding enzyme's activity.
- **Penetrance is low:** ~1% of AIP gene carriers become symptomatic; >20% in families with symptomatic patients. HCP and VP have lower penetrance than AIP. >90% of symptomatic patients experience only 1 or a few lifetime attacks; 3%–5% have recurrent attacks (≥4/year).
- **Biochemical diagnosis:** random (spot) urine ALA, PBG, and creatinine. During acute attacks both ALA and PBG are elevated ≥5-fold ULN, normalized to creatinine; 24-hour collection not recommended. In ALAD porphyria only ALA is elevated (PBG normal). Urine porphyrins alone should NOT be used to screen — secondary porphyrinurias cause overdiagnosis.
- **Timing matters:** testing most useful during an acute attack; ALA/PBG remain elevated for months–years after an AIP attack but fall quickly after HCP/VP attacks. In asymptomatic sporadic AIP, 15%–44% have normal ALA/PBG. In recurrent attackers, ALA/PBG are usually elevated at baseline; normal levels likely rule out AHP.
- **Genetic confirmation:** sequence *ALAD, HMBS, CPOX, PPOX* → ALAD, AIP, HCP, VP respectively; whole-gene sequencing identifies 95%–99%. Screen first-degree relatives once the familial variant is known. Not for initial screening.
- **Acute-attack treatment:** IV hemin 3–4 mg/kg/day, once daily, typically 4 days (3–5 days), via high-flow central vein (PICC/central port) due to thrombophlebitis; heme bound to human serum albumin preferred. Collect random urine ALA/PBG/creatinine before starting hemin. Symptom relief typically 48–72 h.
- **Supportive care:** stop CYP450-inducing precipitants; analgesics and antiemetics; IV carbohydrate loading ~300 g/day; monitor/correct hyponatremia (SIADH — correct slowly) and hypomagnesemia. Seizures: barbiturates, hydantoins, carbamazepine, and valproic acid are contraindicated; magnesium sulfate, benzodiazepines, and levetiracetam appear safe.
- **Trigger avoidance:** alcohol, porphyrinogenic (CYP450-inducing) medications, sex hormones (progesterone), acute illness/infection, physical/psychological stress, tobacco, caloric deprivation. Safe/unsafe drug databases: porphyria.org and drugs-porphyria.org.
- **Prophylaxis (≥4 attacks/year):** off-label prophylactic IV hemin (monitor iron overload) or subcutaneous givosiran (ALAS1-specific siRNA-GalNAc; FDA/EMA approved — adults in US, ≥12 y in EU). Prescribe givosiran only for biochemically AND genetically confirmed recurrent attacks; avoid in pregnancy/pregnancy planning; monitor liver enzymes, BUN/creatinine, homocysteine, amylase, lipase. Menstrual-associated attacks: GnRH agonists (limited success).
- **Liver transplantation:** curative (corrects hepatic HMBS deficiency, normalizes ALA/PBG); reserved for intractable symptoms refractory to pharmacotherapy. European Liver Transplant Registry: 38 recipients; 1- and 5-year survival 92% and 82%; poorer outcomes with severe neuropathy/renal dysfunction. Genetically screen related living donors (asymptomatic *HMBS* variant donors → poor outcomes).
- **Long-term surveillance:** monitor liver disease annually (baseline aminotransferase elevations in 28% in ENVISION; abnormal LFTs warrant alternative-etiology workup). Chronic hemin (9% iron by weight) → iron overload; on ≥monthly prophylactic hemin check ferritin/iron every 3–6 months and consider therapeutic phlebotomy if ferritin >1000 ng/mL. Givosiran: ALT >3× ULN in first 3–6 months → monthly liver enzymes during that period.
- **HCC surveillance:** risk 1.5%–1.8%; begin at age 50 y with liver ultrasound every 6 months regardless of symptom severity. Female predominance 2.5×; HCC may occur without fibrosis/cirrhosis; AFP role unclear.
- **CKD surveillance:** annual serum creatinine and eGFR (more frequent on givosiran). CKD 29% and hypertension 43% (US Porphyrias Consortium); porphyria-associated kidney disease up to 59% of symptomatic AIP (eGFR decline ~1 mL/min/1.73 m²/year); EXPLORE — 68% reduced eGFR, 28% stage 3a/3b/4 CKD. Kidney transplant for ESRD; optimize blood pressure.

## Best Practice Advice Statements (verbatim)

The 12 BPA statements carry **no formal ratings** of evidence quality or strength (no systematic review performed).

1. **Best Practice Advice 1:** Women aged 15–50 years with unexplained, recurrent severe abdominal pain without a clear etiology after an initial workup should be considered for screening for an AHP.
2. **Best Practice Advice 2:** Initial diagnosis of AHP should be made by biochemical testing measuring δ-aminolevulinic acid, porphobilinogen, and creatinine on a random urine sample.
3. **Best Practice Advice 3:** Genetic testing should be used to confirm the diagnosis of AHP in patients with positive biochemical testing.
4. **Best Practice Advice 4:** Acute attacks of AHP that are severe enough to require hospital admission should be treated with intravenous hemin, given daily, preferably into a high-flow central vein.
5. **Best Practice Advice 5:** In addition to intravenous hemin, management of acute attacks of AHP should include pain control, antiemetics, management of systemic arterial hypertension, tachycardia, and hyponatremia, and hypomagnesemia, if present.
6. **Best Practice Advice 6:** Patients should be counseled to avoid identifiable triggers that may precipitate acute attacks, such as alcohol and porphyrinogenic medications.
7. **Best Practice Advice 7:** Prophylactic heme therapy or givosiran, administered in an outpatient setting, should be considered in patients with recurrent attacks (4 or more per year).
8. **Best Practice Advice 8:** Liver transplantation for AHP should be limited to patients with intractable symptoms and significantly decreased quality of life who are refractory to pharmacotherapy.
9. **Best Practice Advice 9:** Patients with AHP should be monitored annually for liver disease.
10. **Best Practice Advice 10:** Patients with AHP, regardless of the severity of symptoms, should undergo surveillance for hepatocellular carcinoma, beginning at age 50 years, with liver ultrasound every 6 months.
11. **Best Practice Advice 11:** Patients with AHP on treatment should undergo surveillance for chronic kidney disease annually with serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate.
12. **Best Practice Advice 12:** Patients should be counseled on the chronic and long-term complications of AHP, including neuropathy, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, and hepatocellular carcinoma, and need for long-term monitoring.
