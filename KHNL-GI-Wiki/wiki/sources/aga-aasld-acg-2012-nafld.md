## Summary

The **oldest document in the wiki corpus** and the founding tri-society (AGA–AASLD–ACG) practice guideline on nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). It is GRADE-rated using the AASLD Practice Guidelines Committee's modification: **strength is strong (1) or weak (2)**, and **quality of evidence is high (A), moderate (B), or low (C)** — so a rating reads "(Strength – 1, Evidence – A)". Recommendations are numbered **1 through 45** by the document itself.

It established the definitional architecture still in use: NAFLD requires (a) hepatic steatosis by imaging or histology and (b) no cause of secondary hepatic fat accumulation — significant alcohol consumption, steatogenic medication, or hereditary disorder. NAFLD is split histologically into **nonalcoholic fatty liver (NAFL)** — steatosis with no hepatocellular injury in the form of ballooning — and **nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)** — steatosis and inflammation with hepatocyte injury (ballooning), with or without fibrosis. The natural history is described as "fairly dichotomous": NAFL is generally benign; NASH can progress to cirrhosis, liver failure, and liver cancer.

Its durable clinical contributions are the **alcohol threshold defining NAFLD**, the **incidental-steatosis workup**, the position **against population or family screening**, the use of **metabolic syndrome and the NAFLD Fibrosis Score to select patients for biopsy**, the **weight-loss dose–response** (3%–5% for steatosis, up to 10% for necroinflammation), and the **pharmacotherapy positions** that defined the field for a decade: pioglitazone and vitamin E 800 IU/day in biopsy-proven NASH, metformin and ursodeoxycholic acid rejected, statins declared safe.

**Superseded — read for what changed, not for current practice.** Under source priority (newer wins within tier), [[aasld-2023-nafld]] and the later AGA Clinical Practice Updates supersede this guideline on every overlapping claim, including the disease name itself (now metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease). It is ingested for historical and what-changed value. Where it is still the better source is in the **grading and explicit wording of the 2012 positions** that modern readers may still carry.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definitions (Table 3, verbatim content)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **NAFLD** | "Encompasses the entire spectrum of fatty liver disease in individuals without significant alcohol consumption, ranging from fatty liver to steatohepatitis and cirrhosis." |
| **NAFL** | "Presence of hepatic steatosis with no evidence of hepatocellular injury in the form of ballooning of the hepatocytes or no evidence of fibrosis. The risk of progression to cirrhosis and liver failure is minimal." |
| **NASH** | "Presence of hepatic steatosis and inflammation with hepatocyte injury (ballooning) with or without fibrosis. This can progress to cirrhosis, liver failure and rarely liver cancer." |
| **NASH cirrhosis** | "Presence of cirrhosis with current or previous histological evidence of steatosis or steatohepatitis." |
| **Cryptogenic cirrhosis** | "Presence of cirrhosis with no obvious etiology. Patients with cryptogenic cirrhosis are heavily enriched with metabolic risk factors such as obesity and metabolic syndrome." |
| **NAFLD Activity Score (NAS)** | "An unweighted composite of steatosis, inflammation, and ballooning scores. It is a useful tool to measure changes in liver histology in patients with NAFLD in clinical trials." |

**Risk factors (Table 4).** Established association: obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, metabolic syndrome. Emerging association: polycystic ovary syndrome, hypothyroidism, obstructive sleep apnea, hypopituitarism, hypogonadism, pancreatoduodenal resection.

**Metabolic syndrome — the Adult Treatment Panel III definition used by this guideline** requires **three or more** of: (1) waist circumference >102 cm in men or >88 cm in women; (2) triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL; (3) high-density lipoprotein cholesterol <40 mg/dL in men, <50 mg/dL in women; (4) systolic blood pressure ≥130 mm Hg or diastolic ≥85 mm Hg; (5) fasting plasma glucose ≥110 mg/dL.

### Recommendations 1–45 (verbatim, with the document's own numbering and ratings)

**Defining significant alcohol consumption**
1. "Ongoing or recent alcohol consumption > 21 drinks on average per week in men and > 14 drinks on average per week in women is a reasonable definition for significant alcohol consumption when evaluating patients with suspected NAFLD in clinical practice." (Strength – 2, Quality – C)

**Incidentally discovered hepatic steatosis**
2. "When patients with unsuspected hepatic steatosis detected on imaging have symptoms or signs attributable to liver disease or have abnormal liver biochemistries, they should be evaluated as though they have suspected NAFLD and worked-up accordingly." (Strength – 1, Evidence – A)
3. "In patients with unsuspected hepatic steatosis detected on imaging who lack any liver-related symptoms or signs and have normal liver biochemistries, it is reasonable to assess for metabolic risk factors (e.g., obesity, glucose intolerance, dyslipidemia) and alternate causes for hepatic steatosis such as significant alcohol consumption or medications." (Strength – 1, Evidence – A)
4. "In patients with unsuspected hepatic steatosis detected on imaging who are asymptomatic and have normal liver biochemistries, a liver biopsy cannot be recommended." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)

**Screening**
5. "Screening for NAFLD in adults attending primary care clinics or high-risk groups attending diabetes or obesity clinics is not advised at this time due to uncertainties surrounding diagnostic tests and treatment options, along with lack of knowledge related to the long-term benefits and cost-effectiveness of screening." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)
6. "Systematic screening of family members for NAFLD is currently not recommended." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)

**Initial evaluation**
7. "When evaluating a patient with suspected NAFLD, it is essential to exclude competing etiologies for steatosis and co-existing common chronic liver disease." (Strength – 1, Evidence – A)
8. "Persistently high serum ferritin and increased iron saturation, especially in the context of homozygote or heterozygote C282Y HFE mutations may warrant a liver biopsy." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)
9. "High serum titers of autoantibodies in association with other features suggestive of autoimmune liver disease (very high aminotransferases, high globulin) should prompt a more complete work-up for autoimmune liver disease." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)

**Noninvasive assessment of steatohepatitis and advanced fibrosis**
10. "As the metabolic syndrome predicts the presence of steatohepatitis in patients with NAFLD, its presence can be used to target patients for a liver biopsy." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)
11. "NAFLD Fibrosis Score is a clinically useful tool for identifying NAFLD patients with higher likelihood of having bridging fibrosis and/or cirrhosis." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)
12. "Although serum/plasma CK18 is a promising biomarker for identifying steatohepatitis, it is premature to recommend in routine clinical practice." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)

**When to obtain a liver biopsy**
13. "Liver biopsy should be considered in patients with NAFLD who are at increased risk to have steatohepatitis and advanced fibrosis." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)
14. "The presence of metabolic syndrome and the NAFLD Fibrosis Score may be used for identifying patients who are at risk for steatohepatitis and advanced fibrosis." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)
15. "Liver biopsy should be considered in patients with suspected NAFLD in whom competing etiologies for hepatic steatosis and co-existing chronic liver diseases cannot be excluded without a liver biopsy." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)

**Lifestyle intervention**
16. "Weight loss generally reduces hepatic steatosis, achieved either by hypocaloric diet alone or in conjunction with increased physical activity." (Strength – 1, Evidence – A)
17. "Loss of at least 3–5% of body weight appears necessary to improve steatosis, but a greater weight loss (up to 10%) may be needed to improve necroinflammation." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)
18. "Exercise alone in adults with NAFLD may reduce hepatic steatosis but its ability to improve other aspects of liver histology remains unknown." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)

**Metformin**
19. "Metformin has no significant effect on liver histology and is not recommended as a specific treatment for liver disease in adults with NASH." (Strength – 1, Evidence – A)

**Thiazolidinediones**
20. "Pioglitazone can be used to treat steatohepatitis in patients with biopsy-proven NASH. However, it should be noted that majority of the patients who participated in clinical trials that investigated pioglitazone for NASH were non-diabetic and that long term safety and efficacy of pioglitazone in patients with NASH is not established." (Strength – 1, Evidence – B)

**Vitamin E**
21. "Vitamin E (α-tocopherol) administered at daily dose of 800 IU/day improves liver histology in non-diabetic adults with biopsy-proven NASH and therefore it should be considered as a first-line pharmacotherapy for this patient population." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
22. "Until further data supporting its effectiveness become available, vitamin E is not recommended to treat NASH in diabetic patients, NAFLD without liver biopsy, NASH cirrhosis, or cryptogenic cirrhosis." (Strength – 1, Quality – C)

**Ursodeoxycholic acid, omega-3 fatty acids**
23. "UDCA is not recommended for the treatment of NAFLD or NASH." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
24. "It is premature to recommend omega-3 fatty acids for the specific treatment of NAFLD or NASH but they may be considered as the first line agents to treat hypertriglyceridemia in patients with NAFLD." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)

**Bariatric surgery**
25. "Foregut bariatric surgery is not contraindicated in otherwise eligible obese individuals with NAFLD or NASH (but without established cirrhosis)." (Strength – 1, Quality – A)
26. "The type, safety and efficacy of foregut bariatric surgery in otherwise eligible obese individuals with established cirrhosis due to NAFLD are not established." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
27. "It is premature to consider foregut bariatric surgery as an established option to specifically treat NASH." (1B)

**Alcohol use**
28. "Patients with NAFLD should not consume heavy amounts of alcohol." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
29. "No recommendation can be made with regards to non-heavy consumption of alcohol by individuals with NAFLD." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)

**Statins**
30. "Given the lack of evidence to show that patients with NAFLD and NASH are at increased risk for serious drug-induced liver injury from statins, statins can be used to treat dyslipidemia in patients with NAFLD and NASH." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
31. "Until RCTs with histological endpoints prove their efficacy, statins should not be used to specifically treat NASH." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)

**NAFLD with other chronic liver disease**
32. "When steatosis and steatohepatitis are evident in patients with other types of chronic liver disease, it is important to assess for metabolic risk factors and alternate etiologies for hepatic steatosis." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
33. "In patients with other types of chronic liver diseases who have co-existing NAFLD and NASH, there are no data to support the use of vitamin E or pioglitazone to improve the liver disease." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)

**Miscellaneous (cirrhosis surveillance, repeat biopsy)**
34. "Patients with NASH cirrhosis should be screened for gastroesophageal varices according to the AASLD/ACG practice guidelines." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
35. "Patients with NASH cirrhosis should be considered for HCC screening according to the AASLD/ACG practice guidelines." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
36. "Current evidence does not support routinely repeating a liver biopsy in patients with NAFL or NASH." (Strength – 2, Quality – C)

**Children and adolescents**
37. "Children with fatty liver who are very young or not overweight should be tested for monogenic causes of chronic liver disease such as fatty acid oxidation defects, lysosomal storage diseases and peroxisomal disorders, in addition to those causes considered for adults." (Strength – 2, Quality – C)
38. "Low serum titers of autoantibodies are often present in children with NAFLD, but higher titers, particularly in association with higher serum aminotransferases and high globulin should prompt a liver biopsy to evaluate for possible autoimmune hepatitis." (Strength – 2, Quality – B)
39. "Due to a paucity of evidence, a formal recommendation cannot be made with regards to screening for NAFLD in overweight and obese children despite a recent expert committee recommendation for biannual screening for liver disease with liver enzyme measurements in this population." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
40. "Liver biopsy in children with suspected NAFLD should be performed in those where the diagnosis is unclear, where there is possibility of multiple diagnoses, or before starting therapy with potentially hepatotoxic medications." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
41. "A liver biopsy to establish a diagnosis of NASH should be obtained prior to starting children on pharmacologic therapy for NASH." (Strength – 2, Quality – C)
42. "Pathologists interpreting pediatric NAFLD biopsies should recognize the unique pattern frequently found in children to not misidentify pediatric NAFLD." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
43. "Intensive lifestyle modification improves aminotransferases and liver histology in children with NAFLD and thus should be the first line of treatment." (Strength – 2, Quality – B)
44. "Metformin at 500 mg twice daily offers no benefit to children with NAFLD and thus should not be prescribed. The effect of metformin administered at a higher dose is not known." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)
45. "Vitamin E 800 IU/day (RRR α–tocopherol) offers histological benefits to children with biopsy-proven NASH or borderline NASH but confirmatory studies are needed before it's use can be recommended in clinical practice." (Strength – 1, Quality – B)

### Supporting data the recommendations rest on

- **NAFLD Fibrosis Score** — six readily available variables: age, body mass index, hyperglycemia, platelet count, albumin, aspartate aminotransferase/alanine aminotransferase ratio. Meta-analysis of 13 studies, 3,064 patients: area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) **0.85** for advanced fibrosis (bridging fibrosis or cirrhosis). **Score < −1.455 → 90% sensitivity, 60% specificity to exclude** advanced fibrosis; **score > 0.676 → 67% sensitivity, 97% specificity to identify** it.
- **Enhanced Liver Fibrosis panel** — plasma hyaluronic acid, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 1, and amino-terminal propeptide of type III procollagen: AUROC **0.90**, 80% sensitivity, 90% specificity for advanced fibrosis.
- **Cytokeratin-18 fragments** — meta-analysis: sensitivity 78%, specificity 87%, AUROC 0.82 (95% confidence interval 0.78–0.88) for steatohepatitis. Rejected for routine use because the assay was not commercially available and **no established cut-off value existed** (each study used its own).
- **Autoantibodies** — in the NASH Clinical Research Network cohort, antinuclear antibody >1:160 or anti-smooth muscle antibody >1:40 was present in **21%** of well-phenotyped patients with NAFLD and was **not** associated with more advanced histologic features. Mildly elevated ferritin is common and does not necessarily indicate increased iron stores.
- **Weight loss dose–response** — 48-week randomized trial of intensive lifestyle change (diet, behaviour modification, 200 minutes/week moderate physical activity) vs structured basic education in 31 obese persons with NASH: intensive arm lost **9.3%** vs 0.2%, improving steatosis, necrosis and inflammation **but not fibrosis**. Participants with **≥7% weight loss** had significant improvement in steatosis, lobular inflammation, ballooning, and NAS. In a second trial, **>5%** loss improved steatosis and **≥9%** loss improved steatosis, lobular inflammation, ballooning, and NAS. Magnetic resonance spectroscopy studies: liver fat fell by an average of ~40% (range 20%–81%), generally requiring ~5%–10% body weight loss.
- **Exercise alone** (2–3 sessions/week, 30–60 minutes, 6–12 weeks) reduced liver fat content without significant change in body weight in all but one of four studies.
- **PIVENS** (247 non-diabetic adults with NASH, 96 weeks, pioglitazone 30 mg/day vs vitamin E 800 IU/day vs placebo): primary histologic endpoint met in **19% placebo, 34% pioglitazone (p = 0.04), 43% vitamin E (p = 0.001)**; because of two primary comparisons, **p = 0.025 was the a priori significance threshold**, so pioglitazone did not formally meet the endpoint. Resolution of NASH (key secondary endpoint) 47% pioglitazone vs 21% placebo (p = 0.001). Vitamin E number needed to treat **4.4**. Pioglitazone caused **4.7 kg weight gain** vs placebo.
- **Pioglitazone safety** — meta-analysis of 19 trials, 16,390 patients with type 2 diabetes: ~18% reduction in death, myocardial infarction, or stroke (p = 0.005), but **higher congestive heart failure rate (2.3% vs 1.8%, p = 0.002)** — caution with impaired myocardial function. Rosiglitazone was no longer marketed in Europe and highly restricted in the United States.
- **Vitamin E harm signal** — some meta-analyses reported increased all-cause mortality at high dose, others did not; one randomized trial found **400 IU/day increased prostate cancer risk in relatively healthy men** (absolute increase 1.6 per 1000 person-years).
- **Bariatric surgery** — prospective study of 381 adults with severe obesity, histology before and at 1 and 5 years: significant improvement in steatosis and ballooning; in the 99 with probable/definite NASH at baseline, improvement in steatosis, ballooning, NAS, and resolution of NASH. Most benefit evident at 1 year. A small but statistically significant mean fibrosis-score rise at 5 years (0.27 ± 0.55 → 0.36 ± 0.59, p = 0.001), yet **96% had fibrosis ≤ F1 and 0.5% had F3** — no clinically significant worsening. No patient had F3/F4 at baseline, so advanced fibrosis was not evaluable.
- **Heavy drinking definition** cited from the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism: more than 4 drinks on any day or more than 14 drinks per week in men; more than 3 drinks on any day or 7 drinks per week in women. Note this **differs from the guideline's own NAFLD-defining threshold in Recommendation 1** (>21/week men, >14/week women), which is drawn from a NASH clinical-trials consensus meeting.
- **Transient elastography** was described as having a high failure rate at higher body mass index and as **not commercially available in the United States**; magnetic resonance elastography was available but "rarely used in clinical practice."
- **Familial clustering** — 18% of patients with NASH had a similarly affected first-degree relative; in overweight children, heritability of magnetic-resonance-measured liver fat fraction was 0.386, and fatty liver was present in 18% of family members of children with NAFLD despite normal alanine aminotransferase and lack of obesity. Screening of family members was nonetheless not recommended (Recommendation 6).
- **Hepatocellular carcinoma risk** in NAFLD is "likely limited to those with advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis." Patients with NASH cirrhosis are at significantly **lower** risk of hepatocellular carcinoma than patients with hepatitis C cirrhosis; one international study of 247 patients with advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis followed a mean 85.6 ± 54.5 months found 10-year survival **81.5%**, not different from matched hepatitis C cirrhosis.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

**Superseded by newer tier-1 sources — the newer source wins on the page in every case below** (source priority: within the guideline tier, newer publication date wins). The 2012 positions are recorded here because readers trained in that era still carry them.

- **Disease name and definition.** This guideline defines NAFLD by *exclusion* of alcohol and other causes. [[aasld-2023-nafld]] and subsequent nomenclature replace it with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease, defined by the *presence* of cardiometabolic criteria rather than by exclusion.
- **Screening (Recommendations 5 and 6).** 2012 advised **against** screening in primary care, diabetes, and obesity clinics and against family screening. Later guidance recommends risk-stratified case-finding in type 2 diabetes and other high-risk groups — see [[nafld-masld]] and [[noninvasive-liver-disease-assessment]].
- **Fibrosis assessment.** 2012 called transient elastography commercially unavailable in the United States and leaned on biopsy. Current practice uses a tiered noninvasive pathway (Fibrosis-4 index → vibration-controlled transient elastography / magnetic resonance elastography / Enhanced Liver Fibrosis) — [[noninvasive-liver-disease-assessment]] is the home for that algorithm. The **Fibrosis-4 index is not mentioned anywhere in this 2012 document**.
- **Pharmacotherapy.** 2012 named vitamin E 800 IU/day first-line for non-diabetic biopsy-proven NASH and permitted pioglitazone; it predates every subsequently approved agent and the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist data. Apply current therapy guidance from the newer sources on [[nafld-masld]].
- **Hepatocellular carcinoma risk.** 2012 confines the risk to advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis; later sources address surveillance in non-cirrhotic NAFLD — see [[aga-2020-hcc-screening-nafld]] and [[hcc-surveillance]].
- **Internal inconsistency within the document.** The alcohol threshold *defining* NAFLD (Recommendation 1: >21 drinks/week men, >14 drinks/week women) is **more permissive** than the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism "heavy or at-risk drinking" definition the same document quotes in its alcohol section (>4 drinks/day or >14 drinks/week in men; >3 drinks/day or >7 drinks/week in women). A man drinking 20 drinks per week is therefore at-risk by one definition and still eligible for a NAFLD diagnosis by the other. Recommendation 1 is itself rated **2C — the weakest rating in the document**.
- **Open questions the guideline names explicitly:** no established cytokeratin-18 cut-off; whether treating steatosis alters the natural history of other chronic liver diseases; whether vitamin E or pioglitazone help when NAFLD coexists with another liver disease; the effect of bariatric surgery in advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis (not evaluable — no F3/F4 at baseline); the effect of non-heavy alcohol consumption (Recommendation 29 declines to make any recommendation); whether metformin at doses above 500 mg twice daily benefits children.
