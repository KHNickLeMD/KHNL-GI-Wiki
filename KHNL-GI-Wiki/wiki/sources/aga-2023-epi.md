## Summary

Expert Review on exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI) — failure of the pancreas to deliver a threshold level of digestive enzymes to the intestine, causing maldigestion of macro- and micronutrients, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency, and protein-calorie malnutrition. EPI is frequently underdiagnosed and undertreated.

The update frames EPI as a syndrome to be **suspected** in high-risk conditions and **considered** in moderate-risk conditions, diagnosed primarily by **fecal human elastase-1 (FE-1)** on a formed stool, and treated with **pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT)** plus fat-soluble vitamin supplementation and nutritional monitoring. Imaging and a therapeutic enzyme trial are both explicitly rejected as diagnostic tests.

**Fifteen Best Practice Advice (BPA) statements.** Because systematic reviews were not performed, the document states that these statements **carry no formal ratings for quality of evidence or strength of recommendation** — there is no Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) scheme, no "strong/conditional" vocabulary, and no evidence levels anywhere in the document. The numbering below is the document's own.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Best Practice Advice statements (verbatim / near-verbatim; ungraded)

**Whom to suspect, and what EPI looks like (BPA 1–3)**

1. EPI should be **suspected** in patients with high-risk clinical conditions: chronic pancreatitis (CP), relapsing acute pancreatitis (AP), pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, cystic fibrosis, and previous pancreatic surgery.
2. EPI should be **considered** in patients with moderate-risk clinical conditions: duodenal diseases, including celiac and Crohn's disease; previous intestinal surgery; longstanding diabetes mellitus; and hypersecretory states (e.g., Zollinger–Ellison syndrome).
3. Clinical features of EPI include steatorrhea with or without diarrhea, weight loss, bloating, excessive flatulence, fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies, and protein-calorie malnutrition.

**How to diagnose (BPA 4–9)**

4. **Fecal elastase test is the most appropriate initial test** and must be performed on a **semi-solid or solid stool specimen**. FE-1 **<100 µg/g stool provides good evidence of EPI**; levels of **100–200 µg/g are indeterminate**.
5. Fecal elastase testing **can be performed while on PERT**.
6. **Fecal fat testing is rarely needed** and must be performed on a **high-fat diet**. Quantitative testing is generally not practical for routine clinical use.
7. **Response to a therapeutic trial of pancreatic enzymes is unreliable for EPI diagnosis.**
8. Cross-sectional imaging methods — computed tomography (CT) scan, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) — **cannot identify EPI**, although they play an important role in the diagnosis of benign and malignant pancreatic disease.
9. Breath tests and direct pancreatic function tests hold promise but are **not widely available in the United States**.

**How to treat and monitor (BPA 10–15)**

10. Once EPI is diagnosed, **treatment with PERT is required**. If EPI is left untreated, it will result in complications related to fat malabsorption and malnutrition, having a negative impact on quality of life.
11. PERT formulations are **all derived from porcine sources and are equally effective at equivalent doses**. There is a **need for histamine-2 (H2) receptor antagonist or proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy with non–enteric-coated preparations**.
12. PERT should be taken **during the meal**, with the initial treatment of **at least 40,000 United States Pharmacopeia (USP) units of lipase during each meal in adults and one-half of that with snacks**. The subsequent dosage can be adjusted based on the meal size and fat content.
13. **Routine supplementation and monitoring of fat-soluble vitamin levels are appropriate.** Dietary modifications include a **low-moderate fat diet with frequent smaller meals and avoiding very-low-fat diets**.
14. Measures of successful treatment with PERT include reduction in steatorrhea and associated gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms; a gain of weight, muscle mass, and muscle function; and improvement in fat-soluble vitamin levels.
15. EPI should be monitored and **baseline measurements of nutritional status should be obtained** (body mass index [BMI], quality-of-life measure, and fat-soluble vitamin levels). A **baseline dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan should be obtained and repeated every 1–2 years.**

### Dosing detail beyond BPA 12 (Figure 1A and supporting text)

The BPA statement gives only the floor dose. The figure and text give the full titration rule:

| Dosing parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Typical adult starting dose (weight-based) | **500 units lipase/kg/meal** (≈40,000 U for an 80-kg patient) |
| Starting dose with snacks | **250 units lipase/kg/snack** (≈20,000 U for an 80-kg patient) — i.e. one-half the meal dose |
| Reasonable initial dose (stated as a range) | **40,000–50,000 USP units of lipase per meal**, one-half that with snacks |
| Titration | **Up** as needed to reduce steatorrhea or GI symptoms of maldigestion; up for larger or higher-fat meals, **down** for smaller or low-fat meals |
| **Maximum dose** | **2,500 units lipase/kg/meal**, or **10,000 units lipase/kg/day** |
| Dose seldom required above | **120,000 USP units of lipase per meal** |
| Cystic fibrosis / advanced CP ceiling | **<10,000 units lipase/kg body weight/day**, or **<4,000 units per gram of dietary fat per day** |

- Physiologic anchor: a normal pancreas makes **≈900,000 USP units of lipase during an average meal**; **≈90,000 units (10% of normal)** are needed to prevent steatorrhea. Even at high doses PERT is less efficient than native secretion and does not abolish steatorrhea entirely.
- PERT must be taken **during** the meal — "PERT treats the meal, not the pancreas."
- PERT is **generally lifelong**, though dose adjustment and even reduction can be considered in patients doing well with acceptable nutritional parameters.
- **Over-the-counter pancreatic enzyme products should not be used** — classified as dietary supplements only; dosing and efficacy are neither standardized nor regulated, so utility and safety are unknown.
- Adverse effects: nausea, abdominal cramping, bloating, diarrhea, constipation. Rare: **fibrosing colonopathy**, allergic reactions to porcine protein, hyperuricosuria.
- Acid-reducing agents are **not required** for enteric-coated preparations, although many such patients are on a PPI for other reasons.
- Drugs are equipotent at similar lipase doses — generally no reason to switch between brands based on response; switching is usually driven by formulary/out-of-pocket cost.
- Endoscopic or surgical pancreatic ductal drainage, while useful for pain, is **not effective in preventing or improving EPI**.

### Diagnostic detail beyond BPA 4–9

- **FE-1 bands:** **<200 µg/g = abnormal**; **<100 µg/g more consistent with EPI**; some investigators suggest **<50 µg/g is most reliable for severe EPI**. Under controlled conditions the test distinguishes normal, moderate, and severe EPI.
- **FE-1 is insensitive to mild EPI**; most accurate on formed or semi-formed stool; **falsely abnormal when stool is very watery** (dilution), and susceptible to false-positives where EPI prevalence is low. Repeat if indeterminate and pretest probability is high.
- Assay specificity: the commonly used stool test measures chymotrypsin-like elastases 3A and 3B; humans do not secrete elastase-1, so **porcine PERT does not cross-react**.
- **Exogenous PERT does not alter FE-1 results, and repeat FE-1 is not helpful for assessing treatment response.**
- **Skip the test when pretest probability is very high** — e.g. steatorrhea in a patient with known pancreatic disease: PERT can be started without FE-1 testing. With those conditions but *without* symptoms, measure FE-1 to confirm.
- **Steatorrhea is defined as a coefficient of fat absorption <93%** (>7% of ingested fat present in the stool). Visible steatorrhea usually requires moderate fat in the diet.
- **Quantitative fecal fat protocol:** known-fat diet for **5 days**, with stool collected over the **final 3 days**. Burdensome, difficult to implement, rarely performed outside research.
- Qualitative stool fat measures, including **Sudan stain, are nonspecific for EPI**; useful when clinical features are inconclusive or when assessing an inadequate clinical response to PERT.
- **Serum pancreatic enzyme levels** (e.g. trypsin) are unaffected by PERT but **unreliable if there is ongoing pancreatic inflammation**.
- Direct pancreatic function tests (secretin stimulation with duodenal aspiration over 30–60 min) are the most accurate but invasive, and are used mainly to diagnose **early-stage CP** rather than EPI.
- Secretin-enhanced magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) defines ductal anatomy well but **correlates poorly with EPI**.

### Monitoring detail beyond BPA 13–15

- **Screen for vitamin and mineral deficiencies at diagnosis and annually:** vitamins **A, D, E, K, B12, folate, magnesium, selenium, zinc, and iron**.
- In stable CP, also assess annually: **prealbumin and retinol-binding protein** (malnutrition markers), **thiamine**, and **hemoglobin A1c** (diabetes screening).
- Anthropometrics: height, weight, **handgrip strength**, muscle mass (CT or other techniques), BMI. Other muscle assessments: **psoas muscle size at L3**, timed up-and-go test.
- **DXA:** baseline, then repeat **every 1–2 years**. Osteopenia/osteoporosis → calcium and vitamin D intake, weight-bearing exercise, avoidance of alcohol and smoking.
- **Vitamin D and K deficiency is associated with osteopathy and fracture in CP; treatment reduces fracture rates.**
- Inflammatory markers (**C-reactive protein, or the C-reactive protein–to–albumin ratio**) reflect chronic low-grade inflammation and a catabolic state.
- Stable patients should have pancreatic disease state and treatment effectiveness assessed **at least annually**.
- Referrals: **certified dietitian** for nutritional assessment at diagnosis; **endocrinologist** if prediabetes or diabetes.

### Epidemiology / supporting detail

- EPI develops in **>½ of CP patients**; risk **>80%** with chronic alcohol use, smoking, ductal obstruction, atrophy, duct calcifications, or diabetes; typically after **5–10 y** of disease. Abstinence from smoking and alcohol may delay onset.
- Pooled EPI prevalence during follow-up of AP and relapsing AP: **27%–62%** (similar for both).
- Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma: **50%–92%** unresectable, **40%–50%** resectable before treatment, **75%** after treatment.
- **Cystic fibrosis: 85%**, presenting at birth or in infancy. Autoimmune pancreatitis is often associated with EPI.
- Ampullary cancer and main-duct intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm can cause EPI by obstructing the pancreatic duct.
- **Type 3c (pancreatogenic) diabetes** results from loss of pancreatic islet α (glucagon) and β (insulin) cells in pancreatitis.
- Overlapping/mimicking disorders that blunt PERT response: celiac disease, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, longstanding diabetes mellitus, inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's), disaccharidase deficiencies, bile acid diarrhea, and giardiasis — most often considered when a patient with EPI does not respond to PERT.
- Consequences of untreated EPI: deficiencies of vitamins A, D, E, K (most common), osteoporosis and fracture, weight loss, sarcopenia, reduced quality of life, higher rates of surgical complications, and increased mortality.
- Cost is a real barrier: prescription PERT is expensive, especially at higher doses; lower-cost or better insurance coverage is needed, particularly for low-income patients.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **No formal evidence ratings.** All 15 statements are ungraded expert opinion drawn from a literature review; the document performed no systematic review. Any grade or strength attached to these statements elsewhere would not be the document's.
- **The document's own body text mislabels BPA 11.** The abstract gives BPA 11 as the statement about porcine-derived formulations and the need for acid suppression with non–enteric-coated preparations; the body heading for BPA 11 instead repeats the text of BPA 10 verbatim, even though the discussion that follows it is about PERT formulations. The abstract's wording is used above.
- **DXA interval stated two ways:** BPA 15 and the bone-health discussion say **every 1–2 years**; the closing summary of that discussion says DXA **every 2 years**, and Figure 1B says **every 2 years**. The 1–2 year interval is the BPA.
- **FE-1 thresholds are not internally consistent.** BPA 4 gives <100 µg/g as good evidence and 100–200 µg/g as indeterminate, while the text calls **<200 µg/g abnormal** and cites <50 µg/g as most reliable for severe EPI. The indeterminate band requires clinical correlation.
- **Diet advice differs from [[acg-2020-chronic-pancreatitis]]**, which advises small frequent meals *without fat restriction*; this update advises a **low-moderate fat diet**. Both agree on avoiding very-low-fat diets. AGA 2023 is the newer tier-1 statement.
- No head-to-head data support switching PERT brands for inadequate response; no non-porcine product is available.
