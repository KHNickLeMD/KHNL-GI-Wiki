## Summary

An updated ACG clinical practice guideline on acute LGIB, defined as hematochezia or bright red blood per rectum from a **colorectal source** (excluding [[small-bowel-bleeding|small bowel bleeding]], which is a separate entity). The guideline covers risk stratification, resuscitation, reversal of [[anticoagulation-gi-bleeding|anticoagulation]], diagnostic testing ([[colonoscopy]] and computed tomography angiography [CTA]), timing of colonoscopy, endoscopic treatment, and post-discharge antithrombotic management.

Key departure from the 2016 guideline: the panel now recommends **against urgent colonoscopy within 24 hours** for most hospitalized patients, citing two randomized controlled trials (RCTs) showing no benefit — and one showing harm — compared with elective (24–96 hour) colonoscopy.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Epidemiology and Risk Factors

- Over 100,000 hospital admissions for LGIB annually in the U.S.; 271,575 emergency department (ED) visits in 2018
- Incidence of LGIB now exceeds [[upper-gi-bleeding|upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB)]] in population-based studies (Finland, Hong Kong)
- Median age of LGIB patients: 74 years (vs. 68 years for UGIB)
- Overall incidence estimated 33–87 per 100,000
- Risk factors: nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (hazard ratio [HR] 8.61), aspirin (HR 2.75), P2Y12 inhibitors, anticoagulants
- Rivaroxaban has higher risk of LGIB vs. apixaban (HR 1.53)
- Aspirin + NSAID use increases risk of diverticular bleeding via mucosal ulceration at diverticular neck/dome
- Case fatality rate for LGIB in aspirin users: <1%; majority managed as outpatients

### Etiology

- Diverticular bleeding: most common (26% in UK audit; up to 64% in Japanese cohorts with high colonoscopy use)
- Other etiologies: [[colon-ischemia|ischemic colitis]], [[hemorrhoids]], [[angioectasia|angioectasias]], [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal neoplasia]], postpolypectomy bleeding, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)-related colitis (including [[crohns-disease]] and [[ulcerative-colitis]]), infectious colitis, radiation proctopathy, rectal/NSAID-induced ulcers, Dieulafoy lesion, colorectal varices
- No diagnosed etiology in 23% of UK audit cases (low colonoscopy utilization); rates as low as 5% in high-colonoscopy cohorts

### Risk Stratification

- **Oakland score ≤8**: predicts 95% probability of safe discharge; validated with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) 0.87 in U.S. network (140 hospitals); sensitivity 98% for safe discharge; extending to ≤10 maintains 96% sensitivity
- **SHA2PE score ≤1**: predicts low risk of hospital-based intervention (AUROC 0.83); variables: systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥100, hemoglobin (Hgb) >12, no antiplatelets/anticoagulants, pulse <100
- **NOBLADS score**: predicts severe bleeding (AUROC 0.77 derivation; 0.74 external validation)
- **ABC score**: predicts 30-day mortality in undifferentiated gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB); AUROC 0.84 for LGIB
- No single score has excellent predictive ability across all outcomes; scores supplement but do not replace clinical judgment

### Resuscitation and Transfusion

- Intravenous (IV) fluid resuscitation with crystalloids for hemodynamic instability before endoscopy
- **Restrictive transfusion strategy: threshold Hgb 7 g/dL** (extrapolated from UGIB RCTs showing reduced mortality [relative risk (RR) 0.65] and rebleeding [RR 0.58] vs. liberal strategy)
- Threshold of 8 g/dL reasonable for preexisting cardiovascular disease or acute coronary syndrome
- Platelet transfusion threshold: >30×10⁹/L for severe LGIB; >50×10⁹/L if endoscopic procedure planned; no benefit to routine platelet transfusion for patients on antiplatelets

### Excluding Proximal Source

- 15% of patients with severe hematochezia had proximal (UGIB) source on esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) before colonoscopy
- Blood urea nitrogen (BUN):creatinine (Cr) ratio >30: likelihood ratio (LR) 7.5 for UGIB; BUN alone >21 mg/dL predicts UGIB with 93% specificity
- Blood clots in stool: LR 0.05 for UGIB (argues against proximal source)
- Nasogastric aspirate: sensitivity only 28% for UGIB — routine nasogastric (NG) tube not recommended

### Reversal of Anticoagulation

**Vitamin K antagonists (VKAs / warfarin):**

- Most patients unlikely to require reversal; hold VKA at admission for significant bleeding
- Endoscopic hemostasis is safe and effective at international normalized ratio (INR) ≤2.5
- Life-threatening LGIB with INR substantially exceeding therapeutic range: suggest reversal
- **4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) preferred over fresh frozen plasma (FFP)** in patients on VKAs for nonvalvular atrial fibrillation stroke prevention who require reversal (Rec 3, conditional, very low): faster INR reduction; RCT data show PCC superior (90% vs. 75% hemostasis); lower thromboembolic risk; reduced volume overload; decreased mortality vs. FFP

**Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs):**

- Hold DOAC at admission; most resolve with drug cessation alone
- Reversal only for life-threatening LGIB not responding to resuscitation + drug cessation
- **Idarucizumab** for dabigatran; **andexanet alfa** for apixaban and rivaroxaban (if DOAC taken within past 24 hours)
- PCC not definitively established for factor Xa inhibitor reversal
- Limited benefit of vitamin K, FFP, or cryoprecipitate for DOAC-related LGIB

### Antifibrinolytics

- **Tranexamic acid: NOT recommended** (Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence)
- RCT (96 patients): no reduction in Hgb drop, transfusion, intervention, or length of stay (LOS)
- Large international RCT (>12,000 patients with GIB): no benefit; increased venous thromboembolism (VTE) and seizures

### Colonoscopy

- Recommended for most hospitalized LGIB patients (Strong recommendation)
- May omit if: bleeding resolved + high-quality colonoscopy within 12 months + adequate prep showing diverticulosis without neoplasia (Conditional)
- Diagnostic yield 68–79% (definitive or presumptive); stigmata of recent hemorrhage (SRH) seen in 15–31%; active bleeding only 3.8%
- Endoscopic intervention rate low in Western cohorts: 3–6%
- Malignant lesions found in 2.5% of LGIB colonoscopies
- Technical tips: clear cap (recommended), water-jet scope, terminal ileum intubation when no colonic source found

### Timing of Colonoscopy

- **Nonemergent inpatient colonoscopy recommended** (Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence)
- Urgent colonoscopy (<24 hours) does NOT improve rebleeding or mortality vs. elective (24–96 hours)
- RCT 1 (n=159): early colonoscopy associated with **increased** rebleeding (13% vs. 3%) and readmission (11% vs. 3%)
- RCT 2 (n=170): no difference in SRH detection, rebleeding, endoscopic treatment, transfusion, or mortality
- All meta-analyses restricted to RCTs: no benefit of urgent colonoscopy
- Exception: select high-risk patients with high pretest probability of SRH (e.g., postpolypectomy bleeding); should be performed by experienced endoscopists with cap and water-jet

### Bowel Preparation

- Standard: 4–6 L polyethylene glycol (PEG) over 3–4 hours until rectal effluent clear (for urgent colonoscopy)
- **Split-dose preparation preferred** for elective inpatient colonoscopy (adequacy improved from 43% to 86%)
- Low-volume preparations (1 L same-day PEG) show comparable cleansing to split-dose 4 L in small RCT
- Unprepared colonoscopy or routine flexible sigmoidoscopy NOT recommended (unless anorectal source known)
- NG tube for prep delivery: use only when necessary; perform cautiously in patients with risk factors for aspiration

### CTA

- Sensitivity 90%, specificity 92% for LGIB diagnosis (meta-analysis of 14 studies)
- **First-line test for hemodynamically significant ongoing hematochezia** (Conditional recommendation)
- Low yield if minor LGIB or bleeding has subsided
- Positive CTA (extravasation): factors predicting positivity include shock index ≥1, tachycardia, hypotension, transfusion >3 units packed red blood cells (PRBC)/day, recent bowel resection or endoscopic intervention, NSAID/DOAC use, CTA within 4 hours of hematochezia
- Japanese cohort: CTA performed in 97.5% of LGIB admissions; extravasation in 22%
- Negative CTA: 80% settle spontaneously; no role for immediate transcatheter angiography after negative CTA
- Red blood cell (RBC) scintigraphy (99mTc): rapidly diminishing role; use only if CTA unavailable/contraindicated

### Management of Positive CTA

- **Prompt referral to interventional radiology (IR) for transcatheter arteriography (TA) + possible embolization** (Strong recommendation)
- TA within 90 minutes of positive CTA: 9× more likely to detect extravasation
- Embolization agents: microcoils, N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA), ethylene-vinyl alcohol copolymer; superselective with microcatheter
- NBCA embolization: one meta-analysis — 98% technical success, major complications 4.6% (mostly bowel infarction/ulcer); a second review (243 patients) — technical success 98.8%, 30-day rebleeding 15.7%, mortality 12.7%
- Colonoscopy also an option after positive CTA at experienced centers; shorter time to procedure predicts confirmation of bleeding regardless of modality

### Endoscopic Treatment

**Diverticular hemorrhage:**

- Through-the-scope clips, endoscopic band ligation (EBL), or coagulation (Strong recommendation)
- Direct clipping (onto culprit vessel) preferred over indirect/zipper technique (lower rebleeding)
- EBL: lower early rebleeding (8% vs. 19%) and late rebleeding (9% vs. 29%) compared to clipping
- Meta-analysis (16 studies, 384 patients): all three modalities 99–100% initial hemostasis; EBL more effective at avoiding embolization/surgery
- Despite endoscopic treatment, early rebleeding 17.4% and late rebleeding 32.0% for definitive diverticular hemorrhage
- Long-term recurrence: 4.7% at 1 year, 8.3% at 2 years, 15.7% at 5 years; median time to second episode 1.2 years

**Colonic angioectasias / vascular lesions:**

- Argon plasma coagulation (APC): effective first-line (0.8–1.0 L/min, 20–40 W); excellent safety profile
- For large right-colon angioectasias: submucosal fluid injection before APC to reduce perforation risk
- Pooled recurrence risk after endoscopic treatment: 34%

**Postpolypectomy bleeding:** (see also [[polypectomy]])

- Colonoscopy indicated for ongoing bleeding with hemodynamic compromise
- 43% of cases do not require endoscopic hemostasis (managed conservatively)
- Through-the-scope clips: first-line; hemostatic powders: 97% initial hemostasis in postpolypectomy bleeding; 98% hemostasis as monotherapy, combination, or rescue in a 50-patient multicenter LGIB study

### Recurrent Bleeding

- Rebleeding rate in UK audit: 13.6% at median 3 days post-presentation
- Risk factors: older age, hemodynamic instability at presentation, diverticular etiology
- Early rebleeding (within 30 days of initial diverticular treatment): up to 24%
- Repeat colonoscopy can be considered; for failed endoscopy proceed to TA
- Surgery: last resort (used in only 0.2% of UK audit patients); target localized resection to avoid 20% major complication rate; total colectomy associated with higher cardiac/renal complications and ileus

### Antithrombotic Management After Discharge

**NSAIDs:**

- Discontinue nonaspirin NSAIDs after hospitalization for diverticular hemorrhage (Rec 11a, strong, low)

**Aspirin:**

- Primary prevention: suggest discontinuation after diverticular hemorrhage (Conditional)
- Secondary cardiovascular prevention (established cardiovascular disease [CVD]): suggest continuing aspirin — protective against ischemic events (HR 0.59) despite increased rebleeding risk (HR 2.76); also reduces mortality (HR 0.33) (Conditional)

**P2Y12 inhibitors / nonaspirin antiplatelets:**

- Re-evaluate risks/benefits in multidisciplinary setting (Strong recommendation); associated with HR 1.47 for recurrent diverticular hemorrhage
- For dual antiplatelet patients with recent stents (<1 year): resume P2Y12 within maximum 5 days (stent thrombosis risk)

**Anticoagulants:**

- **Resume anticoagulation after LGIB cessation** (Strong recommendation)
- Resumption decreases thromboembolism and mortality; meta-analyses suggest a potentially increased risk of recurrent GI bleeding, while in a diverticular-hemorrhage cohort resumption was not associated with recurrent diverticular hemorrhage (HR 0.98)
- Optimal timing: within 7 days of bleeding event
- Discontinuing anticoagulation after diverticular hemorrhage associated with HR 1.93 for ischemic stroke at median 115 days

## Relevance to Wiki

- Primary source for [[acute-lower-gi-bleeding]] diagnostic schema
- Informs risk stratification (Oakland score, SHA2PE) for all LGIB presentations
- Authoritative guidance on CTA-first vs. colonoscopy-first approach
- Establishes that **urgent colonoscopy (<24h) is NOT standard of care** — paradigm shift from prior 2016 guideline
- Relevant to management of anticoagulated patients with GIB across all etiologies
- Directly relevant to diverticular disease, [[ulcerative-colitis]], [[crohns-disease]], and [[polypectomy]] complication management

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Urgent colonoscopy in select patients:** The guideline recommends against urgent colonoscopy broadly, but acknowledges select high-risk cases (postpolypectomy bleeding, high pretest probability of SRH) may benefit — no validated score to identify these patients prospectively
- **Conservative management vs. colonoscopy:** For stable patients with diverticulosis on recent high-quality colonoscopy, the evidence base for omitting inpatient colonoscopy is very low quality; further multicenter prospective data needed
- **CTA vs. colonoscopy as first test:** No head-to-head RCT; current data observational only
- **DOAC reversal:** No controlled trials for LGIB-specific populations; idarucizumab and andexanet alfa data come from heterogeneous GIB cohorts without control groups
- **EBL vs. clipping:** Most EBL data from Japanese cohorts with predominantly right-sided diverticulosis; may not generalize to Western populations with left-sided predominance
- **Optimal anticoagulant resumption timing:** Recommended within 7 days but based on expert opinion extrapolated from GIB literature broadly, not LGIB-specific RCTs
- **Low-volume bowel prep for urgent colonoscopy:** Promising but requires larger RCT validation
