## Summary

Update to the 2014 USMSTF recommendations on bowel cleansing. The task force (9 members, 3 from each society) formulated 21 PICO questions from a literature search of EMBASE, PubMed, Cochrane Reviews, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials spanning January 2013 – September 2023, which evolved into **25 consensus statements** grouped into three time frames: **before, during, and after colonoscopy**. The document also carries a set of ungraded **"key concepts"** — statements for which the supporting literature was not systematically evaluated, covering definitional and epidemiological points rather than diagnostic or management recommendations.

Evidence quality is rated **high / moderate / low / very low**. Strength is **strong** ("We recommend…") when consensus was that most patients should be managed according to the recommendation, and **weak** ("We suggest…") when there is more latitude in application.

Unless otherwise stated, the recommendations **apply to ambulatory patients at low risk for inadequate bowel preparation**; statements for higher-risk individuals are given separately. The task force cautions that most supporting studies were conducted in selected populations — frequently healthy ambulatory patients without prior gastrointestinal surgery and with limited risk factors — which limits generalizability.

**Definition of adequate bowel preparation quality:** the preparation quality is such that a **standard screening or surveillance interval can be assigned based on the findings of the [[colonoscopy]]**. Quality is assessed and reported **only after all washing and suctioning maneuvers have been completed**.

**What changed since 2014:**

- 2 L regimens now suggested instead of 4 L (2014 made no volume recommendation)
- Same-day regimen explicitly acceptable for afternoon colonoscopy, explicitly inferior for morning colonoscopy
- Split-dose remains the standard for all patients, high- or low-volume
- Adjunctive oral simethicone moved from "not recommended" (2014) to suggested, on strengthened evidence
- Routine tracking of adequate-preparation rate at endoscopist **and** unit level, with a target rate of **≥90%** at both
- Preparation modifications for prior or anticipated inadequate preparation codified, including a specific high-risk regimen

## Terminology and Volume Definitions

The volume thresholds behind the 2 L vs 4 L recommendation (Table 1):

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Bowel preparation process | All the steps communicated to an individual to prepare them for a colonoscopy — what to expect, arranging an escort, diet modification, which medications to hold and take |
| Bowel preparation regimen | The combination of medications and dietary modifications used to achieve a clean colon |
| Bowel preparation quality | The degree of cleanliness of the colon; assess and report **after all washing and suctioning maneuvers have been done** |
| **Adequate** bowel preparation quality | Quality such that a standard screening or surveillance interval **can** be assigned based on the findings |
| **Inadequate** bowel preparation quality | Quality such that a standard screening or surveillance interval **cannot** be assigned based on the findings |
| Purgative | The primary medication consumed to clean the colon of stool |
| Adjunct | Any secondary medication or dietary supplement in the regimen other than the purgative |
| Day prior regimen | Entire purgative consumed the day before colonoscopy |
| Split-dose regimen | Some portion (typically half) the day before, remainder the day of colonoscopy |
| Same-day regimen | Entire purgative consumed on the day of colonoscopy |
| **High-volume** regimen | **≥4 L** of purgative (sometimes called "full volume"); can be day prior, same-day, or split-dose |
| **Low-volume** regimen | **≥2 to <4 L** of purgative; can be day prior, same-day, or split-dose |
| **Ultra-low-volume** regimen | **<2 L** of purgative; can be same-day or split-dose |

## Key Concepts

Ungraded statements (Table 3 and the narrative). The supporting literature for these was not systematically evaluated.

- Choice of regimen should take into account **patient preference, comorbidities, safety, out-of-pocket cost** (prescription and over-the-counter purgatives and adjuncts), and ease of obtaining and consuming it.
- Individuals using a **same-day** regimen should begin drinking the purgative **4–6 hr before** the colonoscopy and complete it **at least 2 hr before** the procedure's start. (Same interval as the split-dose second portion.)
- Given limited data on simethicone timing and dose, **if simethicone is included, a dose of at least 320 mg should be used**. Out-of-pocket cost should also be considered.
- If a colonoscopy is **aborted** for inadequate preparation, the endoscopist should **photograph the segment(s) of colon that caused the abortion** — this aids quality assurance given variability in cancellation rates between endoscopists.
- For screening/surveillance colonoscopy, assess preparation **based on all segments of the colon**. Faced with a small region of colonic mucosa that cannot be cleared of residual stool, the endoscopist may exercise judgement on adequacy based on the **overall likelihood of missing a clinically meaningful lesion**.
- The term **"fair"** must be accompanied by a statement of adequacy (i.e. whether standard intervals can be assigned) — on its own it is nebulous and has been shown to carry ADR and interval recommendations similar to "excellent"/"good" when it is defined with clear descriptors.
- For a **nonscreening/nonsurveillance** colonoscopy (e.g. for diarrhea or hematochezia), the preparation may be adequate **for that indication** even if it is not adequate for screening/surveillance purposes; the preparation description must communicate this distinction.
- Bubbles in the visual field can significantly degrade visualization and procedural quality. If simethicone is used in that circumstance, use the **lowest possible dilution — for example, 0.5 mL simethicone in 99.5 mL water** — administered **only through an instrument channel that is routinely brushed during endoscope reprocessing**.
- Individuals whose colonoscopies are **cancelled for presumed inadequate preparation (i.e. before colonoscope insertion)** should be **included** when calculating unit- and endoscopist-level adequacy rates.
- Significant variability in adequacy between endoscopists sharing the same preparation processes suggests **individual-level variation** in intraprocedural cleaning effort or in assessment of adequacy.
- If the **descending colon, sigmoid colon, and rectum are well visualized** during an average-risk screening colonoscopy with otherwise inadequate preparation (e.g. ascending or transverse colon inadequate), it is reasonable to revisit screening options with the patient and referring clinician. If the individual opts to treat the limited colonoscopy as a flexible sigmoidoscopy and prefers not to repeat the colonoscopy, they should be screened again by sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy **in 5 yr**, or with a nonendoscopic screening test recommended by the USMSTF and the US Preventive Services Task Force.
- When preparation is inadequate **and** the indication is alarm symptoms (e.g. gastrointestinal blood loss) or a positive nonendoscopic colorectal cancer (CRC) screening test (e.g. fecal immunochemical test), repeat colonoscopy with adequate preparation should occur **as soon as possible**; timing should consider the date of symptom onset or the date the screening test was positive.

- Risk factors appear to be **additive**: in one study of 1,588 ambulatory colonoscopies the risk of inadequate preparation increased **linearly with the number of risk factors present, plateauing at a 98% likelihood once 7 risk factors were present**.

Additional safety points from the narrative:

- **Hyperosmolar purgatives** should be avoided in individuals at risk of clinical consequences from fluid shifts (e.g. renal insufficiency) or cardiac conditions (e.g. congestive heart failure).
- Sodium picosulfate + magnesium citrate is **contraindicated in congestive heart failure, hypermagnesemia, and severe renal impairment** because of its hyperosmolar nature. In patients with normal baseline renal function, serum magnesium imbalances are transient and of little clinical concern. Sodium picosulfate was associated with a higher risk of hospitalization with hyponatremia (absolute increase 0.05%, 95% CI 0.04%–0.06%; relative risk [RR] 2.4, 95% CI 1.5–3.9) but not with urgent head computed tomography or death.
- **2 L PEG-ELS + ascorbate** is contraindicated in phenylketonuria and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, should not be used with reduced creatinine clearance (<30 mL/min) or congestive heart failure, and — being hypertonic — hydration with additional water is recommended.
- Sodium sulfate, potassium sulfate, magnesium sulfate: elevated serum magnesium, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia have been observed; oral sulfate solution is associated with transient diminished renal function. These changes were transient and of low clinical significance.
- Bisacodyl has been associated with **rare occurrences of ischemic colitis**. PEG-3350 must not be mixed with a sports drink in a way that makes it non-isotonic; over-the-counter PEG-3350 is widely used but **not FDA-approved** as a bowel preparation.
- Oral sulfate solution carried an increased risk of nausea (RR 1.35, 95% CI 1.03–1.77) and **more than twice the risk of vomiting** (RR 2.30, 95% CI 1.63–2.23) compared with low-volume PEG-ELS.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Choice and Volume of Purgative

- **No single purgative is superior** for adequacy in low-risk ambulatory patients (Rec 5, Strong/High). Head-to-head comparisons are few; noninferiority designs dominate because baseline cleanliness already exceeds 90% with most modern preparations, so demonstrating superiority would require very large trials.
- **2 L is suggested over 4 L** (Rec 6, Weak/Moderate) on tolerability, not cleansing superiority. Supporting data (Table 6, comparing high-volume 4 L vs low-volume 2 L):
  - Adequate cleanliness overall 87.4% (4 L) vs 86.1% (2 L); RR high vs low volume 1.00 (0.98–1.02)
  - Adequate cleanliness, right colon 89.6% vs 91.2%; RR 1.01 (0.99–1.03)
  - Adherence to regimen 86.8% vs 92.8%; RR 1.06 (1.02–1.10)
  - **Tolerability 49.6% vs 72.5%; RR 1.39 (1.12–1.74)**
  - **Willingness to repeat 61.9% vs 89.5%; RR 1.41 (1.20–1.66)**
  - ADR 28.7% vs 27.6%; RR 0.96 (0.87–1.08)
- One large randomized controlled trial (RCT; n = 2,314) comparing 4 L split-dose PEG with 2 L split-dose PEG + bisacodyl found the low-volume arm noninferior; adequacy was slightly higher with 4 L (90% vs 89%; *P* = 0.02) but this lost significance after adjustment. Nausea and pain were more frequent and willingness to repeat lower in the high-volume arm (66.9% vs 91.9%; *P* < 0.01).
- **Ultra-low-volume (≤1 L) regimens are not ready for general use.** Pooled adequacy rates: sodium picosulfate/magnesium citrate 75%, 1 L PEG + ascorbate 83%, <1 L sodium phosphate 82%; a ≤1 L split-dose oral sulfate solution achieved 92%. Heterogeneity was very high (I² 86%–98%).
- **Regimen selection must consider medical history, medications, and prior preparation adequacy** (Rec 7, Strong/Moderate) — and **hyperosmotic regimens are to be avoided** in those at risk for volume overload or electrolyte disturbance (Rec 8, Strong/High).

### Timing — Split-Dose, Same-Day, and Fasting

- **Split-dose for everyone, high- or low-volume** (Rec 9, Strong/High). Rationale: the first dose clears solid stool, the second clears chyme that enters the large bowel overnight after the first dose.
- **Second portion: begin 4–6 hr before the colonoscopy, complete at least 2 hr before procedure start** (Rec 12, Strong/Moderate). The interval between final purgative ingestion and scope insertion correlates **inversely** with preparation quality — in non-split-dose preparations every additional hour was associated with a **10% decrease** in the likelihood of a good or excellent preparation. In a meta-analysis of 29 RCTs the superiority of split-dose was maintained within **3 hr** from the end of the purgative, progressively decreased after **4–5 hr**, and was **no longer present after 5 hr**.
- **Same-day = acceptable for afternoon colonoscopy** (Rec 10, Strong/High), **inferior for morning colonoscopy** (Rec 11, Weak/Low). Pooled same-day vs split-dose relative risk for adequacy 0.95 (0.90–1.00); for morning procedures one RCT (n = 200) showed better quality with split-dosing (Ottawa Bowel Preparation Score 5.52 ± 1.23 vs 6.02 ± 1.34; *P* = 0.017). Same-day regimens produced **better sleep quality** (sleep disturbance 22.3% same-day vs 37.4% split-dose; OR 0.44, 0.24–0.82).
- **Fasting/aspiration:** the 2023 American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) practice guidelines for preoperative fasting in healthy patients allow **clear liquids including up to 400 mL of clear liquids containing simple or complex carbohydrates up to 2 hr before** anesthesia or procedural sedation. Not all anesthesiologists consider purgatives clear liquids, so some prefer longer intervals. Observational studies of patients having upper endoscopy before colonoscopy showed **no excess gastric volumes** with split-dose preparations; in those studies split-dose was completed **no earlier than 2 hr** before endoscopy. In one study residual gastric fluid volume was significantly lower in the split-dose group (11 mL vs 19 mL; *P* < 0.001) with no pH difference.
- **ASA definition of "healthy patients"** for aspiration-risk purposes — "those without coexisting diseases or conditions that may increase the risk for aspiration, including esophageal disorders such as significant uncontrolled reflux disease, hiatal hernia, Zenker diverticulum, achalasia, stricture, previous gastric surgery (for example, gastric bypass), gastroparesis, diabetes mellitus, opioid use, gastrointestinal obstruction or acute intraabdominal processes, pregnancy, obesity, and emergency procedures." **Clinical judgement is recommended for patients meeting any of these criteria.**
- **Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists** ([[glp-1-receptor-agonists]]) are addressed **only** in the context of when to cease drinking the purgative, and carry **no numbered recommendation**. What the document states:
  - These agents **delay gastric emptying** and may therefore affect the timing of when to stop drinking a bowel preparation purgative.
  - The **ASA advises stopping them for 1–7 days (depending on the agent)** before an elective procedure to limit aspiration risk during sedation.
  - **If the agent has not been stopped, the advisement is to proceed, but with the assumption that the stomach is full.**
  - The **AGA position is that continuing or withholding is a case-by-case decision** (see [[aga-2024-glp1-endoscopy]]).
  - The approach is expected to change as better evidence emerges.
  - GLP-1 receptor agonist use is **not** one of the listed risk factors for inadequate preparation, and no odds ratio is reported for it.

### Adjuncts

- **Oral simethicone is suggested** (Rec 13, Weak/Moderate) — an upgrade from the 2014 recommendation against routine adjuncts. It lowers the surface tension of bubbles. Supporting data:
  - Multicentre endoscopist-blinded RCT (n = 583), 2 L PEG ± 30 mL simethicone 6–8 hr before colonoscopy: **ADR 21.0% vs 14.3%** (*P* = 0.04); patients with a Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS) score ≥6, 88.3% vs 75.2% (*P* < 0.001).
  - Meta-analysis of 6 studies: ADR 27.9% vs 23.3% (*P* = 0.02), though regimens and simethicone doses varied and some studies did not report concentration.
  - Meta-analysis of 38 trials (10,505 patients): oral simethicone, **particularly at a dose of 320 mg or higher**, improved preparation quality and reduced bubbles, **but did not improve ADR**. An 18-RCT meta-analysis (7,187 patients) likewise showed improved quality without improved ADR.
  - Adding simethicone to a PEG-based regimen reduced intraprocedural simethicone use from **49% of colonoscopies to 2%** (*P* < 0.05).
  - **Timing:** one RCT of 440 patients found that giving 200 mg during the first vs second litre of a split-dose 2 L PEG regimen made **no difference to BBPS or ADR**; earlier dosing was associated with shorter cecal intubation time (3.80 ± 1.81 vs 4.42 ± 2.03 min; *P* < 0.001), fewer bubbles, and higher detection of diminutive right-sided adenomas. Trials of evening vs morning dosing conflict.
  - **Operative dose: at least 320 mg** (key concept) — the document stresses that the data supporting any specific dose are limited.
- **Non-simethicone adjuncts are not suggested routinely** (Rec 14, Weak/Low). Studied and found insufficient: chewing gum, orange juice, diet cola, menthol candy drops, citrus peel tablets (6 small RCTs, ≤150 patients/arm, I² = 81%); lubiprostone; mosapride citrate (no difference in adequacy by BBPS ≥6, 98.4% vs 98.5%, *P* = 0.97). Bisacodyl improved quality in regimens using ≤1 L but **not** with larger volumes, and was associated with higher patient discomfort.
- **Simethicone through the scope:** if used for intraprocedural bubbles, use the **lowest possible dilution — 0.5 mL simethicone (20 mg/0.3 mL) in 99.5 mL water (0.5%)** — the lowest of three concentrations studied (0.5%, 1%, 3%) and the one associated with least residue in the accessory channel. Administer **only via an instrument channel routinely brushed during reprocessing** ([[endoscope-reprocessing]]).

### Assessing and Describing Preparation Quality

- **Patient self-report of preparation adequacy is unreliable** — among 429 patients whose preparation was adequate at the end of washing/suctioning, **42% of the 52 who had reported brown liquid or solid stool before the colonoscopy** nonetheless had an adequate preparation. Despite this, 97% of academic GI fellowship program directors reported their unit allowed cancellation based on preprocedure patient self-report.
- Hence **Recommendation 15**: insert the colonoscope to the sigmoid colon to confirm inadequacy before aborting.
- **Scales in use:** Aronchick Scale, Harefield Preparation Scale, Chicago Bowel Preparation Scale, Ottawa Bowel Preparation Scale, and the **BBPS**. One large systematic review concluded the **BBPS is the most reliable and thoroughly validated**. The document does not reproduce the scoring criteria for any of them.
- **The actionable BBPS cut-points:**
  - A **BBPS segment score of 2 or 3 in all colonic segments = adequate**, validated against the ability to detect **adenomas larger than 5 mm**.
  - Consider a **total BBPS <6 caused by any segment score <2 to be inadequate**, requiring **repeat colonoscopy as soon as feasible within 12 months**.
- **Reliability vs validity:** reliability (consistent scoring by the same and different endoscopists) is the crucial aspect of any categorical adequacy scale; validity (measuring what it was designed to measure) also matters. A "fair" preparation in the New Hampshire Colonoscopy Registry — which uses explicit descriptors (excellent, good, fair, poor) — carried similar ADRs and screening/surveillance interval recommendations to "excellent" or "good," highlighting that "fair" is meaningless **unless** it has a standardized definition with clear descriptors, and even then should be accompanied by an explicit adequacy statement.
- **Irrigation pumps are suggested** (Rec 18, Weak/Very low) — in favour of routine use. Evidence is sparse: one retrospective study of 1,037 outpatient colonoscopies before (n = 328) and after (n = 709) pump introduction in a single unit (all patients on 4 L PEG-ELS + 15 mg bisacodyl the evening before) found inadequate preparation **10% vs 24%** (*P* < 0.01), with no significant change in ADR. The suggestion is explicitly **conditional**, made because improving intraprocedural quality is often needed and pumps greatly facilitate it compared with repeated syringe flushes.
- Objective artificial-intelligence assessment of adequacy may become feasible ([[artificial-intelligence-endoscopy]]), but currently an individual endoscopist must render a subjective determination, introducing variability — in one retrospective study of 11 endoscopists at one centre, the frequency of "poor/unsatisfactory" or "fair" preparations ranged from **3% to 40%**, mean 22%.

### Salvage and Rescheduling

- **Same-day salvage is suggested when feasible** (Rec 19, Weak/Moderate). One prospective study of 525 patients found **75% of patients with poor (n = 11) or fair (n = 33) preparation at insertion** could be converted to good or excellent with concerted washing and suctioning.
- **Enemas:** PEG 500 mL–1,000 mL and bisacodyl 10 mg enemas instilled into the right colon (patient in right lateral decubitus, withdrawn, allowed to recover from sedation, re-scoped after evacuation) achieve adequate preparation with **success rates ranging 53%–100%**.
- **Additional oral purgative** with same-day (allowing 2 hr from the last dose) or next-day repeat. In an RCT of patients with inadequate preparation, **2 L of PEG after recovering from sedation was superior to a 1 L PEG enema (82% vs 53%)** in achieving an adequate preparation.
- **Rescheduling (Rec 22):** within **12 months** for screening or surveillance; **as soon as possible, generally within 3 months,** when the colonoscopy was for an abnormal noncolonoscopic CRC screening test.
- **Why the interval matters:** a systematic review/meta-analysis of 11 studies (>55,000 colonoscopies) found inadequate preparation carried a **5% absolute lower ADR** and a 1%–2% absolute lower advanced ADR; the OR for detecting ≥1 adenoma comparing high- vs low-quality preparation was **1.41 (95% CI 1.21–1.64)**. Adenoma and advanced adenoma miss rates run **15%–40%**. In one single-centre study of 3,047 patients with inadequate preparation, only **17% had a repeat colonoscopy within 3 years**; among those repeated, **83 previously unseen adenomas** were found — adenoma miss rate **42% (95% CI 35–49)**, advanced adenoma miss rate **27% (95% CI 17–41)**. For those repeated within 1 year, miss rates were 35% and 36%. In the GIQuIC registry, only **32%** of 260,314 inadequately prepared screening/surveillance colonoscopies were accompanied by a recommendation to repeat within a year.
- **Repeat preparations fail often.** In an RCT of 256 patients with previous inadequate preparation (BBPS ≤5), 4 L split-dose PEG achieved adequate preparation (BBPS ≥6) more often than 2 L PEG + ascorbate (**81% vs 67%; OR 2.07, 95% CI 1.16–3.69**); all patients had a 3-day low-residue diet and 10 mg bisacodyl the day before. A second multicentre RCT found 4 L and 6 L split-dose PEG regimens similar (91% vs 88%; *P* = 0.44), with greater willingness to repeat the 4 L regimen (92% vs 66%; *P* < 0.001) — this trial underpins the **4 L** choice in Recommendation 25.
- **Alternatives to a repeat colonoscopy** may be reasonable for average-risk screening: 59% of >1,000 surveyed US adults aged 45–75 preferred a stool-based test (fecal immunochemical test or stool DNA) over repeating a colonoscopy after inadequate preparation; among those who had actually had a colonoscopy (n = 486) the preference was 51%. **Finding adenomas at the index colonoscopy removes average-risk status, so repeat colonoscopy is recommended in that case** to exclude synchronous lesions.

### Adequacy Rate as a Quality Measure

- **Track the adequate-preparation rate at both the individual endoscopist and the endoscopy unit level** (Rec 20, Strong/Moderate); **target ≥90% at both** (Rec 21, Strong/Moderate). See [[colonoscopy-quality-indicators]].
- Supporting benchmark data: the ASGE/ACG quality-indicator document sets a performance target of 90% adequacy. In the GIQuIC consortium registry (3,773,519 screening and surveillance colonoscopies, 2010–2017), **inadequate preparation was reported in 5.3%**. In the Dutch Gastrointestinal Endoscopy Audit, unadjusted adequacy rates ranged **90%–99% across 51 endoscopy units**, and several units' performance changed significantly relative to the benchmark after case-mix adjustment for age, sex, ASA classification, and indication.
- Unit-level rate reflects policy-level factors (quality of written/verbal instructions, purgative prescribed, nursing and navigator support, irrigation pump availability); endoscopist-level rate reflects individual cleaning effort and assessment threshold.
- Cancelled colonoscopies (cancelled for presumed inadequate preparation before scope insertion) **must be included** in the denominator.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **ASA vs AGA on GLP-1 receptor agonists.** The document reports both without resolving them: the ASA advises stopping these agents 1–7 days before an elective procedure; the AGA position is a case-by-case decision. The USMSTF makes no recommendation of its own and expects the area to change.
- **Simethicone in the endoscope.** The USMSTF suggests the lowest possible dilution through a routinely brushed instrument channel; in the same discussion it notes that, **in response to FDA guidance, endoscope manufacturers have recommended against administering simethicone into endoscope accessory channels** because of crystal formation, residue, and biofilm.
- **The adequacy target is stated two ways within the document** — Recommendation 21 and its in-text box read **"≥90%"**; the abstract reads **">90% for both rates."**
- **Recommendation 6 is stated two ways** — Table 2 gives it unqualified ("2 L instead of 4 L"); the in-text recommendation box restricts it to ambulatory patients at low risk for inadequate preparation.
- **No purgative can be shown superior.** Noninferiority designs dominate because baseline adequacy already exceeds 90%, and FDA approval measures cleanliness at insertion rather than at final inspection — so definitive "best preparation" data are not anticipated.
- **Ultra-low-volume (<2 L) regimens** are not ready for general use; the supporting meta-analyses carry I² of 86%–98%.
- **Simethicone**: optimal timing relative to purgative ingestion is not established, and many trials did not report the concentration used. Simethicone improves visualization and reduces bubbles but has **not** been shown to improve ADR in the larger meta-analyses.
- **High-risk regimens are built on few trials** — Recommendation 25 rests on a single multicentre RCT, and predictive models for identifying high-risk individuals have positive predictive values of only 29%–41% and have not been shown to improve outcomes.
- **Preparation scales:** the document names five scales and endorses the BBPS as most validated, but **does not reproduce the scoring criteria of any of them** — only the ≥2-per-segment / total <6 adequacy cut-points.
- **Generalizability:** most supporting studies enrolled selected, healthy ambulatory patients without prior gastrointestinal surgery and with limited risk factors.
