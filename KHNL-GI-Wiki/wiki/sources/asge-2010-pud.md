## Summary

American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) Standards of Practice Committee statement on the role of upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy across [[peptic-ulcer-disease|peptic ulcer disease (PUD)]] — suspected PUD, confirmed uncomplicated PUD, malignancy exclusion, surveillance, atypical ulcers, and the complications (bleeding, perforation, penetration, gastric outlet obstruction). It is an update of a previous ASGE document and supersedes it. ASGE defines a peptic ulcer as **a defect in the gastric or duodenal wall extending through the muscularis mucosa into the deeper layers of the wall (submucosa or muscularis propria)**.

Core theme: the value of esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) in PUD is diagnosis, malignancy exclusion, and therapy — not routine repetition. Duodenal ulcers are essentially never malignant, so routine biopsy and routine follow-up endoscopy are unnecessary; gastric ulcers mostly warrant biopsy because malignant ones can look endoscopically benign, but both biopsy and surveillance decisions are explicitly individualized. The two treatable etiologies the document directs management at are [[helicobacter-pylori-infection|*Helicobacter pylori* (H pylori)]] infection and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). In complicated PUD, endoscopy is central for bleeding (early, therapeutic, repeatable) and for [[gastric-outlet-obstruction|gastric outlet obstruction (GOO)]] (diagnostic, plus balloon dilation for benign disease), and is withheld in acute perforation.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Recommendations — the numbering (1–12) is the document's own**, as is the quality-of-evidence rating. ASGE grades **quality of evidence only** using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) symbols of its Table 1 — **⊕⊕⊕⊕ high · ⊕⊕⊕◯ moderate · ⊕⊕◯◯ low · ⊕◯◯◯ very low**. **No separate strength grade is printed**; per the document's preamble, strength is carried by the verb — *we recommend* marks the stronger statements, *we suggest* the weaker ones.

1. We recommend that testing for the presence of H pylori be performed in all patients with PUD because it is a common etiology. (⊕⊕⊕◯ moderate)
2. Duodenal ulcers are extremely unlikely to be malignant, and routine biopsy of these ulcers is not recommended. (⊕⊕⊕◯ moderate)
3. Endoscopy is not recommended to evaluate benign-appearing, uncomplicated duodenal ulcers identified on radiologic imaging. (⊕⊕⊕◯ moderate)
4. We suggest that surveillance endoscopy be considered in patients with duodenal ulceration who experience persistent symptoms despite an appropriate course of therapy, specifically to rule out refractory peptic ulcers and ulcers with nonpeptic etiologies. (⊕⊕◯◯ low)
5. We suggest that most gastric ulcers undergo biopsy because malignant gastric ulcers may appear endoscopically benign. However, in some clinical situations (eg, young patients taking NSAIDs with multiple benign-appearing ulcers), the risk of malignancy is very low. Therefore, the decision to perform biopsy and/or surveillance endoscopy should be individualized. (⊕⊕◯◯ low)
6. We suggest that the decision to perform surveillance endoscopy in patients with a gastric ulcer be individualized. Surveillance endoscopy **is suggested** for those gastric ulcer patients who remain symptomatic despite an appropriate course of medical therapy. It **should also be considered** in patients with gastric ulcers without a clear etiology and in those who did not undergo biopsy at the index EGD. (⊕◯◯◯ very low)
7. In patients with refractory PUD, we suggest surveillance endoscopy be performed until the ulcer has healed or the etiology has been defined. (⊕⊕◯◯ low)
8. Because endoscopy is an effective tool in the diagnosis, prognostication, and therapy of bleeding peptic ulcers, we recommend that it be performed early in the course of hospitalization. (⊕⊕⊕⊕ high)
9. In patients who rebleed after initial endoscopic hemostasis, repeat endoscopic therapy is recommended before considering surgical or radiologic intervention. (⊕⊕⊕◯ moderate)
10. We recommend against endoscopy in patients with clinical evidence of acute perforation. (⊕⊕⊕◯ moderate)
11. We recommend endoscopy for the evaluation of gastric outlet obstruction. (⊕⊕⊕⊕ high)
12. We suggest endoscopic balloon dilation be considered for the management of benign gastric outlet obstruction. (⊕⊕◯◯ low)

### Suspected PUD — who gets endoscopy

- Presentations of PUD: [[dyspepsia]], GI bleeding, anemia, gastric outlet obstruction.
- Dyspepsia affects **10%–40%** of the general population; PUD is found in only **5%–15%** of dyspeptic patients — the majority have [[functional-dyspepsia|functional dyspepsia]].
- **EGD indicated** (restated by ASGE from its dyspepsia guideline): **age >50 with new-onset dyspepsia**, *or* **any age with alarm features**.
- **Alarm features:** family history of upper GI malignancy · unintended weight loss · overt GI bleeding · [[iron-deficiency-anemia|iron deficiency anemia]] · progressive [[dysphagia]] or odynophagia · persistent vomiting · palpable mass · lymphadenopathy.
- **Age <50 without alarm features:** test for H pylori and treat if positive; stop NSAIDs if being taken. If H pylori-negative → either a **short course (4–8 weeks) of acid suppression** or EGD.
- **Caveat ASGE states explicitly:** alarm features have **limited predictive value** for underlying PUD and/or upper GI malignancy — so EGD is advisable for **any** patient with persistent dyspeptic symptoms.

### Uncomplicated PUD — biopsy and malignancy exclusion

- **Uncomplicated PUD** = absence of GI bleeding, obstruction, and perforation. Main role of endoscopy here: confirm the diagnosis and rule out malignancy.
- Older data suggested **5%–11%** of gastric ulcers represented malignancy — most commonly [[gastric-adenocarcinoma|gastric adenocarcinoma]], less commonly [[gastric-malt-lymphoma|lymphoma]], occasionally metastatic cancer. US gastric cancer incidence is declining, and **there are no recent data to guide whether all gastric ulcers need biopsy**.
- **When routine biopsy may be unnecessary** (the low-risk picture ASGE names): a young patient taking NSAIDs whose ulcer appearance is typical of NSAID injury — **shallow flat antral ulcer with associated erosions**.
- **Biopsy when the appearance suggests malignancy:** associated **mass lesion** · **elevated irregular ulcer borders** · **abnormal adjacent mucosal folds**.
- Endoscopic appearance is a good predictor of absence of malignancy, but some malignant ulcers initially appear benign — hence biopsy in a substantial proportion of cases, individualized.
- **Biopsy technique:** multiple specimens from the **base and edges** of the gastric ulcer, if clinically feasible. **Routine cytologic brushings add little to sensitivity and are not recommended** as an alternative or adjunct to endoscopic biopsy.
- **Endoscopic tests for H pylori:** biopsy for histologic examination, culture, or rapid urease testing.

### Surveillance endoscopy

**Duodenal ulcers**
- Low yield if symptoms resolve after acid suppression + eradication therapy (when H pylori present) + discontinuation of NSAIDs.
- **>90% of duodenal ulcers heal with 4 weeks of [[proton-pump-inhibitors|proton pump inhibitor (PPI)]] therapy.**

**Gastric ulcers**
- Rationale for surveillance: some gastric ulcers initially benign on endoscopy *and* histology eventually prove malignant.
- **False-negative biopsy results occur in 2%–5%** of malignant ulcers.
- **Any unhealed ulcer at follow-up examination after 8–12 weeks of medical therapy should undergo repeat biopsy.**
- Surveillance also for: ulcers **endoscopically suspicious for malignancy even when index biopsies are benign**; patients still symptomatic after antisecretory therapy with previous benign biopsies (to exclude refractory ulceration, nonpeptic benign etiology, occult malignancy); gastric ulcers **without a clear etiology**; and those **not biopsied at the index endoscopy** for any reason (actively bleeding ulcer, coagulopathy, patient instability).
- **Low-yield situation:** benign-appearing gastric ulcer on endoscopy, confirmed on biopsy, with a **defined etiology (eg, NSAID or H pylori related)**, asymptomatic after an appropriate course of therapy.
- ASGE notes **no US or Canadian gastroenterologic society recommends routine surveillance for gastric ulcers**, yet it is common practice — ~25% of patients diagnosed with gastric ulceration undergo repeat upper endoscopy within 3 months. Efficacy and cost-effectiveness are unclear.
- **Gastric ulcer diagnosed radiologically → endoscopy.** Radiologic criteria for benignity (concurrent duodenal ulceration or deformity) are **not reliable**. If the radiologic ulcer appears benign, is responding to medical therapy, and there are no alarm features, it is reasonable to treat medically for a duration expected to produce endoscopic healing (**typically 8–12 weeks**) before endoscopy; **if not healed within that period, obtain biopsy specimens**.

### Atypical PUD

**Giant ulcers**
- **Giant gastric ulcer = >3 cm**; accounted for as many as **10%–24%** of all gastric ulcers in older literature, but with widespread antisecretory therapy they are now rarely encountered.
- **Giant duodenal ulcer = >2 cm**; higher incidence of complications — bleeding, penetration, and perforation.
- Patients with giant ulcers are **older**, may present atypically (**anorexia, weight loss**), and have more aggressive disease: higher incidence of bleeding, **mortality 10% vs 3%**, **urgent surgery 65% vs 12%** compared with smaller ulcers.
- **Endoscopy over barium:** contrast studies may miss giant gastric ulcers (large, shallow craters) and giant duodenal ulcers, which may be mistaken for the entire duodenal bulb, a pseudodiverticulum, or a true bulb diverticulum.
- Endoscopy also excludes malignancy and rare causes of giant ulcers — **[[crohns-disease|Crohn's disease]], eosinophilic gastroenteritis, ischemia** — and may be needed to manage their complications.
- **No data clearly demonstrate improved outcomes from surveillance endoscopy of giant peptic ulcers**, although surveillance and documentation of healing could be justified by their higher adverse-event rates.

**Refractory ulcers**
- **Definition: fails to heal despite 8–12 weeks of antisecretory therapy.**
- Surveillance endoscopy until healing is documented **or** the etiology is defined — **surreptitious NSAID use, high gastrin states, ischemia**.
- **Surgical consultation may be considered** for persistent nonhealing PUD.

### Complicated PUD — bleeding ulcers

- Endoscopy in bleeding PUD reduces blood transfusion requirements, intensive care unit and hospital stay, rebleeding, and mortality.
- **"Early" = within 24 hours of admission** — the interval shown to reduce transfusion requirements and length of stay.
- **Hemodynamically stable patients whose endoscopy shows ulcers without high-risk stigmata may be safely discharged home** after endoscopy.
- **High-risk stigmata — all should undergo [[endoscopic-hemostasis|endoscopic therapy]]:** adherent clot · visible vessel · active arterial bleeding.
- **Recurrent bleeding occurs in as many as 10%** despite endoscopic therapy and high-dose PPIs → repeat endoscopic therapy before surgical or radiologic intervention.

### Perforated and penetrating ulcers

- Clinical evidence of **acute perforation → generally no endoscopy**. Endoscopic therapy (eg, mechanical clips) is **not currently recommended** for acute perforation in PUD; **surgical closure is the usual approach**.
- Why clips are expected to fail here: the **decreased tissue compliance** of a perforated peptic ulcer compared with an acute iatrogenic perforation (where endoscopic clip closure is described).
- In combined laparoscopic-endoscopic series, endoscopy's role has been limited to **identifying the perforation site and guiding laparoscopic intracorporeal suture repair with an omental patch**.
- **Penetrating ulcer:** endoscopic biopsy specimens have occasionally allowed diagnosis of penetration into adjacent organs (liver, spleen). **Endoscopy has no therapeutic role in penetrating ulcers.**

### Gastric outlet obstruction

- Mechanism in PUD: **inflammation and scarring of the pylorus and/or duodenum**.
- Presentation: loss of appetite, epigastric pain, bloating, nausea, vomiting, weight loss.
- Endoscopy **confirms the diagnosis and differentiates benign from malignant** obstruction; **biopsies to exclude malignancy should be considered**. **Active ulcers are found in as many as one third** of patients endoscoped for GOO.
- **Medical management alongside:** acid suppression with a PPI to heal any active ulcers, avoidance of NSAIDs, and **H pylori eradication — which minimizes subsequent ulcer-related complications**.
- **Balloon dilation — the operative detail:**
  - **67%–83%** of patients respond, with good to excellent **short-term** relief of symptoms.
  - **Hydrostatic or pneumatic** balloons; inflation durations **30 seconds to 3 minutes**.
  - **Incremental, sequential** dilation in most series, typically to a diameter of **at least 15 mm** and **up to as large as 20 mm**.
  - **Perforation rates are high: 4%–7%.** In one series, **dilation to 20 mm resulted in perforation in 2 of 3 patients.**
  - **Long-term results are poor:** restenosis in as many as **84%**, and **51% require surgery**. Patients requiring **more than 2 dilations** are at high risk of endoscopic failure and often require surgical intervention.
  - **Counterpoint from one series of 21 patients:** symptomatic remission maintained in all patients managed by balloon dilation (median 2 dilations) over median follow-up of **43 months** — the authors attributed this to careful management of the underlying etiology (H pylori eradication, stopping NSAIDs when possible, antisecretory therapy, and maintenance antisecretory therapy when aspirin was necessary or the PUD was idiopathic).
- **Isolated case reports only:** electrocautery with a sphincterotome, and temporary placement of self-expanding metal stents, for pyloric stenosis failing balloon dilation.

## Relevance to Wiki
- Updates [[peptic-ulcer-disease]] — ulcer definition (through the muscularis mucosa into submucosa/muscularis propria); duodenal ulcer biopsy not routine and >90% heal with 4 weeks of PPI; gastric ulcer biopsy criteria (mass lesion, elevated irregular borders, abnormal adjacent folds), multiple base-and-edge specimens, no brushings; 2%–5% false-negative biopsies; repeat biopsy of any ulcer unhealed at 8–12 weeks; refractory ulcer = unhealed after 8–12 weeks of antisecretory therapy; giant ulcer thresholds (gastric >3 cm, duodenal >2 cm) and their outcome penalty; no endoscopy in acute perforation; no therapeutic role in penetrating ulcers.
- Updates [[dyspepsia]] — endoscopy entry criteria (age >50 with new-onset dyspepsia, or alarm features at any age), the alarm-feature list, the 4–8 week acid-suppression trial for H pylori-negative patients under 50, and the caveat that alarm features have limited predictive value.
- Updates [[upper-gi-bleeding]] / [[endoscopic-hemostasis]] — early endoscopy defined as **within 24 hours of admission**; high-risk stigmata (adherent clot, visible vessel, active arterial bleeding) all get endoscopic therapy; hemodynamically stable patients without high-risk stigmata may be discharged home after endoscopy; ~10% rebleed despite endotherapy plus high-dose PPI; repeat endoscopic therapy before surgery or interventional radiology.
- Updates [[gastric-outlet-obstruction]] — benign (peptic) GOO: active ulcers in up to one third of patients scoped; balloon dilation specifications (hydrostatic or pneumatic, 30 s to 3 min, incremental to at least 15 mm and up to 20 mm, with perforation in 2 of 3 patients dilated to 20 mm in one series), 4%–7% perforation, 84% restenosis, 51% eventual surgery, >2 dilations predicts endoscopic failure; concurrent PPI, NSAID avoidance, and H pylori eradication.
- Updates [[helicobacter-pylori-infection]] — test all patients with PUD (Rec 1); endoscopic test options are biopsy for histology, culture, or rapid urease testing.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Inconsistency within the document itself:** its body text on bleeding ulcers says repeat endoscopic therapy *is suggested* before surgical or radiologic intervention, whereas graded Recommendation 9 says it *is recommended*. The graded statement is the stronger and is what this wiki carries.
- Older (2010) than [[acg-2021-ugib|American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2021 upper gastrointestinal bleeding]]; for bleeding-ulcer hemostasis and PPI management the newer ACG guideline governs. This source contributes endoscopy-role, atypical-ulcer, and gastric-outlet-obstruction detail not covered there.
- ASGE 2010 makes **no recommendation either way on surveillance of giant peptic ulcers** — it states that no data clearly demonstrate improved outcomes, while noting surveillance could be justified by their higher adverse-event rates. This stays a judgement call.
- ASGE 2010 does not quantify how many gastric-ulcer biopsy specimens constitute adequate sampling — only "multiple specimens" from the base and edges.
- The 5%–11% gastric-ulcer malignancy figure comes from older data, and the document states there are no recent data to guide whether all gastric ulcers need biopsy; the "most gastric ulcers" and surveillance recommendations rest on low and very low quality evidence.
