## Best Practice Advice (Complete, Verbatim)

All 9 statements as printed in the body of the article. (The abstract prints the same 9 with trivial wording variants — "where"/"in which" in BPA 1, "may be seen"/"can be seen" in BPA 2, "Management"/"Management of SIBO" in BPA 7. The body heading for BPA 8 is typeset as "Practice Advice 8"; the abstract labels it "BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 8".)

**Best Practice Advice 1.** The definition of SIBO as a clinical entity lacks precision and consistency; it is a term generally applied to a clinical disorder in which symptoms, clinical signs, and/or laboratory abnormalities are attributed to changes in the numbers of bacteria or in the composition of the bacterial population in the small intestine.

**Best Practice Advice 2.** Symptoms traditionally linked to SIBO include bloating, diarrhea, and abdominal pain/discomfort. Steatorrhea can be seen in more severe cases.

**Best Practice Advice 3.** There is insufficient evidence to support the use of inflammatory markers, such as fecal calprotectin, to detect SIBO.

**Best Practice Advice 4.** Laboratory findings can include elevated folate and, less commonly, vitamin B-12 deficiency or other nutritional deficiencies.

**Best Practice Advice 5.** A major impediment to our ability to accurately define SIBO is our limited understanding of normal small intestinal microbial populations — progress in sampling technology and techniques to enumerate bacterial populations and their metabolic products should provide much needed clarity.

**Best Practice Advice 6.** Controversy remains concerning the role of SIBO in the pathogenesis of common functional symptoms, such as those regarded as components of IBS.

**Best Practice Advice 7.** Management of SIBO should focus on the identification and correction (where possible) of underlying causes, correction of nutritional deficiencies, and the administration of antibiotics. This is especially important for patients with significant maldigestion and malabsorption.

**Best Practice Advice 8.** Although IBS has been shown to respond to therapy with a poorly absorbed antibiotic, the role of SIBO or its eradication in the genesis of this response warrants further confirmation in randomized controlled trials.

**Best Practice Advice 9.** There is a limited database to guide the clinician in developing antibiotic strategies for SIBO, in any context. Therapy remains, for the most part, empiric, but must be ever mindful of the potential risks of long-term broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy.

## Summary

This AGA Clinical Practice Update is a deliberately deflationary document. Where the [[acg-2020-sibo|ACG 2020 guideline]] issues graded recommendations for whom to breath-test and how to treat, this CPU sets out to show that SIBO's definition "lacks precision and consistency" (BPA 1), that the small bowel microbiome is not yet characterized well enough to define the disorder (BPA 5), and that both the diagnostic tests and the antibiotic strategies rest on a thin database (BPA 9). It provides a historical account of how the concept arose — blind loops, jejunal diverticulosis, fistulae, pseudo-obstruction, with a demonstrable malabsorption mechanism — and argues that this classical, structurally-driven SIBO accounts for only "a small minority of those diagnosed with SIBO today."

On diagnosis, the CPU adopts the ACG's definition by citation ("the presence of excessive numbers of bacteria in the small bowel, causing gastrointestinal symptoms") and reports the North American Consensus culture threshold of **>10³ CFU/mL coliforms on fresh aspirate culture**, contextualizing it: normal subjects "rarely exceed 10² CFU/mL," and the older **>10⁵ CFU/mL** standard was "derived from subjects with altered intestinal anatomy." **It gives no breath-test cutoff of its own, no substrate preference, and no substrate dose.** Its treatment of breath testing is a list of objections: it "has lacked universal acceptance," "was never fully validated compared with culture," suffers "wide discrepancies in thresholds for defining a positive breath test result," and a proposed 2-H₂-peak criterion "has not been validated."

On treatment, the goal is reframed: "not to eradicate small intestinal microbiota but to modulate them in a manner that leads to symptomatic improvement." Susceptibility-directed therapy is impractical because species with differing sensitivities coexist, so therapy is empiric; an effective regimen "should cover both aerobic and anaerobic enteric bacteria." The CPU supplies the duration figure the ACG guideline omits — **a single 7- to 10-day course** improves symptoms for up to several months in **46%–90%** and normalizes breath tests in **20%–75%** — and reports rifaximin dosing as **800–1200 mg daily in divided doses**, materially lower than the ACG's 550 mg t.i.d. (1,650 mg/day). It also breaks with ACG on post-treatment retesting: "It is not necessary to repeat diagnostic tests for SIBO after antibiotic therapy should gastrointestinal symptoms respond."

The most consequential clinical reversal is on symptoms. Where ACG's Key Concept 1 states that bloating is the most common symptom of SIBO, this CPU states flatly: **"Contrary to common belief, diarrhea and not bloating has the strongest association with SIBO."**

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definition and its numeric history

- Definition adopted, verbatim: "the presence of excessive numbers of bacteria in the small bowel, causing gastrointestinal symptoms" — cited to the ACG 2020 guideline.
- Historical standard: **>10⁵ bacteria/mL of jejunal fluid**, the "now time-honored criterion," derived from intubation studies of the normal proximal small intestine and correlations between proximal-jejunal bacterial numbers and malabsorption parameters.
- **Revised threshold: >10³ CFU/mL coliforms on fresh aspirate culture** (North American Consensus, Rezaie 2017). Note the source's two qualifiers, both easy to lose: the count is of **coliforms**, and the specimen is a **fresh** aspirate.
- Justification for lowering it: bacterial levels in normal subjects "rarely exceed **10² CFU/mL**," and the **>10⁵ CFU/mL** figure was "derived from subjects with altered intestinal anatomy."
- The REIMAGINE large-scale culture + sequencing study "clearly identified that the ideal cutoff for SIBO was **>10³ CFU/mL**," and showed — for the first time — agreement between breath testing (rise in H₂ of **≥20 ppm** above baseline after lactulose), culture, sequencing, and symptoms.
- Counter-evidence, reported in the same paragraph: a study using the traditional single-lumen catheter and the **≥10⁵ CFU/mL** definition found the SIBO–symptom association "not supported," and suggested small-bowel microbial changes in functional disorders "might be related more to diet."
- "The centrality of quantitative intestinal cultures to the definition of SIBO has been challenged."

### Aspirate and culture — technique

- Duodenal aspiration requires invasive access (upper endoscopy); the sample must be acquired without oral-flora contamination as the scope passes the mouth.
- Standardized quantitative small-intestinal culture "long resided in a few academic medical centers"; not easily reproduced in community microbiology labs.
- Improved method: **double-lumen custom protected catheter** passed through the scope, mitigating contamination from the upper GI tract.
- Small intestinal fluid is mucoid, impeding isolation/culture and DNA extraction. Treating aspirates with the mucolytic **dithiothreitol** increased yields for culture, library preparation, and sequencing.

### Breath testing — what the CPU does and does not say

**This CPU issues no breath-testing recommendation and no cutoff.** Recorded exactly as written:

- "The use of breath testing in the diagnosis of SIBO has lacked universal acceptance."
- "Breath testing was never fully validated compared with culture of small intestinal aspirates."
- "Some studies prefer glucose as the substrate, others lactulose." — no preference is expressed.
- "There are also wide discrepancies in thresholds for defining a positive breath test result."
- On the 2-peak rule: "studies suggest that a positive breath test for SIBO requires 2 H₂ peaks; **this criterion has not been validated**."
- Physiology that matters for interpretation: **CH₄ is produced by methanogens; 4 moles of H₂ are required to produce 1 mole of CH₄; the presence of CH₄ suppresses H₂ on the breath test.** Conventional breath testing measures both H₂ and CH₄.
- The only ppm figure anywhere in the document is the **≥20 ppm rise above baseline after lactulose** used as the REIMAGINE study's criterion — reported as that study's method, not adopted as an AGA threshold.
- **No methane cutoff, no glucose dose, no lactulose dose, and no sampling schedule appear anywhere in this source.**
- Table 3 does credit breath testing with one thing: it "appears to identify subjects likely to respond to treatment."

### Symptoms and predictors

- Two numbered summary points, verbatim:
  1. "In those predisposed to SIBO due to anatomic, pathologic, pharmacologic, or other changes that promote stasis or recirculation of colonic contents and/or impaired resistance to bacteria, SIBO will lead to diarrhea and can progress to a full-blown malabsorption syndrome."
  2. "Relationships between SIBO and symptoms in those without obvious predisposing factors are more tenuous and symptoms are weakly predictive at best. **Contrary to common belief, diarrhea and not bloating has the strongest association with SIBO.**"
- "For the most part, diarrhea or 'gas,' but not bloating, seem to be the most predictive symptoms of SIBO in IBS."
- Large retrospective duodenal-aspirate series: main risk factors were **older age, steatorrhea, and the use of narcotics**; significantly associated disorders were **inflammatory bowel disease, chronic pancreatitis, and jejunal diverticulosis**.
- In the very elderly, SIBO is "an important cause of otherwise unexplained diarrhea and weight loss."
- Disease-specific predictors: in **Crohn's disease** — fibrostenotic phenotype and prior surgery, "especially resection of the ileocecal valve"; in **cirrhosis** — complications such as encephalopathy and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.
- SIBO has even been linked with obesity; correlations with nutritional status and outcomes across the Table 1 disorders are "far from consistent."
- The clinical rule of thumb offered (Table 3): "consider SIBO any time there is small intestinal stasis."

### Laboratory findings

- **Fecal calprotectin (BPA 3): insufficient evidence.** Results are mixed — one study failed to detect elevated levels in SIBO; studies in scleroderma and Crohn's disease suggested elevated levels were a valuable indicator.
- Commonly performed laboratory tests "will prove to be normal in most individuals considered to have SIBO today."
- Severe contamination → mucosal injury → loss of brush border enzymes → carbohydrate malabsorption; epithelial barrier damage → protein-losing enteropathy. Bacterial competition for luminal protein also contributes to hypoproteinemia and edema.
- **B-12 deficiency mechanisms (three):** consumption of cobalamin by anaerobes; malabsorption due to competitive binding at the ileal receptor by bacterially generated cobalamin metabolites; in more severe overgrowth, mucosal injury involving the binding site.
- Bacterial utilization of vitamins also invoked in **thiamine and nicotinamide deficiency**.
- Bile acid deconjugation → bile acid pool depletion → fat and fat-soluble vitamin maldigestion → steatorrhea and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency.
- **Vitamin K is the two-way exception.** A vitamin-K–responsive coagulopathy has been described with SIBO; but bacterial production of vitamin K plus enhanced absorption from greater permeability can sustain or even **increase** vitamin K levels "to a degree that warfarin dose might need to be adjusted to maintain therapeutic anticoagulation."
- Bacterial folic acid synthesis "may result in the unusual combination of **high folate and low B-12** levels."

### SIBO and IBS

- Systematic review/meta-analysis: SIBO more common in IBS than matched controls — **odds ratio 4.9** on breath testing; **up to 33.5%** of IBS subjects had SIBO **based on culture** vs **8.2%** in controls.
- Same meta-analysis: **lactulose breath testing yielded a higher rate of positive results than culture or glucose breath testing** — i.e. substrate choice changes the answer.
- CH₄-positive breath tests are linked to **constipation-predominant IBS**; SIBO is associated with IBS with diarrhea.
- Phase 3 studies support rifaximin in IBS-D. Response may relate to SIBO: baseline **positive breath test → 59.7% improved** vs **negative breath test → 29.8%**.
- Stated limitation of that finding: breath testing was conducted in only a small subset of IBS subjects.
- BPA 6 and BPA 8 both preserve the uncertainty — the role of SIBO in functional symptoms remains controversial, and rifaximin's IBS benefit is not established to work via SIBO eradication.

### Treatment

- **Goal, verbatim:** "The goal of antibiotic therapy in SIBO is not to eradicate small intestinal microbiota but to modulate them in a manner that leads to symptomatic improvement."
- Susceptibility-directed choice is "usually impractical as different bacterial species, with different antibiotic sensitivities typically coexist" → therapy is "primarily empirical."
- "In principle, an effective antibiotic regimen should cover **both aerobic and anaerobic enteric bacteria**."
- **Duration:** "In general, a single **7- to 10-day course** improves symptoms for up to several months in **46%–90%** of patients with SIBO and renders breath tests negative in **20%–75%**."
- **Rifaximin:** meta-analysis reported an overall **70% eradication rate** in SIBO. Trial dosing "ranged from 800 mg/d for 4 weeks to 1200 mg/d for 7 days." Table 2 regimen: **800–1200 mg daily in divided doses**.
- Practical barrier: "SIBO is not a recognized indication for this antibiotic and might not be covered by the patient's insurance carrier" (United States).
- **IMO:** "One uncontrolled trial suggested that the combination of **neomycin and rifaximin** might be effective." No doses are given in this source.
- **Recurrence: up to 44% at 9 months** after one antibiotic course; more likely among **older subjects, those who have undergone an appendectomy, and those with a history of chronic proton pump inhibitor use**.
- For recurrent symptoms, some patients need **repeated (eg, the first 5–10 days of every month) or continuous** courses. For continuous therapy, **rotating antibiotic regimens are recommended to prevent the development of resistance**.
- Individualize; weigh **diarrhea, *Clostridium difficile* infection, intolerance, and cost**.
- **Post-treatment testing, verbatim:** "It is not necessary to repeat diagnostic tests for SIBO after antibiotic therapy should gastrointestinal symptoms respond."
- BPA 7 order of operations: identify and correct underlying causes (blind loop, small bowel stricture, coloenteric fistula) → correct nutritional deficits when the underlying problem cannot be corrected → antibiotics.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

**Versus [[acg-2020-sibo]] (both tier-1; AGA is the later 2020 publication — Oct 2020 issue vs ACG's Jan/Feb 2020 — so the AGA claim is followed, with the disagreement stated):**

| Question | AGA 2020 CPU says | ACG 2020 guideline says |
|---|---|---|
| Cardinal symptom | "Contrary to common belief, **diarrhea** and not bloating has the strongest association with SIBO"; bloating/gas are only "thought to be" traditional symptoms | Key Concept 1: "The most common symptom of SIBO is **bloating**" |
| Breath-test cutoffs | **None given.** Thresholds are described as an unresolved "ongoing debate"; the test "was never fully validated" against culture | H₂ rise **≥20 ppm** above baseline within 90 min; CH₄ **≥10 ppm** at any point |
| Substrate and dose | No preference between glucose and lactulose; **no doses given**. Notes lactulose yields more positives than glucose or culture | **75 g glucose** or **10 g lactulose** with ~250 mL water |
| Whom to test | **No recommendation for or against testing in any population** | Graded conditional recommendations to test in IBS, suspected motility disorders, prior luminal abdominal surgery; against testing asymptomatic patients on PPIs |
| Culture threshold wording | "**>**10³ CFU/mL **coliforms** on **fresh** aspirate culture" | "**≥**10³ CFU/mL" duodenal/jejunal aspirate |
| [[rifaximin\|Rifaximin]] dose | **800–1200 mg daily in divided doses** (trials: 800 mg/d × 4 wk to 1200 mg/d × 7 d) | **550 mg t.i.d.** (= 1,650 mg/day) |
| Amoxicillin-clavulanate | **500/125 mg tid** | 875 mg b.i.d. |
| Ciprofloxacin | **250 mg bid** | 500 mg b.i.d. |
| Norfloxacin | **800 mg daily** | 400 mg q.d. |
| Duration | **7–10 days**, single course | Not specified |
| Retest after treatment | "**It is not necessary** to repeat diagnostic tests for SIBO after antibiotic therapy should gastrointestinal symptoms respond" | Key Concept 15: retesting "may correlate with symptom improvement and may be confirmed by normalization of hydrogen or methane levels" |
| Fecal calprotectin | BPA 3: insufficient evidence to use inflammatory markers to detect SIBO | Not addressed |

Agreements worth noting: both adopt the same clinical definition (the AGA CPU cites the ACG guideline for it), both accept the 10³ CFU/mL-order culture threshold as replacing 10⁵, both make antibiotics the mainstay, both prioritize correcting the underlying cause, and both report ~70% rifaximin eradication and ~44% recurrence at 9 months.

**Open questions the CPU itself raises:**

- Normal small intestinal microbial populations are not defined, so no threshold-based definition of SIBO can currently be validated (BPA 5). The definition will change with the technique used (culture vs next-generation sequencing).
- Whether SIBO causes functional/IBS symptoms at all remains unresolved (BPA 6); the two best available datasets disagree, and the negative study used the older ≥10⁵ definition and an unprotected single-lumen catheter — so the disagreement may be methodological rather than biological.
- Whether rifaximin's benefit in IBS-D operates through SIBO eradication needs RCT confirmation (BPA 8); the supporting breath-test data come from a small subset.
- No antibiotic regimen has a comparative evidence base; the whole of Table 2 is "regimens that have been used," not regimens shown to work.
- Retreatment/maintenance strategy (monthly pulses, continuous rotation) is offered without trial support, against an explicit warning about long-term broad-spectrum antibiotic risk (BPA 9).
