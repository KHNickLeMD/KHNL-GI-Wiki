## Summary

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1 have transformed oncology but produce a broad spectrum of immune-related adverse events (irAEs), of which gastrointestinal and hepatic toxicities are among the most common severe forms. Colitis — with or without enteritis — is the single most common GI toxicity, affecting up to 40% of patients depending on the pathway targeted. Severe enterocolitis requiring anti-inflammatory treatment and ICI delay or discontinuation affects 2%–5% of patients on PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and closer to 10% on CTLA-4 inhibitors. Mortality from GI irAEs is rare, but morbidity, treatment interruption, and discontinuation are frequent.

The update's central diagnostic message is that **symptoms do not track with disease**: patient symptoms correlate poorly with endoscopic severity, radiologic findings, and treatment response, and CTCAE grade is explicitly **not** predictive of who will need second-line immunosuppression. Endoscopic findings *do* predict treatment response, and **colonic ulceration is the only established predictor** of the need for second-line immune suppression. Consequently endoscopy with biopsy should be considered before starting high-dose systemic glucocorticoids. Infection must be excluded first, but infectious causes account for <5% of diarrhea on ICIs; conversely, 20%–30% of patients with suspected ICI enterocolitis have no mucosal inflammation at all. Stool inflammatory markers (lactoferrin, calprotectin) are positioned as the triage step that selects who goes to endoscopy. Cross-sectional imaging has a limited role and should not be ordered for diarrhea alone.

Treatment is high-dose systemic glucocorticoids (0.5–2 mg/kg prednisone equivalent daily, taper 4–6 weeks), with the explicit caveat that these doses and schedules have never been rigorously examined. About one-third of patients respond inadequately. Infliximab (5 mg/kg IV) and vedolizumab (300 mg IV), dosed at weeks 0, 2, and 6 on IBD-derived schedules, are both effective for glucocorticoid-refractory disease; no data distinguish them, so the choice turns on malignancy type, infection risk, and concurrent irAEs. Response is typically rapid — under a week — an important contrast with IBD. Budesonide failed as prophylaxis in an RCT but is effective for the distinct entity of ICI-associated **microscopic colitis**.

ICI hepatitis is less common (<5% with monotherapy, rising to ~25% with ipilimumab + nivolumab) and is managed by CTCAE grade, with baseline and per-cycle liver chemistries plus pre-treatment HBV serologies for all patients. The gastroenterologist/hepatologist's primary role is excluding competing etiologies — the differential is wide in patients with cancer — and managing steroid non-responders. Notably, infliximab, the workhorse of refractory ICI colitis, should be used with **caution in ICI hepatitis** because of idiosyncratic liver toxicity and absence of demonstrated benefit.

## Best Practice Advice Statements (all 15, verbatim)

**Best Practice Advice 1:** Infectious causes of diarrhea should be excluded before treatment of suspected ICI colitis.

**Best Practice Advice 2:** Early stool testing for inflammatory markers (lactoferrin and calprotectin) in patients with grade 2 or higher colitis/diarrhea (more than 4 bowel movements daily above baseline) according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, Version 5 (CTCAE) and selected patients with less-severe diarrhea may help stratify high-risk patients for endoscopic evaluation.

**Best Practice Advice 3:** Endoscopic confirmation of the diagnosis and severity of ICI colitis should be considered before initiation of high-dose systemic glucocorticoids.

**Best Practice Advice 4:** Abdominal imaging may be considered to exclude serious complications in patients with dominant symptoms of pain, fever, or bleeding, but should not be performed routinely in patients with diarrhea alone.

**Best Practice Advice 5:** Rapid progression of ICI colitis may occur within a period of days, particularly in patients treated with ipilimumab and, therefore, prompt diagnosis and treatment is required.

**Best Practice Advice 6:** ICI colitis typically responds to high-dose systemic glucocorticoids, given in doses of 0.5–2 mg/kg prednisone equivalent daily with a taper of 4–6 weeks; however, these doses and schedules have not been rigorously examined. Infliximab and vedolizumab are reasonable options for treatment of glucocorticoid refractory colitis.

**Best Practice Advice 7:** Budesonide is ineffective as prophylactic treatment for ICI colitis, but may be used for treatment of ICI-associated microscopic colitis.

**Best Practice Advice 8:** Patients who develop ICI colitis may be retreated with immunotherapy under select conditions.

**Best Practice Advice 9:** Patients with inflammatory bowel disease may have an increased risk of ICI-associated gastrointestinal adverse events, but may derive cancer treatment benefit from checkpoint blockade.

**Best Practice Advice 10:** All patients undergoing ICI therapy should undergo baseline evaluation of liver chemistries (total bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, aspartate aminotransferase [AST], and alanine aminotransferase [ALT]), as well as pretreatment screening for hepatitis B virus serologies (hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis B core antibody, and hepatitis B surface antibody). Liver chemistries should be repeated before each ICI treatment cycle, with management based on CTCAE grade.

**Best Practice Advice 11:** For patients with CTCAE grade 1 hepatitis (AST/ALT 1–3× the upper limit of normal [ULN] or total bilirubin 1–1.5× ULN), liver monitoring should be repeated 1–2 times weekly. For patients with CTCAE grade 2 hepatitis (AST/ALT >3–5× ULN or total bilirubin >1.5–3× ULN), ICI therapy should be held until resolution to grade 1; for patients with clinical symptoms of liver toxicity, prednisone 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/d or equivalent may be administered.

**Best Practice Advice 12:** For patients with grade 3 hepatitis (AST/ALT >5–20× ULN or total bilirubin >3–10× ULN), ICI should be discontinued, urgent gastrointestinal/liver consultation is advised, and glucocorticoids should be initiated at a dose of 1–2 mg/kg methylprednisolone or equivalent. Second-line immunomodulators, such as azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil, may be considered in patients who fail to demonstrate improvement in clinical hepatitis within 3–5 days.

**Best Practice Advice 13:** For patients with CTCAE grade 4 hepatitis (AST/ALT >20× ULN or total bilirubin >10× ULN or hepatic decompensation, such as ascites or encephalopathy) hospitalization is appropriate, preferably at a referral center with expertise in the care of patients with liver failure. ICI should be permanently discontinued, and patients started on 2 mg/kg/d methylprednisolone or equivalent.

**Best Practice Advice 14:** All patients who develop elevated liver chemistries on ICI therapy should undergo diagnostic evaluation for alternate etiologies of liver injury, including consideration of a liver biopsy.

**Best Practice Advice 15:** Patients who develop elevated alkaline phosphatase and/or bilirubin on ICI therapy should undergo biliary imaging to assess for biliary obstruction with hepatic ultrasound, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography, or endoscopic ultrasound.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Epidemiology
- Colitis ± enteritis is the **single most common GI toxicity** — up to **40%** of patients, depending on pathway targeted.
- **Severe** enterocolitis (requiring anti-inflammatory treatment + ICI delay/discontinuation): **2%–5%** on PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, **~10%** on CTLA-4 inhibitors.
- Isolated severe **gastritis** without small bowel/colonic inflammation is rare; isolated upper GI inflammation (gastritis, gastroenteritis, enteritis) occurs at a frequency **likely >10%**.
- ICI **hepatitis**: incidence **<5%** in monotherapy trials; **~25%** with ipilimumab + nivolumab combination. Also amplified by chemotherapy and targeted-therapy combinations — suggesting ICIs sensitize the liver to other drug injury.
- Less common ICI GI/hepatobiliary toxicities: cholangitis, pancreatitis, potentially cholecystitis.

### CTCAE v5 grading (Table 1, recreated)

| Variable | Grade 1 | Grade 2 | Grade 3 | Grade 4 | Grade 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Diarrhea** | Increase of <4 stools/d over baseline; mild increase in ostomy output vs baseline | Increase of 4–6 stools/d over baseline; moderate increase in ostomy output vs baseline; limiting instrumental ADL | Increase of ≥7 stools/d over baseline; hospitalization indicated; severe increase in ostomy output; limiting self-care ADL | Life-threatening consequences; urgent intervention indicated | Death |
| **Enterocolitis** | Asymptomatic; clinical or diagnostic observations only; intervention not indicated | Abdominal pain; mucus or blood in stool | Severe or persistent abdominal pain; fever; ileus; peritoneal signs | Life-threatening consequences; urgent intervention indicated | Death |

### Diagnosis of ICI enterocolitis
- **Symptoms do not correlate** with endoscopic findings and **do not predict** treatment response. **Endoscopic findings do** predict response.
- **Colonic ulceration is the only established factor** predicting how ICI enterocolitis responds to treatment, and the only identified predictor of needing second-line immunosuppression. **CTCAE grading is not predictive.**
- **20%–30%** of patients with suspected ICI enterocolitis have **no mucosal inflammation** (possibly fewer among ipilimumab-treated).
- Infectious causes account for **<5%** of diarrhea on ICIs — but must still be excluded in all patients before immunosuppression. Test: *C. difficile* and stool cultures in all (stool pathogen panels a reasonable alternative); ova & parasite testing based on risk factors and local prevalence.
- **Stool lactoferrin sensitivity up to 90%** for histologic inflammation (retrospective data) — the basis for using lactoferrin/calprotectin to triage to endoscopy.
- **~95%** of patients have inflammation in the **left colon** on biopsy, mostly visible macroscopically → **flexible sigmoidoscopy is often adequate** for diagnosis. Pancolitis is the most common pattern.
- **EGD is reasonable** in patients with a negative flexible sigmoidoscopy, and **may be higher yield than proceeding to full colonoscopy**.
- CT sensitivity **53%–85%**, specificity **75%–78%** → relatively low negative predictive value; the CT pattern is indistinguishable from infectious and ischemic colitis. **Diarrhea alone should not warrant abdominal imaging.**
- Other diagnoses to consider: immune-mediated **pancreatic insufficiency** (fecal elastase + spot/qualitative fecal fat if steatorrhea or non-response); **new-onset celiac disease** (tissue transglutaminase IgA + total IgA); thyroid dysfunction.
- Before biologic immunosuppression: test **hepatitis B** (HBsAg, anti-HBs, anti-HBc) and **latent tuberculosis** if not done pre-ICI; HIV and HCV testing also suggested.
- Blood tests are rarely informative — WBC, CRP, ESR may be elevated but specificity is low given concurrent extraintestinal irAEs and ongoing antitumor responses.

### Treatment of ICI enterocolitis
- **First line:** systemic glucocorticoids **0.5–2 mg/kg prednisone equivalent daily** (oral or IV), taper over **4–6 weeks**. Doses/schedules not rigorously examined; lower doses or steroid-sparing strategies may have benefit, as glucocorticoids may dampen antitumor responses.
- **~One-third** have an inadequate response to first-line glucocorticoids.
- **Trigger for second-line:** no response within **72 hours**, or no complete response within **1 week**, or recurrence during the steroid taper / after completing the course.
- **Second-line biologics** — no data distinguish them:
  - **Infliximab 5 mg/kg IV**, weeks **0, 2, 6**
  - **Vedolizumab 300 mg IV**, weeks **0, 2, 6**
  - Response typically **<1 week** — a key contrast with IBD.
- **Choosing between them** (based on other risk factors — underlying malignancy, comorbidities, infection risk, expected treatment duration, other irAEs):
  - **Avoid infliximab** in **hematologic malignancy** (TNF-α inhibitors associated with rare lymphomas); use with **caution in severe congestive heart failure**.
  - Infliximab in a patient with **both ICI hepatitis and enterocolitis** — case-by-case, since infliximab can induce a rare hepatitis.
  - **Vedolizumab** may interfere with antitumor responses in the GI mucosa in primary GI malignancies or tumors with GI metastases.
- **Non-response to the first biologic → switch class** (infliximab ↔ vedolizumab). **Do not wait for a standard washout**; treat within a few weeks of the last infusion once non-response is evident.
- **Failing both:** not well established. FMT reported in 2 patients. Ustekinumab (p40 inhibitor), tofacitinib (JAK inhibitor), and abatacept (CTLA-4-Ig) may be considered in life-threatening cases — each carries substantial risk of interfering with antitumor responses.
- **Budesonide:** an RCT of colonically formulated budesonide as **prophylaxis** against ipilimumab enterocolitis showed **no benefit**. But for **ICI-associated microscopic colitis** — defined as histologic lymphocytic inflammation in the colon **without macroscopic signs of inflammation or small bowel involvement** — patients were highly responsive to colonic budesonide, and many were able to **continue their ICI** on budesonide.

### Retreatment and high-risk populations
- Retreatment with immunotherapy is possible **under select conditions**, particularly when no alternative effective cancer therapy exists. Risk data are entirely retrospective and likely underestimate risk in unselected patients.
- Risk of **recurrent ICI colitis on retreatment ≈ 30%** for most regimens; **higher** in patients who developed enterocolitis on PD-1/PD-L1 and then switched to **CTLA-4** inhibitors. Switching from ipilimumab → PD-1/PD-L1 generally carries lower risk.
- Maintenance infliximab or vedolizumab may reduce recurrence in patients continuing immunotherapy (small case series); long-term safety and effect on tumor response undetermined.
- Even with recurrent ICI enterocolitis, standard therapies typically remain effective.
- **Enterocolitis before ICI initiation is not an absolute contraindication** to future immunotherapy if ICIs are the only available option.
- **IBD:** in a >100-patient multicenter retrospective analysis (2020, evenly split UC/Crohn's, nearly all quiescent, 50% off IBD-directed therapy, mostly PD-1/PD-L1): **GI irAE rate 41%**, **severe GI irAEs 21%** (including perforations), vs **11%** GI irAE rate in a multicenter control cohort. Tumor responses were **similar to the general population** and **no patients died from GI irAEs**. IBD patients can be cautiously evaluated and treated on an individual basis with close oncology–GI collaboration.

### ICI hepatitis
- Histology: majority show **lymphocytic or granulomatous hepatocellular injury**; **fibrin ring granulomas** are a specific subset seen with PD-1 blockade. **Plasma cell infiltration — characteristic of autoimmune hepatitis — is less common in ICI hepatitis**, suggesting a distinct immune etiology.
- **Differential (all patients with elevated liver chemistries must be evaluated):** hepatic metastasis, thromboembolic disease, biliary compression, perfusion injury, opportunistic infection, drug reactions/DILI. Test hepatotropic viruses (HAV, HBV, HCV, HEV) and non-hepatotropic viruses (EBV, CMV, HSV); evaluate hepatobiliary tree and hepatic vasculature with abdominal ultrasound.
- **Elevated ALP and/or bilirubin → cross-sectional hepatobiliary imaging** (CT/MRI). If risk factors for biliary obstruction (hepatic or intra-abdominal metastases) with a normal ultrasound → **MRCP or EUS**. **ERCP** may be necessary when anatomic obstruction or stricture is seen on imaging.
- **Liver biopsy** should be considered at **grade 2 or higher** once competing etiologies are excluded, ideally **before starting glucocorticoids** to maximize diagnostic yield.
- **Grade-directed management:**

| CTCAE grade | AST/ALT | Total bilirubin | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1–3× ULN | 1–1.5× ULN | Monitor liver chemistries **1–2×/week**; ± delay ICI dosing |
| 2 | >3–5× ULN | >1.5–3× ULN | **Hold ICI** until resolution to grade 1; GI/hepatology consult; stop potential hepatotoxins if feasible; if symptomatic → **prednisone 0.5–1.0 mg/kg/d**. Resume ICI when recovered to grade 1 on **≤10 mg prednisone/d**. If unresolved after a 1–2 week ICI delay → give systemic glucocorticoids |
| 3 | >5–20× ULN | >3–10× ULN | **Discontinue ICI**; urgent GI/hepatology consult; **methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg** with planned 4–6 week taper; biopsy + hospitalization case-by-case; if no response within **3–5 days** → second-line (azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or tacrolimus) |
| 4 | >20× ULN | >10× ULN, or hepatic decompensation (ascites, encephalopathy) | **Hospitalize**, preferably at a liver-failure referral center; **permanently discontinue ICI**; **methylprednisolone 2 mg/kg/d** |

- **Infliximab should be used with caution in suspected ICI hepatitis** — potential idiosyncratic liver toxicity and no demonstrated clinical benefit in this population.
- Steroid doses for ICI hepatitis were **empirically determined and have not been rigorously examined**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **CTCAE grade vs endoscopic findings.** The CPU is explicit that CTCAE grading is **not** predictive of needing second-line immunosuppression, while colonic ulceration is. Oncology-side management algorithms are built on CTCAE grade — the wiki should carry both and name the discordance rather than flattening it.
- **Infliximab, opposite directions in the two organs.** Infliximab is a first-choice rescue for glucocorticoid-refractory ICI **colitis** but is to be used with **caution** in ICI **hepatitis**. In a patient with both, the CPU explicitly defers to case-by-case judgment.
- **Infliximab and cancer outcomes.** One report suggests worse cancer outcomes with infliximab; the CPU cautions that steroid dose and duration were not adequately controlled for.
- **Steroid dosing is unvalidated in both organs.** The 0.5–2 mg/kg / 4–6 week taper (colitis) and the methylprednisolone doses (hepatitis) are stated to be empirically derived and never rigorously examined. Flag as low-quality evidence wherever these numbers appear on entity pages.
- **PPI/biologic choice not resolved.** No data distinguish infliximab from vedolizumab as initial biologic for steroid-refractory ICI colitis; the recommendation is explicitly risk-factor-based, not efficacy-based.
- **No validated severity index exists** for ICI colitis. Two competing endoscopic tools (MD Anderson, Mayo) are presented side by side without a preference.
- **Open questions named in the source:** optimal endoscopic examination for diagnosis is not established; the importance of tissue diagnosis has not been rigorously evaluated; treatment after failing both biologics is not established; the relationship between ICI-hepatitis histologic subtypes and outcome/treatment response is uncertain; long-term safety of maintenance immunosuppression during ongoing ICI therapy is undetermined. AIM-NIVO (NCT03816345) was enrolling to provide the first prospective data on ICI treatment in IBD patients.
