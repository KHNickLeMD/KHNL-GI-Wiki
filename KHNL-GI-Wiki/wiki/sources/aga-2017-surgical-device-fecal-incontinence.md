## Summary

This update covers what to do for fecal incontinence (FI) and defecatory disorders (DDs) **after conservative therapy has genuinely failed** — the surgical and device-aided tier. Its central clinical argument is that the failure is usually in the conservative trial, not in the patient: in the authors' experience many patients labelled refractory *"indeed have not received an optimal trial of conservative therapy,"* and the update specifies what an optimal trial contains before any device or operation is considered — characterization of bowel habits and prior treatment, a dietary history hunting poorly absorbed sugars, an antidiarrheal trial, and anorectal testing for evacuation disorders in patients with constipation.

For FI the recommended sequence is stepwise (Best Practice Advice 1) and is drawn as a single algorithm in Figure 1: clinical evaluation with digital rectal examination → treat the underlying disease, bowel disturbance and local anorectal problem → for persistent symptoms, anorectal manometry with rectal sensation and balloon expulsion → pelvic floor retraining → outlet barrier device → sacral nerve stimulation (SNS) or perianal bulking → and only then colostomy, sphincteroplasty, artificial sphincter or gracilis transposition, or the magnetic anal sphincter. Roughly **25% of patients** are expected to benefit from conservative measures alone. Among the surgical options **SNS is the one the authors call safe and effective**; the evidence for the rest is weaker, and two device-aided therapies are advised against outright — **percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) for FI** (Best Practice Advice 5) and **SNS for defecatory disorders** (Best Practice Advice 13).

The defecatory-disorder half is largely negative. Biofeedback is the treatment (Best Practice Advice 12); anterograde colonic enemas are not an effective long-term solution in adults (Best Practice Advice 14); and the stapled transanal rectal resection (STARR) procedure should not be routinely performed to correct structural abnormalities (Best Practice Advice 15), because the correlation between symptoms and anatomy is weak in both directions and the structural lesions are probably *caused* by the underlying functional disorder that the operation does not correct.

The update closes by sizing the problem: in the authors' experience **surgery is necessary in a very small fraction, perhaps less than 5% of patients with DD**, generally those with considerable pelvic organ and/or rectal prolapse, and there is a critical need for trials comparing surgical against conservative therapies.

## Best Practice Advice (all fifteen, verbatim)

> No statement in this document carries a GRADE, strength or quality rating.

**Best Practice Advice 1:** "A stepwise approach should be followed for management of FI. Conservative therapies (diet, fluids, techniques to improve evacuation, a bowel training program, management of diarrhea and constipation with diet and medications if necessary) will benefit approximately 25% of patients and should be tried first."

**Best Practice Advice 2:** "Pelvic floor retraining with biofeedback therapy is recommended for patients with FI who do not respond to the conservative measures indicated above."

**Best Practice Advice 3:** "Perianal bulking agents such as intra-anal injection of dextranomer may be considered when conservative measures and biofeedback therapy fail."

**Best Practice Advice 4:** "Sacral nerve stimulation should be considered for patients with moderate or severe FI in whom symptoms have not responded after a 3-month or longer trial of conservative measures and biofeedback therapy and who do not have contraindications to these procedures."

**Best Practice Advice 5:** "Until further evidence is available, percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation should not be used for managing FI in clinical practice."

**Best Practice Advice 6:** "Barrier devices should be offered to patients who have failed conservative or surgical therapy, or in those who have failed conservative therapy who do not want or are not eligible for more invasive interventions."

**Best Practice Advice 7:** "Anal sphincter repair (sphincteroplasty) should be considered in postpartum women with FI and in patients with recent sphincter injuries. In patients who present later with symptoms of FI unresponsive to conservative and biofeedback therapy and evidence of sphincter damage, sphincteroplasty may be considered when perianal bulking injection and sacral nerve stimulation are not available or have proven unsuccessful."

**Best Practice Advice 8:** "The artificial anal sphincter, dynamic graciloplasty, may be considered for patients with medically refractory severe FI who have failed treatment or are not candidates for barrier devices, sacral nerve stimulation, perianal bulking injection, sphincteroplasty and a colostomy."

**Best Practice Advice 9:** "Major anatomic defects (eg, rectovaginal fistula, full-thickness rectal prolapse, fistula in ano, or cloaca-like deformity) should be rectified with surgery."

**Best Practice Advice 10:** "A colostomy should be considered in patients with severe FI who have failed conservative treatment and are not candidates for barrier devices, minimally invasive surgical interventions, and sphincteroplasty."

**Best Practice Advice 11:** "A magnetic anal sphincter device may be considered for patients with medically refractory severe FI who have failed or are not candidates for barrier devices, perianal bulking injection, sacral nerve stimulation, sphincteroplasty, or a colostomy. Data regarding efficacy are limited and 40% of patients had moderate or severe complications."

**Best Practice Advice 12:** "For defecatory disorders, biofeedback therapy is the treatment of choice."

**Best Practice Advice 13:** "Based on limited evidence, sacral nerve stimulation should not be used for managing defecatory disorders in clinical practice."

**Best Practice Advice 14:** "Anterograde colonic enemas are not effective in the long term for management of defecatory disorders."

**Best Practice Advice 15:** "The stapled transanal rectal resection and related procedures should not be routinely performed for correction of structural abnormalities in patients with defecatory disorders."

## Key Findings / Claims

### Figure 1 — the stepwise FI algorithm (the document's only decision figure)

Clinical evaluation including digital rectal examination → two parallel arms (treat underlying disease and manage bowel disturbances; treat local anorectal problem) → persistent symptoms → anorectal manometry, rectal sensation and balloon expulsion → pelvic floor retraining as appropriate → persistent symptoms → outlet barrier device → persistent symptoms → consider sacral nerve stimulation or perianal bulking if appropriate → persistent symptoms → one of four terminal options: **colostomy · anal sphincteroplasty · artificial anal sphincter or gracilis transposition · magnetic anal sphincter**. ### Epidemiology and risk factors

- FI = the **recurrent uncontrolled passage of liquid or solid stool**; affects **7% to 15%** of community-dwelling women and men, higher among care-seeking populations, home-care populations, and adults in long-term care settings.
- Because of social stigma many people **do not share the condition with friends or physicians** — physicians should routinely **screen** patients at risk.
- **Independent risk factors for FI in a community-based cohort of 176 women with FI vs 176 without**, as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI):

| Risk factor | OR (95% CI) |
|---|---|
| Diarrhea | **53** (6.1–471) |
| Cholecystectomy | **4.2** (1.2–15) |
| Current smoking | **4.7** (1.4–15) |
| History of rectocele | **4.9** (1.3–19) |
| Stress urinary incontinence | **3.1** (1.4–6.5) |
| Higher body mass index (per unit increase) | **1.1** (1.004–1.1) |

- **Bowel disturbances, particularly diarrhea, and burden of chronic illness — rather than obstetric history (eg, forceps use, complicated episiotomy) — are by far the most important independent risk factors for FI in older women.**
- Other associations: advanced age, disease burden (comorbidity count, diabetes), anal sphincter trauma (obstetrical injury, prior surgery), decreased physical activity. FI may be secondary to diseases causing anorectal inflammation (inflammatory bowel disease), peripheral neuropathy (diabetes), iatrogenic anal sphincter injury, or neurologic disorders (dementia, stroke, spinal cord injury or disease).

### Severity and its relationship to quality of life

- In clinical trials severity is evaluated primarily by the **frequency of FI using daily diaries**; a **50% reduction in the number of episodes or days with FI is considered to be clinically significant improvement**.
- Questionnaire-based instruments named: **Wexner and Modified Manchester Health Questionnaires, the Fecal Incontinence Severity Score** (previously known as the Fecal Incontinence and Constipation Assessment instrument), and the **Fecal Incontinence Severity Index**. All incorporate the frequency and type of leakage; some also incorporate the volume of leakage and rectal urgency.
- **Severity of FI is correlated strongly with its impact on quality of life.**

### What an optimal conservative trial contains (the operative content behind Best Practice Advice 1)

Conservative measures: dietary modification, fiber supplements, fluids, techniques to improve evacuation such as **scheduled toileting**, a bowel training program, pelvic floor exercises to strengthen musculature, and medications to manage diarrhea and constipation. Then pelvic floor retraining with biofeedback therapy.

In the authors' experience many patients considered refractory have **not** had an optimal trial, which includes 1 or more of the following as appropriate:

1. **Meticulous characterization** of the bowel habits, the circumstances surrounding FI (eg, relationship to meals and activity), and prior treatment for FI.
2. Among patients with diarrhea, a **careful dietary history to identify ingestion of poorly absorbed sugars** (eg, sorbitol, fructose) followed by a trial of elimination.
3. For diarrhea, **loperamide (2 mg), generally starting with 1 tablet taken 30 minutes before breakfast and titrated as necessary up to 16 mg/d**. Fiber supplementation can be used to improve stool consistency and reduces diarrhea-associated FI. Because bile-salt malabsorption is common in patients with idiopathic diarrhea, **cholestyramine or colesevelam** may be helpful. **Anticholinergic agents and clonidine** are alternative options for patients with diarrhea and FI.
4. **Laxatives and anorectal testing to identify evacuation disorders** are recommended for patients with constipation. In particular, patients with **fecal seepage** suffer from evacuation disorders with overflow of retained stool in the rectum; these are managed effectively with pelvic floor biofeedback therapy directed at the underlying rectal evacuation disorder. Alternatively, or in addition, **rectal cleansing with a small enema or tap water** reduces the likelihood of stool leakage.

Patients truly refractory to conservative measures should undergo anorectal testing — **anorectal manometry first, followed if necessary by anorectal imaging**. These patients are eligible for surgical therapy or devices.

### Anorectal testing before surgery or devices

- **Anal manometry** identifies anal weakness, reduced or increased rectal sensation, and impaired rectal balloon expulsion — dysfunctions associated with FI and amenable to pelvic floor biofeedback therapy.
- **Imaging should be considered, in particular before surgery or devices.**
- **Endoanal ultrasound (US) vs magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) — the modality split, stated explicitly both ways:** internal sphincter tears are **easier to visualize with endoanal US than MRI**; by contrast, **MRI is superior for visualizing external sphincter defects and atrophy and a patulous anal canal**.

### Perianal bulking injection

- **Dextranomer microspheres in non–animal-stabilized hyaluronic acid (NASHA Dx) is the only Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–approved product for FI.**
- Although the implied mechanism is to enhance the seal of the anal canal, **dextranomer did not significantly increase anal resting or squeeze pressures and was not superior to biofeedback therapy.**
- Randomized, double-blind, sham-controlled study in adults who had failed conservative therapies: treatment response (≥50% improvement from baseline in number of FI episodes) at 6 months in **71 of 136 (52%)** active vs **22 of 70 (31%)** sham (OR 2.36; *P* = .0089). A second injection was given to 112 patients (82%) in the NASHA Dx group and a sham injection to 61 patients (45%).
- At 6 months the NASHA Dx group also had significantly more incontinence-free days and improved FI quality of life (FIQOL) **coping and behavior**, but **not** lifestyle, depression and self-perception, or embarrassment scores.
- Most common adverse events: **proctalgia 14%, fever 8%, rectal bleeding 7%**; no apparent migration, protrusion, or leakage of NASHA Dx was observed during bowel movements.
- **Durability:** at 12 and 24 months, **64.0% of 86** and **62.7% of 83** patients were responders in the NASHA Dx and sham groups **respectively** (≥50% improvement from baseline in number of FI episodes).

### Sacral nerve stimulation for fecal incontinence

- Continuous pulsed electrical stimulation of the sacral nerves with a battery-operated stimulator. Stimulation is initially provided by an **external electrical stimulator for 2 to 3 weeks**; **if the frequency of FI decreases by 50% or more, a stimulator is implanted permanently beneath the skin.**
- Pivotal uncontrolled US multicenter trial: **90% of 133 patients proceeded from temporary to permanent stimulation.** Among **76 of 120 patients (63%) with 5-year follow-up data, 36% reported complete continence** and **89% were deemed a therapeutic success**.
- Two parallel-group RCTs compared SNS with medical treatment and with PTNS: **SNS was significantly better than medical treatment but not significantly better than PTNS.**
- **Most trials were limited to patients with a structurally intact anal sphincter or a defect smaller than 120°.** A few studies suggest SNS may be beneficial irrespective of the degree of sphincter injury, but larger prospective trials are needed — **the efficacy of SNS in patients with larger external sphincter defects is unknown.**
- **Batteries must be replaced after approximately 7 years.** Most common adverse events are **pain and infection at the insertion site, which occurs in up to 10% of patients**.
- In **6 cross-over studies**, patients experienced **equal symptoms with the stimulator on or off**. The discrepancy between symptom improvement and relatively minor effects on anorectal functions is *"puzzling"*; improved continence may be explained by SNS-induced colonic retrograde propagated sequences, which may be anticipated to delay colonic transit.

### Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (the basis for Best Practice Advice 5)

- The posterior tibial nerve can be stimulated via a skin-surface electrode (transcutaneous) or a needle (percutaneous). In predominantly uncontrolled studies, **52% to 82%** of patients reported a ≥50% reduction in FI frequency with PTNS.
- Small randomized trial (n = 30): ≥50% reduction in FI episodes in **9 of 11 (82%)** percutaneous, **5 of 11 (45%)** transcutaneous, **1 of 8 (13%)** sham.
- **The large multicenter RCT that followed observed that transcutaneous stimulation was not significantly better than sham stimulation** (provided by a needle inserted to 2 mm but no electrical stimulation). During that study, **loperamide use was reduced by 29% of patients on PTNS but only 11% in the placebo group; differences were not significant (*P* = .06).**

### Barrier devices

- A Cochrane review identified 4 randomized cross-over or parallel studies of anal plugs or barrier devices. **None of these devices are available in the United States.** Although anal plugs might be helpful in some patients, they are **poorly tolerated, with a drop-out rate ranging from 12.5% to 68% across the 4 studies**.
- **Renew anal insert device** (Renew Medical, Foster City, CA), designed to be better tolerated than other plugs, approved by the FDA: open-label study — **62% reported a ≥50% reduction in FI frequency, and 78% of users were extremely satisfied**. No serious adverse events; only **3 moderate adverse events** (fecal urgency, soreness, bleeding hemorrhoids).
- **Vaginal insert and pressure-regulated pump**, prospective open-label study in women with FI: of 110 participants, **61 completed successful fitting**, of whom **78.7% achieved treatment success** (>50% reduction of incontinent episodes at 1 month). Secondary analysis also showed a reduction in stool frequency, urgency, liquid stool, and incomplete evacuations.

### Anal sphincteroplasty

- End-to-end or overlap repair. **Postoperative complication rates generally are low; rates for the most commonly reported complication of wound infection range from 6% to 35%.**
- **Success rates decrease with time**: in one study **only 28% were continent at 40 months**, and the **predicted median time to relapse of FI after sphincteroplasty is 5 years**. Given these data, anal sphincteroplasty is **reserved primarily for women with postpartum FI**.
- Technical and patient-related factors influencing prognosis are **not clearly established**; age, sex, extent of sphincter injury, etiology, duration of FI, presence or absence of pudendal neuropathy and surgical technique have all been considered as potential factors, but **none has shown a clear correlation with outcomes**.
- One retrospective comparison of sphincteroplasty with SNS **did not clearly show superiority of either intervention**. Given these limitations, **newer modalities with minimally invasive approaches soon may be considered the preferred first-line surgical approach to treatment of FI, except in patients with recent or acute sphincter injuries.**

### Artificial bowel sphincter and dynamic graciloplasty

- **Artificial bowel sphincter** = an inflatable cuff acting as a new sphincter, a control pump, and a balloon regulating pressure and acting as a fluid reservoir. Large single-center study (n = 52 patients, 85 devices; mean follow-up >5 years): **full continence seldom achieved, 14% had device-related infections, 32% required explantation.** Other investigators have reported **higher rates of complications and explantations**.
- **Dynamic graciloplasty** uses the patient's gracilis muscle to encircle the anus and form a new sphincter, with an electrical stimulator implanted in the abdominal wall to sustain its tone. Multicenter international trial: success (≥50% reduction in incontinent episodes) in **47 of 76 (62%) at 12 months, 37 of 67 (55%) at 18 months, and 35 of 62 (56%) at 2 years**. A systematic review reported success rates of **42% to 85%**, most common adverse events **infection 28%, stimulator malfunction 15%, leg pain 13%**.
- **Very few reports and no clinical trials of this procedure have been published during the past few years, suggesting that treatment is not performed routinely.**

### Radiofrequency anal sphincter remodeling (Secca)

- Delivers temperature-controlled radiofrequency energy (**465 kHz, 2–5 W**) to the anorectal junction to remodel, scar and contract collagen tissues in the anal region.
- **Manometry and endoanal ultrasound tests did not show any changes.**
- 5-year follow-up evaluation of 19 patients: sustained improvements in FI symptoms and FIQOL vs baseline. Other studies reported modest improvements in 1 or both measures over shorter durations.
- Review of **10 Secca studies comprising 220 patients: FI improved in 55% to 80%; complications occurred in 20% of patients.** Most studies included small numbers of patients and most were conducted more than 8 years ago.
- **There are no randomized controlled trials, but it has been FDA approved since 2002** for patients who have failed conservative therapy for FI.

### Colostomy

- Fecal diversion offers **definitive therapy** for FI in patients who have failed or are not suitable for standard conservative surgical treatments. Despite its curative potential, colostomy is not commonly used because of concerns for poor quality of life, particularly in the domains of psychological and social function.
- **However**, in 1 cross-sectional survey study, patients with colostomy formation reported **higher social function scores on the Short Form-36 as well as higher coping, embarrassment, lifestyle, and depression scores on the FIQOL** compared with patients with FI. Another survey found **84% of patients who had a colostomy for FI would choose to have it again.** Generalizability may be limited owing to patient selection.
- **Mortality rates for colostomy are approximately 2%**; associated complications may include bleeding, cardiopulmonary events secondary to anesthesia, and parastomal hernia. Major long-term stomal problems may include rashes, leakage, and ballooning.

### Magnetic anal sphincter

- A series of interlinked titanium beads with internal magnetic cores forming a flexible ring, encircling the external anal sphincter and creating a barrier. During defecation the beads separate, allowing stool to pass.
- Prospective, nonrandomized, matched study (n = 20): FI severity and FIQOL scores improved significantly after implantation of a magnetic **or** artificial anal sphincter. **No significant differences in early postoperative complications**, but **surgical time (62 vs 97 min) and hospital stay (4.5 vs 10 d) were shorter for the magnetic sphincter group.**
- Approved in several European countries and, through the **humanitarian device exemption process, by the US FDA**. FDA approval is based on a report of 35 patients and is for patients with FI *"who are not candidates for or have previously failed conservative treatment and less invasive therapy options (eg, injectable bulking agents, radiofrequency ablation, sacral nerve stimulation)."*
- That approval considered a data set in which all patients had completed follow-up evaluation at 36 months with partial follow-up evaluation at 48 and 60 months. **At 36 months, 57% of patients reported a 50% or greater reduction in FI episodes. Seven patients (20%) had the device explanted for infection, erosion, or lack of effect, and another patient required a stoma for relief of obstructed defecation. Including other complications (eg, pain and bleeding), 40% of patients had moderate or severe complications.**

### Defecatory disorders

- **DDs are defined by symptoms of chronic constipation or constipation-predominant irritable bowel syndrome associated with anorectal tests indicative of impaired rectal evacuation.**
- **Common in the community, with a prevalence of 22 (vs 5.8 for Crohn's disease) per 100,000 person-years.**
- In patients with chronic constipation unresponsive to laxatives, **anorectal testing is necessary to identify DDs**.
- **Dyssynergic defecation should be managed by biofeedback therapy, which is not widely available.**
- When patients do not respond to an adequate trial of pelvic floor retraining with biofeedback therapy, options include the following **four**:
  1. Ongoing medical management with an emphasis on **suppositories and enemas**.
  2. Evaluation for **pelvic floor structural abnormalities** (eg, clinically significant rectoceles or enteroceles) with appropriate surgical management for the same.
  3. Management of **colonic motor dysfunction** (eg, prokinetic agents).
  4. **Surgery or devices for defecatory disorders** — this category includes anal sphincter injection of **botulinum toxin**, **SNS**, and the **stapled transanal rectal resection (STARR)** procedure.

### Sacral nerve stimulation for defecatory disorders (the basis for Best Practice Advice 13)

- In addition to several retrospective reports, **3 prospective studies** evaluated SNS for chronic constipation.
- Multicenter European trial (2010): **45 of 62 patients** with constipation refractory to medical management proceeded from temporary to permanent SNS. Of the 62 patients, **81% had slow colon transit and 18% had normal transit with impaired evacuation.** Bowel symptoms (frequency, straining, and incomplete evacuation) and quality of life improved significantly after a median follow-up of **28 months (range, 1–55 mo)**. The effects of SNS on colonic transit and rectal evacuation were evaluated in only a subset of patients; some parameters improved.
- **By contrast**, in a prospective, 18-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled, 2-phase, cross-over study, **neither subsensory nor suprasensory SNS increased the proportion of complete bowel movements compared with sham SNS in 55 patients** with medically refractory slow-transit constipation and normal anorectal functions evaluated with manometry, rectal balloon expulsion, and proctography.
- Hence, although small studies have suggested that SNS may improve rectal sensation in patients with a DD and rectal hyposensitivity and induce colonic propagating sequences, **there is no evidence that SNS improves bowel symptoms or rectal evacuation in defecatory disorders.**

### Anterograde colonic enemas (the basis for Best Practice Advice 14)

- Malone described a surgically created appendicostomy for delivering anterograde colonic enemas in children with constipation or FI. **In adults, in whom the appendix is not always available or is stenosed, Malone anterograde continence enemas have been used in patients with medically refractory severe DDs.**
- Follow-up data in **17 of 20 patients: 13 were satisfied with the outcome, the outcome of 2 patients was unchanged, and 1 patient was worse.**
- Enemas can also be delivered via a **button cecostomy device** created by a colonoscopy and percutaneous technique.
- In these small series follow-up evaluation was short, and **success rates were lower in adults (approximately 50%) than in children (80%)**.
- **Long-term complications** such as stoma stenosis or leakage, or **failure to effectively treat symptoms commonly (>50% at 3 years), require revision, reversal, or conversion to a formal stoma.**
- Moreover, the procedure **does not address the primary dysfunction (ie, pelvic floor dysfunction)** — hence, in the authors' opinion, **this is not an effective long-term solution for adults with DD.**

### Stapled transanal rectal resection and ventral rectopexy (the basis for Best Practice Advice 15)

- Some patients with DDs have **rectoceles and/or rectal intussusception (occult rectal prolapse)**. Because these structural abnormalities may result at least partly from excessive straining and/or pelvic floor dysfunction, **they are managed primarily with pelvic floor biofeedback therapy.**
- Surgical options — transanal (STARR and Contour transtar) or transabdominal (ie, ventral rectopexy) — are considered for **clinically significant rectoceles** (eg, large defects, those that fill preferentially, and/or fail to empty on a defecating proctogram) and **symptomatic rectoceles** (eg, when patients recourse to vaginal stenting during defecation).
- Prospective multicenter trial randomized 119 patients with rectal intussusception or a rectocele, size not specified, to STARR or biofeedback therapy. **At 1 year, 44 of 54 (82%) STARR patients vs 13 of 39 (33%) biofeedback patients reported a >50% reduction in obstructed defecation scores; 25% of biofeedback patients (13 of 52) withdrew before the end of treatment.** Constipation-related quality of life also improved significantly in both groups.
- **However, 8 patients (15%) of STARR patients had adverse events** (infection, pain, incontinence, bleeding, urinary tract infection, or depression), which occasionally were severe and required further surgery, whereas **only 1 biofeedback patient experienced anal pain**. Other complications after the STARR procedure included **fistula, peritonitis, and bowel perforation**.
- **The correlation between symptoms and rectocele size is weak.** The correlation between improvement in symptoms and anatomy after the STARR procedure is **also weak**: symptoms may improve despite modest effects on anatomic disturbances and vice versa.
- **It is quite probable that anatomic abnormalities, such as intussusception and complete rectal prolapse, actually are caused by the underlying disorder of function (impaired pelvic floor relaxation and excessive straining), which is not corrected by the procedure.** Long-term outcomes of patients even ideally suited for STARR are somewhat disappointing; the surgery has **failed to gain widespread acceptance in the United States**.

### Surgery for rectal prolapse

- **Asymptomatic grades 1 to 2 rectal prolapse do not require surgery** and should be managed with conservative and/or biofeedback therapy to correct underlying dyssynergia.
- **Patients with symptomatic grades 3 to 4 prolapse require surgery**, using either an abdominal approach (ie, resection, rectopexy, or both) or perineal resection.
- Cochrane review, 12 randomized trials, 380 patients, verbatim conclusion: *"there is insufficient data to say which of the abdominal and perineal approaches has a better outcome. Division, rather than preservation, of the lateral ligaments was associated with less recurrent prolapse but more postoperative constipation. Laparoscopic rectopexy was associated with fewer postoperative complications and shorter hospital stay than open rectopexy. Bowel resection during rectopexy was associated with lower rates of constipation."*
- **However, bowel resection should be avoided in patients with pre-existing diarrhea and/or incontinence because these symptoms may worsen with resection.**
- In practice, the **perineal approach is used more frequently in clinical practice, has lower perioperative morbidity, and a higher recurrence rate.** In general, **elderly patients, those with significant medical comorbidities, or those with contraindications for abdominal surgery are often the best candidates for a perineal procedure, generally a perineal proctosigmoidectomy (Altemeier procedure)**, which may be combined with transperineal levatoroplasty to reduce the risk of recurrence.
- **Recurrence rates for transabdominal rectopexy are low (0%–8%); however, after posterior rectopexy, 50% of patients complain of severe constipation. Perineal procedures have a recurrence rate of 5% to 21%, with a similar incidence of constipation.**
- **Pouch of Douglas protrusion**, often confused with rectal intussusception and full-thickness rectal prolapse, is best addressed with **sacrocolpopexy** and usually is performed in conjunction with other gynecologic procedures in patients with pelvic floor abnormalities such as cystoceles, rectoceles, and enteroceles, and vaginal vault prolapse.

### Conclusions as stated by the authors

- Among surgical procedures, **SNS is a safe and effective option for FI**. There is **less evidence to support the routine use of other surgical procedures sans a colostomy**.
- For some emerging surgical options (eg, magnetic anal sphincter), **limited evidence suggests modest efficacy and the potential for severe side effects**.
- **In the authors' experience, surgery is necessary in a very small fraction, perhaps less than 5% of patients with DD**, generally patients with considerable pelvic organ and/or rectal prolapse.
- **There is a critical need for clinical trials comparing various surgical procedures and conservative therapies.**

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Dynamic graciloplasty — directly opposed to [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]].** This update's Best Practice Advice 8 says the artificial anal sphincter, dynamic graciloplasty, **may be considered** for medically refractory severe FI after other options fail. ACG 2021 states its morbidity and mortality are unacceptable and it is not used. **ACG 2021 holds the entity pages** — same tier, newer publication date — with AGA 2017's position recorded alongside it. The two are partly reconcilable: this document itself notes that very few reports and no clinical trials have been published in recent years, "suggesting that treatment is not performed routinely."
- **Barrier devices — strength of the recommendation differs.** Best Practice Advice 6 says barrier devices **should be offered** to patients who have failed conservative or surgical therapy; ACG 2021 rates anal plugs and vaginal balloons **conditional/very low**, difficult to tolerate, with an adjunct role. Both cite the same poor tolerability data (12.5%–68% drop-out). **ACG 2021 holds** as the newer source; the AGA framing is the stronger one and is noted on the page.
- **Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation.** Best Practice Advice 5 advises against PTNS in clinical practice until further evidence is available. ACG 2021 does not address PTNS, so this is net-new rather than conflicting.
- **Radiofrequency (Secca).** ACG 2021 says limited long-term data, not routinely recommended. This document issues **no Best Practice Advice on Secca at all** despite describing it — the absence is not an endorsement and should not be read as one.
- **Sphincteroplasty sequencing.** Best Practice Advice 7 places sphincteroplasty **after** perianal bulking and SNS for late-presenting patients ("when perianal bulking injection and sacral nerve stimulation are not available or have proven unsuccessful"), and the text predicts minimally invasive modalities will become the preferred first-line surgical approach except after recent or acute injury. The wiki's existing sequencing comes from ACG 2021; this ordering is compatible but more explicit.
- **Ungraded throughout, and explicitly experience-based in places.** Several of the operative claims — the ~25% conservative-therapy benefit, the <5% of DD patients needing surgery, the judgment that anterograde enemas are not an effective long-term solution — are introduced as the authors' experience or opinion, not as trial results. Weigh accordingly.
- **No time window on the "3-month or longer trial."** Best Practice Advice 4 sets a ≥3-month conservative and biofeedback trial before SNS; the document does not say whether the 3 months is of conservative therapy, of biofeedback, or of both combined.
