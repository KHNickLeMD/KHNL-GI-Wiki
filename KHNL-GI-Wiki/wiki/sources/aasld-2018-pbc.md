## Summary

This AASLD 2018 Practice Guidance updates the 2009 [[primary-biliary-cholangitis|PBC]] guidelines. It covers the etiology, natural history, diagnosis, clinical manifestations, and full therapeutic management of PBC. The major addition compared with 2009 is the formal inclusion of obeticholic acid (OCA), approved by the FDA in May 2016, as second-line therapy.

PBC is a chronic autoimmune cholestatic liver disease defined by its hallmark serologic marker — anti-mitochondrial antibody (AMA) — and progressive nonsuppurative destruction of interlobular bile ducts. The guidance confirms UDCA 13–15 mg/kg/day as the sole first-line therapy, establishes biochemical response criteria (multiple validated systems tabulated), introduces OCA 5 mg/day titrated to 10 mg/day as second-line, acknowledges fibrates as off-label alternatives, and provides comprehensive guidance on symptom management (fatigue, pruritus, sicca syndrome), bone disease, hyperlipidemia, [[portal-hypertension|portal hypertension]], [[hcc-surveillance|HCC surveillance]], [[liver-transplantation|liver transplantation]], recurrent PBC post-transplant, PBC-[[autoimmune-hepatitis|AIH]] overlap (Paris criteria), AMA-negative PBC, and screening of first-degree relatives.

The guidance emphasizes that UDCA does not improve fatigue or pruritus; those require separate, symptom-directed therapy. OCA is contraindicated in decompensated PBC (Child-Pugh B or C) per a September 2017 FDA warning. Fibrates carry a risk of creatinine elevation (non-renal mechanism) and transaminase elevation, with long-term safety data still lacking at time of publication.

## Guidance Statements (Complete, Verbatim)

### Diagnosis

**Guidance Statement 1.** *The diagnosis of PBC can be established when two of the following three criteria are met:*

- *Biochemical evidence of cholestasis based on ALP elevation.*
- *Presence of AMA, or other PBC-specific autoantibodies, including sp100 or gp210, if AMA is negative.*
- *Histologic evidence of nonsuppurative destructive cholangitis and destruction of interlobular bile ducts.*

**Guidance Statement 2.** *The diagnosis of AMA-negative PBC does not require a liver biopsy if other criteria are met, including cholestatic liver tests and PBC-specific autoantibodies such as sp100 or gp210.*

**Guidance Statement 3.** *Liver biopsy to rule out concomitant AIH or other liver disease should be considered in PBC patients when the alanine aminotransferase activity is more than 5 times the upper limit of normal.*

**Guidance Statement 4.** *In cases of suspected PBC/AIH overlap, treatment should be targeted at the predominant histological pattern of injury.*

### Therapy — UDCA

**Guidance Statement 5.** *UDCA in a dose of 13 to 15 mg/kg/day orally is recommended for patients with PBC who have abnormal liver enzyme values regardless of histologic stage.*

**Guidance Statement 6.** *For patients requiring bile acid sequestrants, UDCA should be given at least 1 hour before or 4 hours after the bile acid sequestrant.*

**Guidance Statement 7.** *Biochemical response to UDCA should be evaluated at 12 months after treatment initiation to determine whether patients should be considered for second-line therapy.*

### Therapy — OCA and Fibrates

**Guidance Statement 8.** *Patients who are inadequate responders to UDCA (Table 1) should be considered for treatment with OCA, starting at 5 mg/day.*

**Guidance Statement 9 (original 2018 version — superseded by 2021 update).** *Fibrates can be considered as off-label alternatives for patients with PBC and inadequate response to UDCA.*

**Guidance Statement 10 (original 2018 version — superseded by 2021 update).** *Use of OCA and fibrates is discouraged in patients with decompensated liver disease (Child-Pugh-Turcotte B or C).*

> Note: Guidance Statements 9 and 10 were revised in the AASLD 2021 PBC Practice Guidance Update — see [[aasld-2021-pbc]].

### Symptom Management — Pruritus

**Guidance Statement 11.** *Anion-exchange resins should be used as initial therapy for patients with PBC who have pruritus.*

**Guidance Statement 12.** *The following agents can be used for pruritus refractory to anion-exchange resins:*

- *a. Rifampicin 150 to 300 mg twice daily.*
- *b. Oral opiate antagonists such as naltrexone titrated to a dose of 50 mg daily.*
- *c. Sertraline 75 to 100 mg daily.*

### Symptom Management — Sicca Syndrome

**Guidance Statement 13.** *Management of dry eyes can include the following:*

- *a. Artificial tears should be used initially.*
- *b. Pilocarpine or cevimeline can be used in patients for whom symptoms are refractory to artificial tears.*
- *c. Cyclosporine or lifitegrast ophthalmic emulsion can be used in those whose disease is refractory to other agents, preferably under the supervision of an ophthalmologist.*

**Guidance Statement 14.** *The following therapies should be used for xerostomia and dysphagia:*

- *a. Over-the-counter saliva substitutes can be tried.*
- *b. Pilocarpine or cevimeline can be used if patients remain symptomatic despite saliva substitutes.*

### Portal Hypertension and HCC

**Guidance Statement 15.** *Patients with suspected cirrhosis should undergo endoscopic screening for varices at the time of diagnosis.*

**Guidance Statement 16.** *Regular screening for [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|hepatocellular carcinoma]] with cross-sectional imaging at 6-month intervals is currently advised for men and patients with cirrhosis.*

### Bone Disease

**Guidance Statement 17.** *Patients with PBC should be provided 1,000 to 1,500 mg of calcium and 1,000 International Units of vitamin D daily in the diet and as supplements if needed.*

**Guidance Statement 18.** *Oral alendronate (70 mg weekly) or other effective bisphosphonates should be considered if patients are osteoporotic. Oral bisphosphonates should be avoided if patients have acid reflux or known varices.*

### Hyperlipidemia

**Guidance Statement 19.** *Patients with elevated lipid levels and at risk for cardiovascular disease can be considered for lipid-lowering therapy.*

### Fat-Soluble Vitamins

**Guidance Statement 20.** *Fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies should be treated with parenteral or water-soluble supplements.*

### Liver Transplantation

**Guidance Statement 21.** *Patients with manifestations of end-stage PBC should be referred for liver transplantation when their Model for End-Stage Liver Disease score exceeds 14.*

## Table 1 — Biochemical Response Criteria for UDCA (Reproduced)

| Criteria (by year) | Response definition |
|---|---|
| Rochester I | ALP ≤2× ULN |
| Barcelona | Reduction in ALP ≥40% from baseline or normalization of ALP |
| Paris I | ALP ≤3× ULN; AST ≤2× ULN; and total bilirubin ≤1 mg/dL |
| Rotterdam | Total bilirubin <1× ULN and albumin >1× LLN |
| Toronto | ALP ≤1.67× ULN |
| Paris II | ALP ≤1.5× ULN; AST ≤1.5× ULN; and total bilirubin ≤1 mg/dL |
| Rochester II | ALP ≤2× ULN |
| Global (GLOBE score) | ALP ≤2× ULN |

*ALP, alkaline phosphatase; AST, aspartate aminotransferase; TB, total bilirubin; ULN, upper limit of normal; LLN, lower limit of normal.*

## Key Findings / Claims

- **Epidemiology:** Estimates vary between 19 and 402 cases of PBC per million. One recent paper: **overall prevalence 290 per million**, **430 per million in women**, **110 per million in men** — as opposed to other studies suggesting a 9:1 female:male ratio. AMA positivity in first-degree relatives (FDRs) 13.1% vs. 1% in controls; female FDRs — sisters 20.7%, mothers 15.1%, daughters 9.8% — exceed male FDRs (brothers 7.8%, fathers 3.7%, sons 0%).
- **AMA:** Present in 95% of PBC patients. Absence or presence is more important than titer level. Indirect immunofluorescence has lowest sensitivity (>15% false-negative); ELISA, immunoblotting, Luminex, enzyme inhibition assay are superior methods.
- **AMA-negative PBC:** sp100 and gp210 present in **>30%** of patients AMA-negative by indirect immunofluorescence; anti-kelch-like 12 and anti-hexokinase 1 found in **35%** and **22%** respectively (not yet widely available). Biopsy is required **only in the absence of these PBC-specific autoantibodies** — i.e. only true seronegative PBC needs histology (ideally a florid duct lesion and/or granulomas). Do **not** use "autoimmune cholangiopathy/cholangitis" as a synonym. Differences vs. AMA-positive: higher prevalence of ANA and anti-smooth muscle antibody, lower serum IgM, more nonhepatic autoimmune conditions, worse social/emotional HRQoL, and greater bile duct damage/loss histologically — but **treatment response to UDCA appears similar**, and clinical-outcome differences are unresolved.
- **Histology:** Lesions classically divided into **4 stages** — Stage I = portal inflammation ± florid bile duct lesion (inflammation confined to portal triads); Stage II = interface hepatitis (lymphocytic *or* biliary piecemeal necrosis; severity of interface hepatitis is highly predictive of extensive fibrosis); Stage III = distortion of hepatic architecture with numerous fibrous septa (bridging); Stage IV = cirrhosis with regenerative nodules. At least 10–15 portal tracts required for adequate assessment (bile ducts absent in <50% of portal tracts in PBC, and the lesion is patchy). Nakanuma et al. proposed an alternative 4-stage model scoring bile duct loss, fibrosis, and cholestasis.
- **Prognostic models:** **GLOBE score** — derived from 2,488 UDCA-treated patients, validated in 1,631; five variables: serum bilirubin, albumin, ALP, and platelet count **after 1 year of treatment**, plus **age at start of therapy**; score >0.30 = shorter transplant-free survival than age/sex-matched healthy population. **UK-PBC score** — cohort of 3,165; serum ALP, aminotransferases, and bilirubin **after 12 months of therapy** plus albumin and platelets **at baseline**; predicts risk of LT or liver-related death within 5, 10, or 15 years. Both superior to prior models; validation in other ethnic groups still needed.
- **Bilirubin is the best single predictor of survival** and the most important component of every PBC prognostic model. Serum ALP <2× ULN on treatment is a reliable predictor of treatment response.
- **Transient elastography as risk stratifier:** liver stiffness **>9.6 kPa** → 5× more likely to progress to clinical decompensation, death, or transplant. Liver biopsy is *not* indicated to monitor response to therapy.
- **UDCA efficacy:** 13–15 mg/kg/day superior to 5–7 and 23–25 mg/kg/day. UDCA reduces LDL cholesterol, reduces risk of esophageal varices, delays histologic progression. Does NOT improve fatigue, pruritus, bone disease, or autoimmune features.
- **Biochemical response:** Assessed at 12 months; up to 40% will have inadequate response by one of the validated criteria (Table 1).
- **OCA:** FXR agonist, **100× more potent** than the endogenous ligand chenodeoxycholic acid. FDA-approved **May 2016** in combination with UDCA for inadequate response to **≥1 year** of UDCA, or as monotherapy in UDCA-intolerant patients.
  - Phase 3 (n=210, 1 year, placebo vs. 10 mg/day vs. titration arm starting 5 mg/day → 10 mg/day at 6 months): primary endpoint = **ALP <1.67× ULN + >15% reduction from baseline + normal bilirubin**; met by **46%** (titration), **47%** (10 mg/day), **10%** (placebo).
  - Phase 2 (n=165, 3 months, 10/25/50 mg vs. placebo): ALP fell 21–25% on OCA vs. 3% placebo. Second phase 2 monotherapy study (n=59): ALP dropped 53.9% (10 mg), 37.2% (50 mg), 0.8% (placebo); pruritus caused discontinuation in 38% of the 50-mg arm.
  - **Dosing:** start **5 mg daily** in preserved synthetic function / well-compensated PBC; after **3 months** may increase to **10 mg daily** if liver chemistries remain abnormal and the drug is tolerated.
  - **FDA warning September 2017** — inappropriate dosing in moderate-to-severe liver impairment (**Child-Pugh-Turcotte B and C**) was associated with worsening PBC and death; OCA is not recommended in decompensated PBC.
- **Fibrates:** PPAR agonists (isoforms α, δ, γ); FDA-approved only as lipid-lowering drugs, so use in PBC is **off-label**.
  - Open-label, n=20 with ALP >2× ULN on UDCA: **fenofibrate 160 mg/day × 48 weeks** → ALP fell ~50%.
  - n=48 incomplete responders: **bezafibrate 400 mg/day**, median 38 months → 54% normalized ALP within the first 4 months; older patients and those with lower fibrosis scores more likely to respond; most with baseline pruritus improved.
  - **BEZURSO** (n=100 inadequate responders, 2 years, UDCA/bezafibrate vs. UDCA/placebo): **67% normalized ALP** and **30% normalized all liver tests** vs. **0%** on placebo.
  - **Adverse effects:** myalgias, heartburn; **serum creatinine elevation** — typically reversible, **not** a decline in GFR, attributed to **increased creatinine production**; **bilirubin increase** — attributed to competitive inhibition of the organic-anion-transporting polypeptide (which transports both bile acids and bilirubin); transaminase elevation (also typically reversible). **Not studied in decompensated liver disease and should be avoided there**; long-term safety warrants further study.
- **Fatigue:** Most common symptom, found in 50–78% of patients. Not improved by UDCA or OCA. Ondansetron and fluoxetine both failed in trials; modafinil RCT negative. Consider non-PBC causes (hypothyroidism, depression, anemia, sleep apnea). Chronic fatigue is **not** an indication for liver transplantation — the symptom is not universally reversible after LT (moderate-to-severe fatigue still affects nearly half of patients 2 years post-LT).
- **Pruritus:** Stepwise: anion-exchange resins (cholestyramine **4 g per dose, max 16 g/day**; give UDCA at least 1 hour before or 4 hours after) → rifampicin **150–300 mg twice daily** → oral opiate antagonists (naltrexone start **12.5 mg daily**, increase by 12.5 mg every 3–7 days, titrate to **50 mg daily**) → sertraline **75–100 mg daily**. Colesevelam was **not** effective in a controlled trial. Severe intractable pruritus is an **exceptional indication** for liver transplantation.
- **Sicca syndrome:** Sjögren's syndrome co-exists significantly more often in PBC. Dry eyes: artificial tears → pilocarpine/cevimeline → cyclosporine/lifitegrast ophthalmic. Dry mouth: saliva substitutes → pilocarpine/cevimeline.
- **Bone disease:** Significantly increased risk of osteopenia/osteoporosis. BMD every 2 years. Calcium 1,000–1,500 mg/day + Vitamin D 1,000 IU/day. Alendronate 70 mg/week effective; ibandronate monthly comparable. Oral bisphosphonates contraindicated with acid reflux or known varices.
- **Hyperlipidemia:** No increased cardiovascular risk in PBC despite hypercholesterolemia in most retrospective studies, but one meta-analysis found pooled risk ratio 1.57 (95% CI 1.21–2.06). Statins safe even with abnormal LFTs. Fibrates have also been used safely.
- **Portal hypertension:** PBC can cause portal hypertension from biliary cirrhosis **or, pre-cirrhotic, from nodular regenerative hyperplasia**. Management follows the 2016 AASLD portal hypertension guidance — screening EGD when cirrhosis is first suspected; nonselective beta blockers or endoscopic variceal ligation for **large** esophageal varices, choice guided by local expertise, resources, and patient preference.
  - Thresholds prompting endoscopic surveillance: platelet count **<200,000/mm³** (one study) or **140,000/mm³** (another); transient elastography **≥17 kPa** could also be considered, though not yet studied. **6%** of PBC patients without cirrhosis had varices; varices are virtually never found unless the **Mayo risk score is ≥4.1**.
  - For variceal bleeding refractory to pharmacologic + endoscopic therapy, **liver transplantation is not desirable when synthetic function is good — TIPS is the therapeutic alternative**; distal splenorenal shunts are rarely used but have not been associated with accelerated liver failure in PBC.
- **HCC surveillance:** Men and patients with advanced disease are most apt to develop HCC; incidence **3.9 cases per 1,000 per year of follow-up**. Suboptimal UDCA response is an important risk factor, though UDCA does not change overall risk. Cross-sectional imaging every **6 months** for men and patients with cirrhosis. In patients without a liver biopsy, consider surveillance if **low platelet count**, **Mayo risk score >4.1**, or **transient elastography ≥17 kPa**.
- **Liver transplantation — referral triggers:** decompensated cirrhosis, **MELD ≥15**, **total bilirubin >6 mg/dL**, or **Mayo risk score >7.8**; severe intractable pruritus is an exceptional indication. (Guidance Statement 21 phrases the threshold as MELD **>14**.) Outcomes are more favorable than for nearly all other disease categories: 1/3/5/10-year **graft** survival 85%, 80%, 78.1%, 71.9%; **patient** survival 90.2%, 86.7%, 84.4%, 79%.
- **Recurrent PBC post-transplant:** 20–30% by 10 years, up to 50% by 20 years; median time to recurrence 3–6 years. Recurrence infrequently affects long-term patient or graft survival. Preventive UDCA post-transplant associated with lower recurrence (**21% vs. 62%, P=0.004**). Long-term cyclosporine-based immunosuppression *seems* associated with reduced recurrence but this is **not proven**. AMA may persist or reappear post-LT without signaling recurrence.
- **PBC-AIH overlap (Paris criteria):** Requires 2 of 3 AIH criteria: (a) ALT >5× ULN; (b) IgG ≥2× ULN or positive anti-smooth muscle antibody; (c) liver biopsy with moderate or severe interface hepatitis. Present in 1–14.2% of PBC patients. No randomized controlled data on optimal therapy.
- **Hormone replacement and pregnancy:** Estrogens promote cholestasis, so oral contraceptives and estrogen supplements may induce or worsen pruritus; itching in pregnancy may become severe early and may not fully resolve after delivery. Limited data on fertility or infant outcome in PBC. As with any woman with cirrhosis, **check for varices in the second trimester** (after maternal blood volume rises markedly). **Beta blockers are safe in pregnancy.** A **short second stage of labor** is optimal, since Valsalva may precipitate variceal hemorrhage.
- **Family screening:** Screen female first-degree relatives (especially sisters and daughters) starting at age 30 with serum ALP; if elevated, test AMA; if AMA-negative, repeat at 5-year intervals.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Superseded by [[aasld-2021-pbc]] — Guidance Statements 9 and 10 were reissued verbatim as:**
  - *9. Fibrates can be considered as off-label alternatives for patients with PBC and inadequate response to ursodeoxycholic acid, although fibrates are discouraged in patients with decompensated liver disease.* (2018 lumped fibrates with OCA into a single "discouraged in Child-Pugh-Turcotte B or C" statement.)
  - *10. OCA is contraindicated in patients with advanced cirrhosis. This is defined as cirrhosis with current or prior evidence of liver decompensation (e.g., encephalopathy, coagulopathy) or portal hypertension (e.g., ascites, gastroesophageal varices, or persistent thrombocytopenia). Furthermore, we would recommend careful monitoring of any patient with cirrhosis, even if not advanced, receiving OCA.* (Follows a **May 2021** FDA warning; the 2018 page reflects only the **September 2017** warning and defines the contraindication as Child-Pugh-Turcotte B/C rather than by decompensation/portal-hypertension criteria.)
- Fibrate long-term safety in PBC was not established at time of publication; fibrates have not been studied in decompensated liver disease and should be avoided there.
- Fatigue: ondansetron, fluoxetine, and modafinil all failed in trials — the guidance identifies **no** effective pharmacotherapy for PBC fatigue.
- Bezafibrate is not FDA-approved for PBC (fibrates are approved only as lipid-lowering agents), and bezafibrate availability varies by country; BEZURSO is a European trial.
- OCA survival benefit is unproven — efficacy studies were still ongoing; the projected reduction in 15-year decompensation/HCC/LT/liver-related death comes from **microsimulation modeling**, not trial outcomes.
- **Table 1 caveat:** the ≤ / ≥ operators in the biochemical-response criteria are rendered inconsistently by text extraction from the source PDF; the response thresholds themselves (2×, 3×, 1.67×, 1.5× ULN etc.) are verbatim from Table 1.
