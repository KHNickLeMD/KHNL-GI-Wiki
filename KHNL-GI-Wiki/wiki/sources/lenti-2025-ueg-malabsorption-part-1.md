## Summary
- First European consensus on malabsorption as a syndrome rather than single diseases; it explicitly does **not** cover the diagnosis and management of individual causative diseases (p. 2).
- **13 statements**, numbered by the document's section headings **3.1.1–4.2.6**, in two chapters: **3 — definitions, clinical phenotypes, diagnostic criteria** (6 statements) and **4 — initial assessment and testing** (7 statements). Each is followed by explanatory text. None is graded.
- **Key positions:** maldigestion vs malabsorption is of limited clinical value — "malabsorption" alone is acceptable; no universal diagnostic test — diagnosis rests on documented micro- or macronutrient deficiency not explained by intake or needs and on evidence of a condition causing malabsorption, as worded in 3.3.2; **celiac serology in every unexplained malabsorption**, then esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) with duodenal biopsies, selectively capsule endoscopy and ileocolonoscopy with biopsies; **D-xylose testing of limited use**; **breath testing not supported for diagnosing small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)**; **¹³C-mixed triglyceride breath test or steatocrit for fat malabsorption, with suspected steatorrhea, fecal elastase or ¹³C-mixed triglyceride breath test for exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI)**; **cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene study and sweat chloride testing in young patients with EPI and suspected cystic fibrosis**.
- Tables: conditions causing enteropathy (Table 1), etiologies by mechanism (Table 2), micronutrient deficiency manifestations (Table 3).

**How items are labelled here.** Statements carry the document's own section numbers (3.1.1–4.2.6). The document gives no grade, strength or per-statement agreement; every final statement reached >80% agreement (section 2.3). Page numbers are PDF pages (journal pages 599–613).

### 3 — Definitions, clinical phenotypes, and diagnostic criteria

**3.1 What are the definitions of maldigestion and malabsorption?**

| # | Statement (p. 4) |
|---|---|
| 3.1.1 | **Maldigestion** = defective hydrolysis of large-molecular nutrients into absorbable small molecular components. **Malabsorption** = impaired ability to absorb nutrients through the intestinal mucosa into the blood stream or lymphatic vessels. In the clinical context, **the sole use of the term "malabsorption" is acceptable** |
| 3.1.2 | The **malabsorption syndrome** (the clinical symptoms and signs caused by malabsorption) is determined by the type and amount of the malabsorbed substrates, the deficiency of the malabsorbed nutrients, the underlying disease, and the consequences of accumulation of the malabsorbed substrate in the gastrointestinal lumen |

- **Malassimilation** = maldigestion + malabsorption combined; presentation and complications are similar, so the distinction is of limited clinical value (p. 4).
- **Mechanisms:** premucosal (flawed intraluminal digestion) · mucosal (maldigestion or malabsorption) · mucosal intracellular/postcellular, vascular and lymphatic transport (malabsorption) (p. 4).
- **Colon (pp. 4–5):** colonic bacteria ferment carbohydrates to short-chain fatty acids → calorie salvage; but colonic salvage also causes **kidney stones from colonic hyperabsorption of oxalate** (hyperoxaluria) and gas symptoms. Healthy colon absorbs sodium, chloride, water, short-chain fatty acids, calcium, magnesium, water-soluble vitamins (biotin, folate, B5, B6, B2, B1) and vitamin K.
- **Symptom types (p. 5):** from nutrient deficiency — anemia, weight loss, edema, bleeding, osteomalacia, sexual dysfunction; from excess substrate in the gut — steatorrhea, diarrhea, bloating, abdominal pain, kidney stones.

**3.2 Which clinical phenotypes underlie malabsorption?**

| # | Statement (p. 5) |
|---|---|
| 3.2.1 | Malabsorption may be **congenital** (e.g., mucosal carrier protein defects), **secondary to surgical procedures**, or caused by many diseases or conditions originating from, or affecting, the **small intestine, pancreas, liver, biliary tract and stomach**. Mechanisms: premucosal, mucosal, and mucosal intracellular/postcellular and vascular transport abnormalities |
| 3.2.2 | Malabsorptive disorders have a **wide clinical spectrum** — subtle presentations, clinically obvious malabsorption symptoms, or presentations dominated by **extra-intestinal manifestations** |

- Congenital carrier-protein defects can cause dermatologic, neurologic and psychiatric manifestations and intellectual disability; in practice mechanisms are often mixed and several organs affected (p. 5).
- Absorption also needs solubilization (micelles for fat and fat-soluble vitamins; acidification for calcium), liberation from food binding or binding to intrinsic factor (vitamin B12), chemical change (iron Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺), intact sensory and motor function, and neural and hormonal control (p. 5).
- Malabsorption may become clinically evident only after an extended duration (p. 5).
- **Typical symptoms:** weight loss, malnutrition or failure to thrive, dehydration, steatorrhea, chronic diarrhea. **Also consider malabsorption with:** hypoproteinemia, anemia, electrolyte imbalance, bloating, change in bowel habit, abdominal pain (p. 5).
- Chronic diarrhea from malabsorption can mimic functional diarrhea and irritable bowel syndrome; the UEG guideline on functional bowel disorders with diarrhea (cited) favors a symptom-based approach over diagnosis by exclusion, but suggests full blood count, C-reactive protein, celiac serology and fecal calprotectin in all cases, plus further testing in selected cases when organic disease is suspected (p. 5).
- **Single-nutrient presentations:** hypocalcemia → osteomalacia and osteoporosis; iron or vitamin B12 deficiency → blood count changes (isolated anisocytosis or mean cell volume change, anemia, thrombocytopenia or thrombocytosis, leukopenia). Extra-abdominal: menstrual disturbance, growth delay in children, neurologic change, miscarriage, infertility (p. 5).

**3.3 What are the most appropriate diagnostic criteria for malabsorption?**

| # | Statement (p. 6) |
|---|---|
| 3.3.1 | Clinical history, physical examination and routine laboratory tests may help raise the suspicion of malabsorption, select specific tests to document deficiencies and their sequelae, and detect the underlying disease. **Consider malabsorption in patients with known gastrointestinal, pancreatic, liver and biliary diseases** |
| 3.3.2 | **There is no universal diagnostic test for malabsorption.** The diagnosis "can be made in case of" **clinical and laboratory evidence of micro- or macronutrient deficiency** in a patient with **adequate eating habits and no increased nutritional needs** explaining it, "and in case of" evidence of a **medical or surgical condition leading to malabsorption** (the statement does not say whether both are required) |

- Explanatory text widens 3.3.1 to "all patients with symptoms of gastrointestinal, pancreatic and biliary diseases" (p. 6).
- The list of first-line examinations for initial assessment is in the algorithm of Part 2; Part 1 does not print it (p. 6).
- Finding the cause often needs endoscopy with small-intestinal biopsies, and in some settings non-invasive tests or imaging (abdominal ultrasound, computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance (MR) for inflammatory bowel disease or chronic pancreatitis) (p. 6).
- Approach varies with travel history and epidemiologic or ethnic background; **previous surgery** → suspect reduced digestive or absorptive capacity or complications such as SIBO (p. 6).

### 4 — Initial assessment, testing for malabsorption and its causes

**4.1 How should physicians establish a diagnosis of malabsorption?**

| # | Statement (p. 6) |
|---|---|
| 4.1.1 | **Order tests according to clinical data, medical and surgical history, and family history.** Recognizing the cause often requires several examinations — **endoscopy with small intestinal biopsies, non-invasive tests of gastrointestinal function, or radiological imaging** |

- Systematic approach to the long differential (Table 2): thorough history (family, medical, surgical, drug use), examination, laboratory results (p. 6).
- Macronutrient malabsorption → protein-energy malnutrition; micronutrient deficiency → specific presentations (Table 3); isolated carbohydrate malabsorption → bloating, flatulence, abdominal pain, nausea or diarrhea (p. 6).
- **Next step usually the digestive tract:** stool assessment (steatorrhea, fecal elastase, ova and parasites), breath tests, duodenal biopsies, CT or MR enterography. Follow existing guidelines for breath tests, intestinal failure and seronegative villous atrophy (p. 6).

**4.2 Which tests need to be performed for identifying malabsorption and its causes?**

| # | Statement | Page |
|---|---|---|
| 4.2.1 | Malabsorption of some ingested nutrients or substrates can be confirmed by measuring their **increased stool concentration**, or their **decreased serum or urine concentration or urinary excretion** | 6 |
| 4.2.2 | **Clinical suspicion of malabsorption with no apparent cause → celiac disease serology.** **EGD with duodenal biopsies**, and in selected cases **small bowel capsule endoscopy** and **ileocolonoscopy with biopsies**, should be considered to detect mucosal diseases explaining the malabsorption | 7–8 |
| 4.2.3 | **D-xylose testing** has been used in the past to assess small bowel mucosal absorptive function, but its **clinical usefulness is limited** | 9 |
| 4.2.4 | The currently available evidence **does not support the use of breath testing for diagnosing SIBO** | 9 |
| 4.2.5 | **"We suggest"** the **¹³C-mixed triglyceride breath test or steatocrit** to measure **fat malabsorption** when available. With **suspected steatorrhea**, **fecal elastase or the ¹³C-mixed triglyceride breath test may be used to detect EPI** | 10 |
| 4.2.6 | **CFTR gene study and chloride sweat testing** should be conducted in **young patients with EPI and suspicion of cystic fibrosis** | 10 |

#### Stool, urine and serum markers (explanatory text to 4.2.1, pp. 6–7)

| Test | What it shows | Points as printed |
|---|---|---|
| Quantitative fecal fat | Steatorrhea (fat malabsorption) | Grams of fat excreted after a standardized fat intake, **typically 100 g**; needs **72-h stool collection**; does not distinguish pancreatic, enteric or biliary causes; clinical value now limited, mostly used when other tests are inconclusive (p. 6). Physiologically **no more than 7%** of fat ingested on a **high-fat diet (100 g/d)** is excreted; quantitative fecal fat is the **reference method** for fat malabsorption (p. 10) |
| Fecal steatocrit | Semiquantitative fecal fat | Fat excretion in a random spot stool; suggested alternative (p. 7) |
| Fecal pancreatic elastase-1 | EPI | Specific marker for EPI; details under 4.2.5 (p. 7) |
| Fecal chymotrypsin | Advanced chronic pancreatitis | Not widely available; replaced by pancreatic elastase-1 (p. 7) |
| Fecal alpha-1 antitrypsin | Protein-losing enteropathy | Resists digestive degradation; increased stool concentration consistent with protein-losing enteropathy (p. 7) |
| Bile acid malabsorption tests | Bile acid malabsorption | Fecal bile acid concentration and ¹⁴C-cholylglycine test rarely used now, given their limitations and alternatives such as **23-seleno-25-homotaurocholic acid (SeHCAT) testing, which has high sensitivity and specificity**; serum **7α-hydroxy-4-cholesten-3-one (C4)** and **fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF-19)** proposed as promising alternatives (p. 7). No cut-offs given |
| Urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) | Neuroendocrine tumors | May sometimes be elevated in intestinal malabsorption such as celiac disease (p. 7) |
| Fecal calprotectin | Intestinal inflammation | Raises suspicion of intestinal inflammation (p. 7) |

- Older tests (triglyceride test, oxalate loading test, ¹⁴C-triolein breath test, Schilling test) and enterocyte damage markers (intestinal fatty acid binding protein, diamine oxidase, citrulline) never entered practice or were abandoned for disease-specific tests such as celiac serology (p. 2).

#### Celiac disease and mucosal disease (explanatory text to 4.2.2, pp. 8–9)
- Malabsorption dominated by **diarrhea, steatorrhea, weight loss or failure to thrive without an apparent cause → celiac disease must be suspected.**
- **Serology:** tissue transglutaminase 2 antibodies (TGA), the target antigen of anti-endomysium antibodies (EMA), allow reliable, inexpensive screening; immunoglobulin A (IgA)-EMA is part of the serological diagnosis. In adults, **IgA anti-deamidated gliadin peptide (anti-DGP) can substitute for IgA-TGA if that assay is unavailable**. **Immunoglobulin G (IgG)-based assays (TG2 or DGP) in selective IgA deficiency** → **measure total IgA concurrently** with serology.
- **Biopsy:** per available guidelines, **adult diagnosis must be confirmed by intestinal biopsy**; in children, ESPGHAN guidelines allow a **no-biopsy approach in selected patients with high TGA titres (>10 times the upper limit of normal (ULN))**.
  - Newer prospective studies and a systematic review support no-biopsy in **selected adults: TGA >10 times ULN and a moderate to high pre-test probability**; more evidence and future guidelines are awaited before wide use.
  - A serology-only approach in adults misses **potential, refractory and seronegative** celiac disease.
- **Endoscopy:** **one-third** of patients with celiac disease have a normal endoscopic appearance → biopsies needed; **duodenal scalloping and flattened Kerckring's folds must always prompt duodenal biopsies**, especially when celiac disease was not suspected. Duodenal changes classified by Marsh-Oberhuber or Corazza-Villanacci; the consensus does not list the criteria.
- Biopsy also separates other enteropathies (Table 1); disaccharidase assays on biopsies are mostly research-only; endoscopy with biopsy is indicated where both endoscopy and histology are needed (e.g., Crohn's disease).
- **Beyond the duodenum:** in **complicated celiac disease** (enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma, ulcerative jejuno-ileitis, refractory celiac disease) → double-balloon enteroscopy, and to some extent video capsule endoscopy (no biopsies). Suggested sequence: **capsule first** to select patients for double-balloon enteroscopy — good accuracy in this setting.

#### D-xylose test (explanatory text to 4.2.3, p. 9)
- Five-carbon monosaccharide, absorbed like glucose, mostly excreted unchanged by the kidney; accuracy data are few, dated, mostly in celiac disease.
- **Adult protocol (seminal review):** **25 g D-xylose in 250–600 mL water, fasting**; **5-h urine collection and 1-h serum sample**; separates normal subjects from proximal small-intestinal malabsorption with high specificity and sensitivity (**>95%**).
- **Children:** 1-h serum test after **5 g** D-xylose "highly sensitive (< 91%)" as printed, and specific (nearly 100%).
- **False positives:** liver disease with ascites, dehydration, gastroparesis, renal insufficiency, SIBO. To offset impaired renal function, especially in older adults, some prioritize the 1-h blood result corrected to a body surface area of 1.73 m² (xylose level × actual surface area / 1.73); others favor urine measurement.
- ¹⁴C- and ¹³C-xylose breath tests and hydrogen breath tests with D-xylose: varying sensitivity, one study only.
- **Bottom line:** **not currently indicated** in the setting of malabsorption (few small, possibly biased studies; scarce substrate). **May be useful in limited settings in tertiary referral centers** — follow-up of intestinal failure after small bowel transplant or during glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) analogue treatment.

#### Breath testing and SIBO (explanatory text to 4.2.4, p. 9)
- Breath tests measure exhaled hydrogen (H₂) and methane (CH₄), plus symptoms, at intervals after a carbohydrate load; carbohydrate malabsorption testing is covered in Part 2.
- The most recent guidelines and an expert consensus **discourage breath tests for diagnosing SIBO**: results do not correlate with symptoms or small bowel aspirates and **do not predict response to antibiotics**.

| Breath test for SIBO (latest meta-analysis, p. 9) | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|
| Lactulose breath test (pooled) | 42% | 70.6% |
| Glucose breath test, **only in patients with a predisposing condition** (i.e., history of abdominal surgery) | 81.7% | 78.8% |

- Glucose test accuracy higher but "still suboptimal" → **high false-positive rate → risk of unnecessary antibiotic treatment**. Adding an oro-cecal transit time test has been suggested, but such tests are highly variable, perform poorly, or have been abandoned (e.g., scintigraphy).
- **Consider SIBO only with predisposing conditions:** anatomical abnormalities or post-surgical changes (e.g., gastric bypass or Roux-en-Y), medications or conditions slowing gut motility, small bowel dysmotility, and diseases such as hepatic encephalopathy, gastroparesis, Parkinson's disease, chronic pancreatitis and end-stage renal disease.
- SIBO may be asymptomatic or cause bloating, discomfort, flatulence, diarrhea, fatigue and poor concentration, up to malabsorption, nutrient deficiency and weight loss.
- **No consensus on a universal SIBO test.** Jejunal aspirate culture was the gold standard but is invasive, difficult and may lack sensitivity; the concepts of SIBO and small bowel dysbiosis are likely to change.

#### Fat malabsorption and EPI (explanatory text to 4.2.5, p. 10)
- Fat malabsorption: typical of **severe** EPI, but also untreated celiac disease, diseases of the terminal ileum, short bowel syndrome.
- **¹³C-mixed triglyceride breath test:** established alternative to quantitative fecal fat; exhaled ¹³CO₂ after a labelled meal correlates with intestinal lipid absorption and with pancreatic lipase secretion. Against the reference standard (pancreatic enzyme and/or bicarbonate output in duodenal aspirates after exogenous stimulation): **sensitivity 90%–100% for moderate to severe EPI, specificity 80%–90% in adults**. Repeated tests before and after starting pancreatic enzyme therapy can **monitor therapeutic efficacy**. Availability still limited.
- **Fecal elastase:** widely available; recommended additionally or primarily by gastroenterology societies for EPI; stable in transit and in samples; random stool sample, usually an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) specific for the human enzyme.

| Fecal elastase (p. 10) | Interpretation as printed |
|---|---|
| **<200 µg/g stool** | "Considered pathological" |
| **<100 µg/g** | Good evidence of EPI (citing the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) Clinical Practice Update on EPI) |
| **100–200 µg/g** | Indeterminate (same AGA citation) |

- Negative correlation between fecal elastase and probability of EPI, but **no clear cut-off has been established**.
- Meta-analyses: **10% false-negative rate** even in cohorts with a high pre-test probability of EPI; **11% false-positive rate** with a low pre-test probability → **a normal fecal elastase cannot exclude EPI**, and accuracy for the differential diagnosis of chronic diarrhea is limited.

#### Cystic fibrosis (explanatory text to 4.2.6, p. 10)
- **85%–90%** of patients with cystic fibrosis present with EPI → severe malabsorption at birth or in infancy; cystic fibrosis is the most important differential diagnosis in **children with EPI**.
- Outside newborn screening: diagnosis = clinical evidence (e.g., EPI) + evidence of CFTR dysfunction (sweat chloride test, CFTR molecular genetic analysis, or CFTR physiologic tests). Cystic fibrosis guidelines: **sweat chloride test first**, other tests in equivocal cases.

### Table 1 — Conditions causing enteropathy that may cause generalized malabsorption (p. 3)

| Condition | Main features |
|---|---|
| Celiac disease (incl. seronegative celiac disease, ulcerative jejuno-ileitis, refractory celiac disease type I and II, enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma, dermatitis herpetiformis) | HLA-restricted, gluten-sensitive villous atrophy; positive celiac serology in most cases |
| Collagenous sprue | Significant small bowel sub-epithelial collagen band; often associated with celiac disease or other enteropathies (e.g., olmesartan enteropathy) |
| Autoimmune enteropathy | Pediatric or adult onset; often with other autoimmune disorders; anti-enterocyte antibodies positive |
| Iatrogenic enteropathies | Olmesartan and other angiotensin II receptor blockers, mycophenolate mofetil, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, checkpoint inhibitors, graft-versus-host disease, transplanted small intestine, small bowel radiation |
| Common variable immune deficiency | Deficient serum IgA, IgG and immunoglobulin M (IgM); more infections, autoimmunity and cancers; **absent plasma cells in duodenal biopsies** |
| Crohn's disease | May affect the whole gastrointestinal tract with skip, deep, transmural ulcers; granulomas may be present |
| Tropical sprue | Villous atrophy in people living in poor hygienic conditions, especially rural developing countries; incidence falling as hygiene improves |
| Eosinophilic enteropathy | Eosinophilic infiltration of small bowel mucosa; may accompany other T-helper 2 (Th2) disorders or wider gastrointestinal eosinophilic involvement |
| Food protein-induced enterocolitis | Rare, cell-mediated food hypersensitivity in infants; severe diarrhea with villous atrophy, dehydration and sepsis-like picture after cow's milk, soy or other food proteins |
| Protein energy malnutrition | Usually partial villous atrophy in severely starved patients |
| Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth | In patients with predisposing conditions; may rarely cause villous atrophy |
| Indolent CD4+ T-cell lymphoma | Slow-progressing non-Hodgkin lymphoma |
| HIV enteropathy | Villous atrophy in human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection; may cause severe weight loss and "wasting syndrome"; usually recovers with antiretroviral therapy |
| Giardiasis | Parasitic infection that may become chronic, causing villous atrophy and diarrhea; diagnosis by finding *G. lamblia* in feces, duodenal aspirate or duodenal biopsy |
| Whipple's disease | *Tropheryma whipplei*; typically middle-aged men; usually starts with arthritis; may be systemic; periodic acid–Schiff (PAS)-positive macrophages in the duodenal lamina propria |
| Idiopathic/unclassified villous atrophy | Villous atrophy not falling under any of the above conditions |

## Relevance to Wiki

No wiki page on malabsorption as a syndrome exists; this source plus Part 2 would seed one.
- **[[exocrine-pancreatic-insufficiency]]** — ¹³C-mixed triglyceride breath test as alternative for fat malabsorption and EPI, sensitivity 90%–100% / specificity 80%–90% for moderate–severe EPI, usable to monitor enzyme therapy (4.2.5); fecal elastase false-negative 10% and false-positive 11% by pre-test probability; CFTR gene study and sweat chloride in young patients with EPI and suspected cystic fibrosis, sweat test first (4.2.6). - **[[celiac-disease]]** — no-biopsy approach in selected adults (TGA >10 times ULN plus moderate–high pre-test probability) as an evolving European view; one-third normal endoscopy; scalloping/flattened folds → biopsy; capsule before double-balloon enteroscopy in suspected complicated celiac disease (4.2.2 text).

- **[[bile-acid-diarrhea]]** — SeHCAT high sensitivity and specificity; C4 and FGF-19 promising; no cut-offs (4.2.1 text). Adds nothing decision-changing.
- **[[short-bowel-syndrome]]** — D-xylose limited to tertiary follow-up of intestinal failure after small bowel transplant or on GLP-2 analogue (4.2.3).
- **[[giardiasis]]**, **[[nsaid-enteropathy]]**, **[[atrophic-gastritis]]**, **[[iron-deficiency-anemia]]** — listed as malabsorption causes or presentations (Tables 1–3); minor.
- **[[capsule-endoscopy]]**, **[[device-assisted-enteroscopy]]** — capsule-first sequence for suspected complicated celiac disease (4.2.2 text).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

### Inconsistencies within the document
- **Agreement rule:** section 2.3 says no agreement cut-off was set and all statements finished at >80% agreement; section 2.4 says "each final statement was agreed upon by all the participants." Per-statement agreement is not reported.
- **Pediatric D-xylose sensitivity printed as "(< 91%)"** while called "highly sensitive" (p. 9) — the sign reads opposite to the claim.
- **Fecal elastase cut-off:** the text calls <200 µg/g "pathological" and in the next sentences says no clear cut-off has been established, then quotes the AGA <100 / 100–200 µg/g bands (p. 10).
- **Statement 3.3.1 vs its text:** the statement says consider malabsorption in patients with known gastrointestinal, pancreatic, liver and biliary **diseases**; the text says in **all patients with symptoms** of gastrointestinal, pancreatic and biliary diseases (liver omitted) (p. 6).
- **Working-group arithmetic:** three research questions were assigned to each of four working groups "for a total of 11 areas" (p. 3).
- **Angiotensin receptor blockers:** Table 1 lists olmesartan and other angiotensin II receptor blockers; Table 2 prints "angiotensin II type 2 receptor blockers".
- **Overlap:** statement 4.2.1 restates a sentence of the explanatory text to 3.3.2 almost word for word.
- **Abstract vs Part 1 content:** the abstract's statements on nutritional support and teduglutide in short bowel syndrome belong to Part 2; Part 1 has no statement on them.

### Differences from US sources on file
- **Breath testing for SIBO** — [[acg-2020-sibo|ACG 2020]] Recs 1–3 (conditional, very low quality) suggest glucose or lactulose hydrogen breath testing in irritable bowel syndrome, suspected motility disorders, and symptomatic patients after luminal abdominal surgery. This source (4.2.4) says the evidence **does not support** breath testing for diagnosing SIBO. Opposite direction — **ACG 2020 wins on the wiki**. [[aga-2020-sibo|AGA 2020]] makes no recommendation either way but says breath testing "appears to identify subjects likely to respond to treatment"; this source says results do not predict antibiotic response.
- **Breath-test accuracy** — this source's meta-analysis (lactulose 42% / 70.6%; glucose in predisposed patients 81.7% / 78.8%) differs from the ranges [[acg-2020-sibo|ACG 2020]] prints (lactulose 31–68% / 44–100%; glucose 20–93% / 30–86%). Different analyses; not directly comparable.
- **Celiac disease without biopsy in adults** — [[acg-2022-celiac|ACG 2022]] Rec 1A (strong, moderate) recommends EGD with multiple duodenal biopsies to confirm the diagnosis in children and adults; Rec 1B (conditional, moderate) suggests high-level tissue transglutaminase (TTG) IgA (>10× ULN) with a positive EMA in a second blood sample as reliable for diagnosis in children, and in symptomatic adults unwilling or unable to undergo upper GI endoscopy the same criteria may be considered after the fact as a diagnosis of likely celiac disease. This source states adult diagnosis must be confirmed by biopsy per current guidelines, but cites evidence for no-biopsy in selected adults with TGA >10 times ULN and moderate–high pre-test probability, without mentioning EMA, and defers wide use until more evidence. Not a recommendation; US position stands.
- **Fecal elastase** — cut-offs (<100 µg/g good evidence; 100–200 µg/g indeterminate) are quoted from the AGA Clinical Practice Update and agree with [[aga-2023-epi|AGA 2023]] on the EPI page; AGA 2023's text also calls <200 µg/g abnormal.
- **¹³C-mixed triglyceride breath test** — this source suggests it for fat malabsorption and allows it to detect EPI (4.2.5); [[aga-2023-epi|AGA 2023]] Best Practice Advice 9 says breath tests and direct pancreatic function tests hold promise but are not widely available in the United States. Same direction; availability differs.
- **Cystic fibrosis and EPI** — this source: 85%–90% of patients with cystic fibrosis have EPI; the EPI page (AGA 2023) prints 85%. Compatible.
- **Fecal fat** — this source: 72-h collection after typically 100 g fat; ≤7% of ingested fat excreted on a 100 g/d diet. [[aga-2023-epi|AGA 2023]] (Best Practice Advice 6): known-fat diet for 5 days with stool collected over the final 3 days; steatorrhea = coefficient of fat absorption <93%. Same threshold; this source gives no diet duration.
