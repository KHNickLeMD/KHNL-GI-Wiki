## Summary

ASGE consensus conference on the use of endoscopy in eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE). Earlier EoE guidelines focused on definitions, diagnostic criteria and medical therapy; this document fills the gap on **biopsy sampling, endoscopic grading, response assessment, dilation, and monitoring**. Part 2 of the series is [[asge-2026-eoe-endoscopic-management-part-2]].

Method: modified RAND/University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) appropriateness methodology (Delphi). A core group of 9 experts proposed statements in Patient/Population/Problem, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) format; literature search 1993–present (Ovid MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, Embase); the full 20-member international panel (adult and pediatric gastroenterology, pediatric allergy, therapeutic endoscopy, a quality-measures expert) then voted online. A statement was accepted when **at least 80% of voters scored it 7–9 on a 1–9 acceptance scale**. Process ran October 16, 2020 – June 11, 2021. **20 statements reached consensus (Table 1); 8 did not (Supplementary Table 2).**

The authors stress an **absence of high-quality evidence** — much of the consensus rests on expert clinical judgment plus published (largely retrospective, uncontrolled) studies. Core messages: ≥6 esophageal biopsies from distal and mid/proximal levels regardless of appearance (and at a food impaction); the Endoscopic Reference System (EREFS) at every endoscopy; endoscopy + biopsy, not symptoms, to judge response; dilation is safe (including with active inflammation), targets typically ≥16 mm, is reached gradually ("start low and go slow"), and is paired with anti-inflammatory therapy; perforation in EoE is usually managed conservatively.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Consensus statements (all 20)

Grading: the document assigns **no evidence grades, no strength labels, and no per-statement vote percentages** — every listed statement met the acceptance threshold (≥80% of voters scoring 7–9). Numbers are the document's own (Table 1); wording is Table 1's, verbatim.

| # | Statement |
|---|---|
| | **Endoscopic diagnosis — biopsy** |
| 1 | For the diagnosis of EoE, at least 6 biopsy samples should be taken from the esophagus. |
| 2 | Biopsy samples should be taken from the distal and mid-/proximal esophagus for the diagnosis of EoE. |
| 3 | In a patient with suspected EoE, biopsy samples should be obtained from the esophagus regardless of endoscopic appearance. |
| 4 | At the index endoscopy for EoE patients, multiple biopsy samples should be taken from the stomach and duodenum to assess for eosinophilic gastroenteritis in patients with compatible symptoms and/or endoscopic abnormalities. |
| 5 | Esophageal biopsy samples should be obtained at the time of food impaction if that is the initial presentation of suspected EoE. |
| | **Endoscopic grading** |
| 6 | The EoE EREFS should be used routinely for assessing EoE activity during endoscopy. |
| 7 | In patients with suspected or established EoE, the EREFS applied to the highest scoring area should be used to assess the entire esophagus at each endoscopy. |
| 8 | In patients with EoE, an improvement in inflammatory features (ie, furrows, exudates, and edema) at each endoscopy is a desired endpoint of pharmacologic or dietary therapy. |
| | **Assessing response to therapy** |
| 9 | Endoscopy and biopsy sampling, and not symptoms alone, are needed to assess EoE activity before and after any change in dietary elimination therapy or pharmacologic treatment. |
| | **Endoscopic dilation** |
| 10 | Endoscopic dilation can be considered for all patients with EoE and an esophageal stricture with dysphagia. |
| 11 | The immediate endpoint of endoscopic dilation is the appearance of a mucosal disruption or reaching the target diameter. |
| 12 | In adult and adolescent patients with EoE, a goal luminal diameter that relieves dysphagia and food impaction (typically ≥16 mm) should be achieved over ≥1 sessions based on the initial caliber of the lumen and effect noted during dilation. |
| 13 | To lower the risk of perforation, achieving an esophageal luminal diameter of 16 mm may necessitate gradual dilation in >1 session of dilation. |
| 14 | Effective management of esophageal inflammation in patients with EoE and dysphagia attenuates the need for future endoscopic dilation. |
| 15 | In patients with EoE, different dilation techniques chosen on the basis of stricture characteristics and endoscopists' preference are acceptable for performing dilation therapy. |

| | **Monitoring disease** |

### Statements that did NOT reach consensus (Supplementary Table 2)

Not recommendations — each failed the ≥80% threshold. The document prints **no vote numbers** for them. Numbering is Supplementary Table 2's.

| 2 | An EREFS score ≤2 (most involved areas; scale 0–9: edema 0/1, rings 0/1/2/3, exudates 0/1/2, furrows 0/1/2, stricture 0/1) is an adequate definition of remission |
| 3 | An EREFS score >3 is considered evidence of relapse |
| 4 | Baseline impedance planimetry should be performed during endoscopy |
| 5 | Using the sponge or string device is sufficient to monitor response to therapy |
| 6 | Esophageal stenting may be performed to treat iatrogenic or spontaneous perforation due to EoE |
| 7 | All esophageal biopsy samples should be placed into 1 jar |
| 8 | Periodic dilation therapy is inadequate for treating symptoms of EoE with esophageal fibrostenosis without effective pharmacologic or diet therapy |

- **Jars (item 7):** distal biopsy is 100% sensitive, so distal eosinophilia does not separate EoE from gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)–induced eosinophilia; isolated proximal/mid eosinophilia without distal eosinophilia does not occur. Most experts still favor sampling the mid/proximal esophagus; **some endorse separate jars by location** (Discussion).
- **Dilation monotherapy (item 8):** periodic dilation alone has relieved dysphagia for as long as 15 months and may spare long-term proton pump inhibitor (PPI) or topical steroid risk; weighed against evidence that no medical therapy may be associated with fibrosis and stricture formation.
- **EREFS remission/relapse cut-points (items 2–3):** data only beginning to emerge.

### Supporting evidence — biopsy (Statements 1–5)

- **Number (Statement 1):** EoE is patchy; normal and abnormal counts coexist in one patient.
  - 66 adults: sensitivity for >15 eosinophils per high-power field (eos/hpf) **100% with 5 biopsies vs 55% with 1**.
  - Pediatric study: sensitivity **84%, 97%, 100%** with **2, 3, 6** biopsies.
  - 102 patients (mid + distal): probability of >15 eos/hpf with **1, 4, 5, 6** biopsies = **.63, .98, .99, >.99**.
- **Sites (Statement 2):** multiple levels, not 1 location; ideally target visible inflammation.
  - Distal density higher than proximal (mean 82 vs 68 eos/hpf); distal-only biopsy diagnosed 100%; proximal-only would have missed 20%.
  - Biopsy just below the upper esophageal sphincter is unlikely to show eosinophilia.
  - Proximal vs mid sensitivity unclear; 4-quadrant vs variable-level protocol not evaluated.
- **Normal-appearing mucosa (Statement 3):** normal endoscopy in **10%–32%** of pediatric and adult EoE; up to **46%** in adult dysphagia series; up to **38%** of pediatric food impactions normal or only erythematous.
  - Typical findings: sensitivity 31%, specificity 93% in one small study (13 EoE patients); sensitivities 95%–100% in large phase II/III trials and >90% in careful studies — subtle findings may be missed by less-experienced endoscopists.
- **Stomach and duodenum (Statement 4):** biopsy when symptoms or endoscopic features suggest a non-EoE eosinophilic gastrointestinal disorder (EGID) — **nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain**; endoscopic **erythema, nodularity, ulceration**, or a normal appearance.
  - Also biopsy the duodenum when family history, symptoms or endoscopic features suggest [[celiac-disease|celiac disease]].
  - Routine extraesophageal biopsy is low yield: only **2.5%–3%** of adults with EoE had eosinophilic gastritis, duodenitis or celiac disease, most with symptoms/endoscopic features; pediatric follow-up gastric/duodenal biopsies mostly normal or nonspecific.
- **Food impaction (Statement 5):** common initial presentation in children and adults.
  - Examine the mucosa **away from the impaction site**; take standard **distal and proximal** biopsies, **avoiding the impaction site** (acute local changes confound histology).
  - Skipping biopsy forces another endoscopy, delays diagnosis and treatment, and risks loss to follow-up.
  - Patient instability or aspiration risk may not allow time to biopsy.

### Supporting evidence — grading and response (Statements 6–9)

**Simplified EREFS for clinical use** (document's suggested scoring, page 581):

| Feature | Grades |
|---|---|
| **E**dema | absent or present |
| **R**ings | absent, mild, moderate, or severe |
| **E**xudate | absent, mild, or severe |
| **F**urrows | absent or present |
| **S**tricture | absent or present, with estimated diameter |

*Simplified EREFS grading for clinical reporting*
*Source: data from the simplified scoring described in the text of the ASGE 2022 consensus*
*[[asge-2022-eoe-endoscopic-approach-consensus|ASGE 2022 EoE Endoscopic Approach Consensus]]*
*EREFS = Endoscopic Reference System*

- **Shorthand:** feature letters + severity — e.g., edema present, rings moderate, exudate severe, furrows present, stricture 10 mm = **E1R2Ex2F1S10**. A treatment response example: E1R0Ex2F1S0 → E0R0Ex0F0S0.
- **Inflammatory features** = edema, exudates, furrows; **fibrostenotic/remodeling** = rings, stricture. Anti-inflammatory treatments (PPI, swallowed/topical corticosteroids, diet) act on the inflammatory features; dilation acts on rings, strictures and dysphagia.
- **Exudates correlate best** with biopsy eosinophil counts.
- **EREFS performance:** validated inter-/intraobserver agreement; sensitivity and specificity 90% (pediatric and adult); responsive to therapy, nonsignificant change with placebo.
- **But EREFS cannot replace histology for response:** area under the curve .79–.88 for histologic remission (peak ≤15 eos/hpf) → misclassifies **12%–21%**. Use it **with, not in place of**, histology.
- **Statement 7 rationale:** peak (most affected area) scoring is what trials and validation studies use; data are inadequate to substitute a mean whole-esophagus score.
- **Symptoms alone are not enough (Statement 9):** patient-reported outcome instruments (EoE Activity Index, Dysphagia Symptom Questionnaire, Pediatric EoE Symptom Score v2.0) do not adequately predict endoscopic or histologic remission. Endoscopic features were not endorsed as a primary therapy endpoint either — not pathognomonic.
- **Goal:** eosinophil infiltration below the diagnostic threshold.
- **When to re-scope with biopsy:** to evaluate a regimen, after a new treatment when the previous one failed, with changes in symptoms or adherence, and to identify food triggers (children and adults).
- **Timing:** repeat **no earlier than 4 weeks after a change in diet therapy**, or **8–12 weeks** after a change in pharmacologic treatment.
- No peripheral or noninvasive marker currently predicts esophageal inflammation.

### Supporting evidence — dilation (Statements 10–18)

**Indication and effect (Statements 10, 14, 16)**
- Dilation preferably **combined with medical and/or dietary treatment** — dilation does not reduce mucosal eosinophilia.
- Effective in diffusely narrowed (small-caliber) esophagus and single or multiple strictures; also in severe strictures **<10 mm**, most reaching **≥15 mm**. No outcome differences by device in clinical series.
- **Dilation-predominant long-term strategy** can be considered for a persistently symptomatic refractory stricture, or as a temporizing measure while medical therapy starts or continues.
- Without maintenance anti-inflammatory therapy, **50% have recurrent dysphagia at 15 months** after dilation.
- Histologic response to steroids (peak <15 eos/hpf): **65% decrease** in subsequent dilations (19 months); 1.6 vs 4.6 subsequent dilations in responders vs nonresponders.
- Maintenance therapy: repeat dilation **29% vs 89%** without maintenance over 3 years (hazard ratio .12); 75% of those on maintenance needing repeat dilation had stopped therapy or lost response.
- Adults with strictures <10 mm: histologic remission (<15 eos/hpf) significantly associated with reaching ≥15 mm. In 66 patients with a diameter ≤10 mm, histologic remission (odds ratio 34.97) and initial diameter (odds ratio 1.58) predicted reaching ≥15 mm.
- Stopping therapy after remission: caliber fell in patients with baseline strictures (16.7 mm at remission → 15.4 mm at symptom recurrence off treatment).

**Endpoint and target (Statements 11–13)**
- **Immediate endpoint:** mucosal disruption or target diameter. Visible mucosal disruption after through-the-scope balloon or between dilators is expected, **not an adverse event** — it signals the goal for that session is at or near.
- Factors in dilation success: predilation diameter, location/extent of fibrostenosis, inflammation or tissue fragility, moderate resistance, blood on the dilator, visible mucosal disruption. Absence of heme on the withdrawn bougie does not exclude dilation effect.
- **Target:** limited evidence for a specific diameter (eg, 16–18 mm).
  - 207 adults: mean diameter 11 ± 3 → 16 ± 2 mm with significant dysphagia improvement.
  - 50 adults with a bougie cap: median 12 → 16 mm; EoE Activity Index score median 32 → 0 at 2 weeks.
  - Functional lumen imaging probe diameter **<17 mm** associated with higher food-impaction risk → supports a target of **16–18 mm**.
- **Per-session increment:** when serial dilations are needed, common practice is **not to exceed >3 mm in a single session** → multiple sessions may be needed to reach **15–18 mm**.
- **"Start low and go slow":** as many sessions as needed to reach symptom relief and **16 mm** in a controlled fashion. Expert opinion: perforation risk depends more on the **change in diameter in 1 session** than on absolute dilator size.
- Deep rents/tears may cause **chest pain in up to 17%**. Forewarn patients; worsening chest pain, shortness of breath, fever or chills suggest perforation. Counsel that more than 1 session may be needed, especially for narrow strictures.

*EoE dilation adverse-event rates per meta-analysis*
*Source: data from the three systematic reviews as cited in the ASGE 2022 consensus*

*EoE = eosinophilic esophagitis*

- No deaths after dilation (Moawad); none of the 9 Dougherty perforations needed surgery or caused death. Most perforations reported before 2009 (adult rate .44% before vs .030% after 2009).
- Larger dilators (>17 mm): perforation 1.35% vs .03% with smaller dilators; also more hospitalization and chest pain.
- Bougie vs balloon does not appear to influence risk.

**Technique (Statements 15, 18)**
- Endoscopy **misses narrowing of 12–15 mm**: vs barium esophagram, sensitivity 14.7% and specificity 79.2%; at a cutoff of <15 mm, sensitivity only 25%. Look for strictures at multiple sites.
- Suspected nonvisualized symptomatic stricture → careful **empiric panesophageal dilation** toward **at least 16 mm**, with any technique.
- Bougies and through-the-scope balloons have **equal efficacy**.
  - **Bougie:** fixed diameter, fast, inexpensive, dilates the whole esophagus. **Savary over a guidewire** preferred for narrower strictures (**<15 mm**); fluoroscopy rarely needed when adult and 5-mm pediatric endoscopes are available. **Maloney** for larger diameters, excellent tactile feedback.
  - **Stop** if moderate resistance is met or repeat endoscopy shows mucosal disruption.

> [!howto] Balloon pull-through for panesophageal dilation
> 1. Examine the whole esophagus.
> 2. Position a multisize balloon across the esophagogastric junction: **8-9-10 mm** if the adult endoscope meets resistance; **10-11-12 mm** if it passes easily (starting size may need to be modified for strictures narrower than 8 mm).
> 3. Inflate to the smallest size in front of the scope; withdraw slowly distal → proximal. Narrowing = balloon will not pull through easily.
> 4. No resistance → repeat with the next size, serially.
> 5. Stop when a mucosal tear is seen through the balloon.

- **Bougie cap** (single-use, dome-shaped transparent cap taped to the scope tip): feasible, safe, short-term symptom improvement in 50 adults.
- **Inflammatory activity (Statement 17):** early case reports of perforation/hemorrhage caused hesitancy; larger series and systematic reviews show adverse-event rates similar to other benign strictures; major adverse events <1%. Smaller studies suggested younger age, repeat dilation and proximal stricture as risk factors (not confirmed in larger studies). Panel: dilation is supported when indicated **regardless of inflammatory activity or treatment status**.
- **Empiric dilation (Statement 18):** longstanding inflammation can leave irreversible structural change despite endoscopic and histologic remission → consider dilation of visible strictures or empirically when none is seen; same initial-size and caliber rules apply.

### Supporting evidence — perforation (Statement 19)

- Uncommon. Causes: prolonged food impaction in undiagnosed EoE (Boerhaave syndrome) or instrumentation — **pushing an impacted food bolus** is a more common cause than dilation alone.
- Management as for non-EoE perforation; **nonsurgical in most**.
  - Small, recognized early: nothing per mouth (NPO) + antibiotics covering oral flora.
  - Clip closure (through-the-scope or over-the-scope) can be tried; often fails because of underlying fibrosis.
  - **Covered self-expandable stent, short duration (≤4 weeks)** — an option, particularly for larger perforations. Use a smaller-diameter stent (usually **18 mm** mid-body, flanges often 5 mm larger); fully covered stents are easier to remove but migrate more than partially covered ones. (The stenting statement itself failed consensus — see above.)
  - Extraluminal fluid/debris → surgery and/or tube drainage (through the perforation and/or pleural) often required.
- Treat early: in a non-EoE series, mortality **10%–25%** when treated within 24 hours vs **40%–60%** when delayed beyond 48 hours. **No death from perforation in EoE has been reported.**

### Supporting evidence — monitoring (Statement 20)

- EoE is chronic, progressive, not curable; symptoms and inflammation recur after stopping successful therapy; symptoms correlate only modestly with inflammation.
- Practical consequences: long-term management strategy; **continue anti-inflammatory maintenance** after remission; scheduled clinic visits for disease complications and drug/diet side effects.
- Absence of symptoms does not guarantee remission → periodic endoscopy with structured biopsy, or less-invasive methods (string or sponge test), can be considered in symptom-free patients.
- Interval: little data; expert opinion — **at least annual clinical evaluation** in well-controlled patients is reasonable.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

Newer tier-1 sources ([[acg-2025-eoe|ACG 2025]], [[asge-2026-eoe-endoscopic-management-part-2|ASGE 2026 Part 2]]) govern the wiki where they differ.

- **Re-scope timing after a diet change.** This consensus: no earlier than **4 weeks** after a diet change; 8–12 weeks after a pharmacologic change. ASGE 2026 Statement 23: **6–8 weeks** for diet elimination, 8–12 weeks for PPIs and topical steroids, up to 24 weeks for biologics. ACG 2025 text: 8–12 weeks after starting PPI, swallowed topical corticosteroid or food elimination diet. **ASGE 2026 / ACG 2025 govern.**
- **Gastric and duodenal biopsy at diagnosis — children.** This consensus: only with compatible symptoms and/or endoscopic abnormalities (routine sampling failed consensus). ASGE 2026 Statement 4: in **children** with suspected EoE, esophageal, gastric **and** duodenal biopsies should be obtained. **ASGE 2026 governs for children**; the symptom-based rule here still stands for adults.
- **Monitoring in remission.** Statement 20 asks for symptom monitoring with *consideration* of periodic endoscopy. ACG 2025 advises against monitoring symptoms alone to assess treatment response and calls for structured follow-up; ASGE 2026 Statement 28 calls for **structured clinical and endoscopic monitoring**. Newer sources are firmer; not a direct conflict.
- **Less-invasive monitoring.** "Sponge or string device is sufficient to monitor response" failed consensus here. ASGE 2026 Statements 26–27: transnasal endoscopy, string and sponge/balloon devices **can be considered** for monitoring, but not for initial diagnosis or for patients who may need dilation. **ASGE 2026 governs.**
- **EREFS remission threshold.** EREFS ≤2 as remission and >3 as relapse both failed consensus here. ACG 2025 text: an endoscopic response threshold of EREFS ≤2 (0–9 scale) "has been proposed and evaluated"; ASGE 2026 lists validating EREFS response thresholds as a research gap.
- **Pathology jars.** One-jar statement failed consensus here. ASGE 2026 (Statement 24 discussion): separate jars may make sense with topical therapy; may combine with systemic therapy. Compatible; ASGE 2026 adds the rule.
- **Food-impaction biopsy sites.** This consensus: distal and proximal, avoiding the impaction site. [[asge-2011-foreign-body-ingestion|ASGE 2011]] (older): mid and distal, with dilation deferrable pending pathology. Same tier; **this 2022 consensus is newer** for the sites; deferral of dilation is not addressed here.
- **Dilation target and approach — no conflict.** Statement 12 (typically ≥16 mm, over ≥1 sessions) is carried verbatim into ACG 2025 Table 7, and ACG 2025 separately advises "start low and go slow".
- **Internal tension — stenting.** The text calls a short-duration covered stent "an option" for perforation, yet the stenting statement failed consensus.
- **Evidence base.** No randomized trials of endoscopy-guided vs empiric management; dilation data are largely retrospective, uncontrolled, single-center. All 20 statements rest on expert consensus.
