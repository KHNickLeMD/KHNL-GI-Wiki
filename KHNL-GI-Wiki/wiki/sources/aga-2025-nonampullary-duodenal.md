## Summary

Nonampullary duodenal polyps are found in up to **5% of upper endoscopies** — vast majority incidental in asymptomatic patients. Most are benign; **adenomas account for 10%–20%** of these lesions. Duodenal adenomas (DAs) arise sporadically (seen in up to **0.5%** of patients) or with a genetic condition ([[familial-adenomatous-polyposis|FAP]] or *MUTYH*-associated polyposis); **>90% of FAP patients** eventually develop 1 or many DAs. Most DAs contain only low-grade dysplasia, but **up to 20% progress to high-grade dysplasia and ~5% to cancer**, so endoscopic resection is generally recommended.

The Update describes how patients should be **evaluated and risk-stratified**, best approaches to **endoscopic resection and surveillance**, and **management of complications**. International guidelines recommend all DAs be considered for endoscopic resection, but this carries a **~15% adverse-event rate** (predominantly bleeding and perforation) with substantial local recurrence on surveillance. Because the time course to malignant transformation is prolonged and resection morbidity is high, patient comorbidities and anticipated longevity must be weighed. Technique is individualized: **piecemeal cold-snare resection** for flat lesions <20 mm (minimal recurrence, less bleeding); **conventional hot-snare EMR + thermal ablation of the margin** for lesions >20 mm or with bulky Paris Is components. Postprocedural bleeding (usually first 48 h, risk proportional to size, >25% for lesions >3 cm) and perforation are the major complications. Initial surveillance at **6 months**. FAP duodenal disease is managed by **Spiegelman staging**.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Best Practice Advice statements (verbatim, complete):**

1. Non-neoplastic duodenal lesions (eg, metaplastic foveolar epithelium and gastric heterotopia) may mimic neoplastic adenomatous pathology. Careful optical evaluation and pathologic correlation may be necessary to exclude dysplasia. Nondysplastic lesions do not require endoscopic resection unless they are symptomatic or bleeding.

2. Ideal duodenal endoscopic inspection includes identification of the major and minor papilla with photodocumentation to ensure no involvement by the lesion. Adding a clear distal attachment device to a forward-viewing gastroscope improves visualization of the papilla and the medial wall. A side-viewing duodenoscope should be used when the major and minor papilla are not visible with the gastroscope and for most lesions on the medial wall of the duodenum within 5 cm of the ampulla.

3. All duodenal polyps should be described according to their size, Paris morphology, suspected histologic layer of origin (mucosal lesion or subepithelial lesion), duodenal location (D1–4) and orientation (anterior, posterior, medial, or lateral wall), and proximity/relationship to the major papilla to facilitate therapeutic planning and subsequent surveillance.

4. Given the high frequency of concomitant colonic adenomas in patients with duodenal adenomas, on identification of a duodenal adenoma, a colonoscopy should be performed if a high-quality examination has not been performed in the last 3 years.

5. Routine small bowel investigation (ie, capsule endoscopy) is not advised in patients with sporadic and nonsporadic duodenal adenomas. Periodic small bowel inspection with capsule endoscopy may be of benefit in patients with Peutz-Jeghers syndrome.

6. Definitive treatment of duodenal adenomas by endoscopic resection is less morbid, resource-intensive, and expensive than surgery and is therefore the preferred treatment option.

7. Due to the risk of malignant transformation, all sporadic duodenal adenomas should be considered for endoscopic resection. However, in comparison with colonic adenomas, the time course to malignant transformation may be much prolonged, and the risk of resection-related morbidity much greater. Therefore, the comorbidities and anticipated longevity of the patient must be carefully factored into the decision-making process.

8. The approach to endoscopic duodenal resection (ie, hot vs cold and conventional vs underwater endoscopic mucosal resection) should be individualized to reduce bleeding risk, based on lesion size, morphology, patient comorbidities, and endoscopic comfort level with specific techniques. Piecemeal cold snare resection for flat duodenal adenomas mitigates postprocedural bleeding risk and, for lesions <20 mm, is effective and carries a minimal risk of recurrence. In patients with comorbidities with flat nonbulky lesions measuring <20 mm, cold snare resection can be considered.

9. Currently, duodenal adenomas >20 mm or with large Paris subtype Is components should be removed by conventional hot snare endoscopic mucosal resection. Thermal ablation of the post–endoscopic mucosal resection margin to mitigate the risk of recurrence to <2%–5% is safe and effective and should be considered.

10. Endoscopists performing duodenal polyp resection should be aware of the increased risk of postprocedural bleeding (compared with elsewhere in the gastrointestinal tract), which usually occurs in the first 48 hours after the procedure, with the risk proportional to the lesion size. For lesions >3 cm, bleeding risk is >25% and may be life-threatening and associated with hemodynamic compromise; however, after resuscitation, endoscopic hemostasis is generally effective.

11. Evaluation of the postpolypectomy/endoscopic mucosal resection defect is critical to identify concerns for postprocedural duodenal perforation, which, if unrecognized and left untreated, may be life-threatening and often mandates surgery.

12. Initial endoscopic surveillance for a completely resected duodenal adenoma should be undertaken at an interval of 6 months. Although usually diminutive, recurrence is often scarred and not amenable to conventional snare resection and may require avulsion techniques to achieve cure.

13. Nonampullary duodenal adenomas associated with familial adenomatous polyposis should be considered for endoscopic resection based on size (≥1 cm), morphologic characteristics, advanced histology (ie, high-grade dysplasia), and/or based on Spiegelman criteria.

**Supporting quantitative data (from the narrative):**

- **Prevalence/malignant potential:** nonampullary duodenal polyps in up to 5% of upper endoscopies; adenomas = 10%–20% of these. Sporadic DAs in up to 0.5% of patients. FAP: >90% eventually develop DAs. Most DAs low-grade dysplasia; up to 20% → HGD, ~5% → cancer. Covert adenocarcinoma within a DA is rare (≤1%).
- **Progression (BPA 7):** in 1 study, 20.9% (9 of 43) of incidental adenomas progressed to HGD at 6 months, incl. 4.7% (n=2) to carcinoma in situ; lesions initially ≥20 mm and proximal to the ampulla were associated with histologic progression.
- **Concomitant colonic neoplasia (BPA 4):** colonic neoplasia risk 2–7-fold greater than general population. In a prospective study: colonic polyps 61% (DA group) vs 37% (controls); advanced colonic adenomas (HGD, >10 mm, or villous) 18% vs 5%. Small-bowel polyp risk in DA patients is negligible — capsule not needed for screening, but colonoscopy is mandatory.
- **Endoscopic vs surgical resection (BPA 6):** in a modeling study of 102 large DAs (mean 40 mm), endoscopic resection had less morbidity (18% vs 31%), shorter hospital stay (median 1 vs 4.75 days), and was less costly (mean $11,093 vs $19,358).
- **Recurrence — conventional hot-snare EMR:** 10%–15% at 6 months, size-dependent. **Thermal ablation of the post-EMR margin (EMR-T):** recurrence 1 of 49 (2.3%) vs 19 of 108 (17.6%) with conventional EMR (median lesion 30 mm).
- **Underwater EMR** (lesions ≤20 mm): recurrence 2%–3%, perforation <1%, delayed bleeding <2%.
- **Cold-snare EMR (CS-EMR)** of large DAs (median 30 mm, n=50) vs EMR-T: lower intraprocedural bleeding (2.0% vs 37%) and postpolypectomy bleeding (4.0% vs 16.7%); 2 immediate perforations (4.0%) closed with clips; but **higher recurrence at 6 months (24.4% vs 2.3%)** — recurrence related to primary-lesion size, so CS most attractive for lesions <15 mm, especially with comorbidities.
- **Bleeding (BPA 10):** duodenal postprocedural bleeding usually in first 48 h, proportional to size; 25% for lesions ≥30 mm; >25% and potentially life-threatening for lesions >3 cm.
- **Perforation:** hot-snare excision carries ~1% perforation risk; ESD associated with high perforation rates (thin duodenal muscularis propria) with no therapeutic advantage over piecemeal EMR; clip closure of the EMR defect is generally only effective for defects ≤15 mm.
- **Periprocedural care (BPA 11):** high-risk patients (multiple comorbidities, anticoagulation, bulky lesions ≥30 mm, significant intraprocedural bleeding, possible/closed perforation) → admit for observation. Clear-liquid diet overnight, advance as tolerated; twice-daily PPI for 6–8 weeks; individualize resumption of anticoagulation/antiplatelet therapy — hold ≥48 h after resection if acceptable. Insufficient data to support prophylactic hemostatic spray/topical agents.
- **Surveillance (BPA 12):** initial at 6 months; after a negative 6-month exam, general practice is to repeat at 1 year and, if clear, annually for 2–3 years (few data on optimal timing).
- **Non-neoplastic / other entities:** non-neoplastic lesions (metaplastic foveolar epithelium, gastric heterotopia, inflammatory/pseudopolyps) most frequent in the duodenal bulb. **Brunner gland hamartomas** — uncommon, benign, often solitary pedunculated, up to 2–5 cm, from lobular proliferation of Brunner glands; resection unnecessary unless symptomatic or large (>2 cm) causing obstruction/pain/bleeding; large lesions may cause GI hemorrhage with iron deficiency; rare dysplasia. **Duodenal hamartomas** — associated with Peutz-Jeghers, Cowden, juvenile polyposis; solitary PJ-type polyps can occur without PJ syndrome; endoscopic resection generally recommended. **Subepithelial lesions** (GIST, NET, lipoma, leiomyoma) covered in a prior CPU.
- **FAP duodenal disease (BPA 13):** DAs tend to spare the duodenal bulb, mostly involve the descending duodenum with decreasing severity distally. Most adenomas flat/subtle. Tissue confirmation important, but repeated/aggressive forceps biopsy or submucosal tattooing is discouraged (avoid interference with future mucosectomy). Deep enteroscopy/capsule reserved for advanced polyposis or polyps extending toward the ligament of Treitz. Chromoendoscopy increases detection and upstages severity but clinical value not established. Surveillance may begin at age 25; **Spiegelman scoring** guides interval and management (Table 1). Stage IV (score 9–12) → surgical consult + surveillance q6–12mo; HGD and lesion size ≥10 mm are risk factors for duodenal cancer (~4% overall). Polyp removal may down-stage the Spiegelman score.

|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 4 y | — |

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Cold-snare vs hot-snare EMR tradeoff:** CS-EMR nearly eliminates bleeding but has much higher recurrence for large lesions (24.4% vs 2.3%) — the Update resolves this by lesion size (CS for flat lesions <20 mm / <15 mm; hot-snare EMR + thermal ablation for >20 mm).
- **No formal evidence ratings** — Best Practice Advice statements do not carry GRADE-style quality/strength ratings (systematic review not performed), unlike the [[asge-2015-ampullary-duodenal-adenomas]] guideline.
- **Optimal long-term surveillance timing** beyond the 6-month exam is expert-opinion-driven; few data.
- **Spiegelman thresholds** (this Update) are consistent with those cited on [[familial-adenomatous-polyposis]] via [[acg-2015-hereditary-gi-cancer]]; adenocarcinoma-risk percentages by stage differ across source cohorts (e.g., Stage IV 36% here vs higher lifetime figures elsewhere) — cohort-dependent.
- **ESD** is not advised in the duodenum here (high perforation, no advantage over piecemeal EMR), consistent with [[asge-2015-ampullary-duodenal-adenomas]].
