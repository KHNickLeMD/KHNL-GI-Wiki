## Summary

This AGA Clinical Practice Update reviews diagnostic criteria for severe acute alcoholic hepatitis (AH) and sets out best practices for its medical management. The authors state the best practices are based on clinical trials, systematic reviews including meta-analysis, and expert opinion. The document is an **Expert Review**: it is **not** a GRADE-based guideline, it **assigns no evidence grades or strength ratings** to any statement, and the statements are labelled **Best Practice Advice (BPA) 1–12**.

The framing is that AH is an *inflammatory* decompensation of [[alcohol-associated-liver-disease|alcohol-associated liver disease]] presenting with rapid-onset [[jaundice]], malaise, anorexia, tender hepatomegaly and features of SIRS. SIRS on admission predicts multi-organ failure (MOF), and MOF — usually driven by infection developing *after* the AH diagnosis — is what kills these patients. [[aki-in-cirrhosis|AKI]] is the early manifestation of MOF, which is why the update devotes separate advice to culturing everyone with jaundice, avoiding iodinated contrast, and using diuretics and other nephrotoxins sparingly.

On therapy the update is deliberately modest. Glucocorticoids (methylprednisolone 32 mg daily, equivalent to prednisolone 40 mg) are offered for MDF >32 or MELD >20 without contraindication, with the explicit caveats that survival benefit does not extend beyond 28 days and that the benefit within 28 days is *modest*. Pentoxifylline 400 mg three times daily is positioned only as an option when glucocorticoids are contraindicated, with "conflicting" efficacy data — a stance that has since been superseded (see *Contradictions*). Severe AH with MELD >26 plus good insight and social support should be referred for liver transplant evaluation. Nutrition is treated as a primary therapy, not an adjunct.

The concluding section is unusually candid that current anti-inflammatory therapy is "clearly suboptimal" — 1-year mortality was 56% in STOPAH, and 4-year mortality in the VA Cooperative Study for AH superimposed on cirrhosis was 65%, worse than many cancers.

## Best Practice Advice (verbatim, BPA 1–12)

**No evidence grades, strength ratings, or GRADE assignments appear anywhere in this document.** The twelve statements below are the complete set, numbered as the article numbers them.

> **Best Practice Advice 1:** Abstinence from drinking alcohol is the cornerstone of treatment for alcohol hepatitis (AH).

> **Best Practice Advice 2:** Patients with jaundice and suspected AH should have cultures of blood, urine, and ascites, if present, to determine the presence of bacterial infections regardless of whether they have fever.

> **Best Practice Advice 3:** Patients with AH who have jaundice should be admitted to the hospital to encourage abstinence, restore adequate nutrition, and exclude serious infections.

> **Best Practice Advice 4:** Imaging of the liver is warranted as part of the evaluation, but caution should be used in administering iodinated contrast dye, as it increases the risk of acute kidney injury (AKI).

> **Best Practice Advice 5:** Patients with AH require a diet with 1-1.5 g protein and 30-40 kcal/kg body weight for adequate recovery. If the patient is unable to eat because of anorexia or altered mental status, a feeding tube should be considered for enteral feeding. Parenteral nutrition alone is inadequate.

> **Best Practice Advice 6:** Severity and prognosis of AH should be evaluated using Maddrey Discriminant Function (MDF), Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD), age, bilirubin, international normalized ratio, and creatinine (ABIC), or Glasgow scoring systems. Current treatments are based on this assessment.

> **Best Practice Advice 7:** Presence of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) on admission is associated with an increased risk of multi-organ failure (MOF) syndrome. Development of MOF, usually due to infections developing after initial diagnosis of AH, is associated with a very high mortality rate.

> **Best Practice Advice 8:** Nephrotoxic drugs, including diuretics, should be avoided or used sparingly in patients with AH, since AKI is an early manifestation of MOF.

> **Best Practice Advice 9:** Patients with MDF > 32 or MELD score > 20 without a contraindication to glucocorticoid, such as hepatitis B viral infection, tuberculosis, or other serious infectious diseases, may be treated with methylprednisolone 32 mg daily, but the appropriate duration of treatment remains a subject of controversy. Methylprednisolone does not improve survival beyond 28 days, and the benefits for < 28 days are modest.

> **Best Practice Advice 10:** Patients with a contraindication to glucocorticoids may be treated with pentoxifylline 400 mg three times daily with meals. Data regarding the efficacy are conflicting.

> **Best Practice Advice 11:** Patients with severe AH, particularly those with a MELD score > 26 with good insight into their alcohol use disorder and good social support should be referred for evaluation for liver transplantation, as the 90-day mortality rate is very high.

> **Best Practice Advice 12:** Patients with mild to moderate AH defined by a MELD score < 20 and MDF < 32 should be referred for abstinence counseling and prescribed a high protein diet supplemented with B vitamins and folic acid.

**One internal wording difference.** Table 2 restates BPA 3 in expanded form: *"Patients with AH who have jaundice require further evaluation to determine severity. Those with severe AH or inadequate social and medical support should be admitted to the hospital to encourage abstinence, restore adequate nutrition, and exclude serious infections."* The BPA list in the text admits all jaundiced AH patients; the table version admits those with severe disease or inadequate support.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Definition and diagnosis**

- Histologic features of AH: steatosis, ballooning degeneration of hepatocytes (steatohepatitis), intrahepatic cholestasis (bilirubinostasis), chicken-wire fibrosis, Mallory-Denk bodies, megamitochondria. **Cirrhosis is present in the vast majority of those who are severely ill.**
- The NIAAA Alcoholic Hepatitis Consortium consensus statement defines AH as jaundice within **60 days** of heavy consumption (**>50 g/day**) of alcohol for a minimum of **6 months**, serum bilirubin **>3 mg/dL**, AST **50–400 U/L**, **AST:ALT >1.5**, and no other obvious cause for hepatitis.
- Consensus classification: **definite** when a liver biopsy established the diagnosis; **probable** when clinical and laboratory features were present without potential confounding problems; **possible** when confounding problems were present.
- Rationale for the classification: **10%–15% of subjects diagnosed with acute AH on clinical criteria alone did not have characteristic histologic features** on biopsy.
- Risk factors identified: **female gender, elevated body mass index, and the G allele of *PNPLA3***. Because elevated BMI is a risk factor for both AH and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, distinguishing the two usually rests on the amount of alcohol consumed and the rapid onset of jaundice.

**Prognostic scores (as printed in this document)**

- Maddrey et al (1977) used **serum bilirubin and prothrombin time** to define a group of AH patients whose 28-day mortality was above 50% — the Maddrey Discriminant Function (MDF).
- Early trials and a subsequent randomized multicenter trial indicated **only patients with more severe manifestations of AH and MDF >32 benefited from glucocorticoids**.
- MELD predicts 28-day and 90-day mortality; **ABIC** (age, bilirubin, INR, creatinine) and the **Glasgow AH Score** also predict 90-day mortality. All these scoring systems have been validated to predict severe outcomes with a high degree of reliability.
- **The Lille score** uses data obtained from the **first week of treatment with prednisolone** to predict whether an individual patient is likely to have a favorable outcome; combining Lille with MELD has been proposed as a further refinement. **This document names the Lille score but does not print its numeric cut-point**, and it prints no score formulas or component weightings for MDF, ABIC, or GAHS beyond the variables listed above.
- Although MDF >32 initially defined a group with 28-day mortality >50%, **short-term mortality in the placebo group in recent studies is much better — 17% in STOPAH vs 35% in the U.S. multicenter trial** — likely because of improvements in best supportive care. Improved outcome with BSC makes direct comparison with historical data difficult.

**Trial evidence**

- More than 20 RCTs of glucocorticoids have been conducted in severe AH defined by MDF >32. Two meta-analyses that included primarily high-quality studies concluded there is a benefit to treatment; a third showed no benefit. A **2008 Cochrane systematic review of 15 trials** concluded there was no benefit from glucocorticoids **except possibly in the group with MDF >32**, primarily because of substantial variability in bias — low-bias trials showed benefit, high-bias trials showed none.
- **STOPAH** (UK, 2011–2014, n = **1103**) — largest trial to date. Inclusion: **MDF >32**, onset of jaundice within **2 months**, bilirubin **>4.7 mg/dL**, AST **<500 U/L**; neither liver biopsy confirmation nor a minimum AST elevation was required. **Excluded serum creatinine >5.7 mg/dL.** Randomized to prednisolone 40 mg daily (equivalent of methylprednisolone 32 mg), PTX 400 mg three times daily, the combination, or placebo. Results in Table 1 above.
  - **Infections were more frequent with prednisolone: 13% vs 7% in those not treated with prednisolone (P = .002).** No difference in development of AKI.
  - The lower-than-expected 28-day placebo mortality adversely affected the power analysis and may explain why prednisolone narrowly missed statistical significance for short-term mortality.
- **Network meta-analysis (22 recent trials, n = 2621):** on **moderate-quality** evidence, glucocorticoids alone or in combination with PTX or NAC reduced **28-day but not 90-day** mortality; on **lower-quality** evidence, **PTX alone** also reduced 28-day but not 90-day mortality.
- **Prednisolone + IV NAC** (NAC given intravenously over the first **5 days**): 1-month mortality **8% vs 24%** with prednisolone alone. Infections at 6 months **19% vs 42% (P = .001)**; hepatorenal syndrome **12% vs 25% (P = .02)**. The authors call these findings important but requiring confirmation.
- **Pentoxifylline:** reported to reduce mortality in severe AH (MDF >32), most of the reduction related to a decrease in hepatorenal syndrome in survivors; subsequent larger trials reached similar conclusions, but **2 meta-analyses of all trials concluded there were no differences in short-term mortality related to PTX**, and combining PTX with glucocorticoids showed **no additive benefit**.
- **Anti-TNF therapy abandoned.** Larger trials of etanercept and of infliximab combined with glucocorticoids led to **higher risk of infections and higher mortality than best supportive care**.
- **Vitamin E and an antioxidant cocktail including NAC** have been tried with little success in severe AH.

**Infection, AKI, and MOF**

- Infection can be **both a precipitating event and a consequence** of a poor outcome in AH. Glucocorticoids may improve short-term survival but the increased risk of infection leads to **no change in survival at 90 days and 1 year**.
- Infections often precede development of AKI and MOF, which carries a high mortality rate.
- **A high index of suspicion is required** to identify bacterial and fungal infections in AH: **fever may not always be present, and an elevated white blood cell count is an unreliable indicator of infection.**
- AKI develops from infection, hemodynamic change (particularly intravascular volume depletion), and nephrotoxic drugs. **Iodinated contrast dye** is a risk factor for AKI in patients with diabetes mellitus, heart failure and other conditions, and is likely to cause similar problems in patients with cirrhosis.

**Nutrition**

- Malnutrition is common in AH and impairs recovery.
- One multicenter study indicated **enteral nutrition was comparable to glucocorticoids in reducing 28-day mortality and more effective in reducing long-term mortality**.
- Another multicenter study reported that adding supplementation by **nasogastric tube to glucocorticoid therapy was no more effective than glucocorticoids alone** — that study was underpowered and had a higher-than-expected rate of NG tube complications.
- **Nutritional intake regardless of study arm was a major determinant of mortality**: those consuming **<21.5 kcal/kg/day** had lower survival. All patients with AH should be encouraged to meet nutritional goals as early as possible.
- Whether NG tubes should be used to provide enteral nutrition is a subject of controversy. **Normal-to-high protein diets are safe and do not increase the risk of encephalopathy in patients with AH.**
- Further studies are needed to define the role of specific micronutrients such as **zinc** and **probiotics** in AH.

**Duration of glucocorticoids and transplantation**

- The appropriate duration of glucocorticoid use is a subject of controversy. **If used, glucocorticoids should be discontinued in patients who fail to improve on the basis of Lille criteria.**
- Patients with a **MELD score >26** should be considered for liver transplantation, which may be lifesaving.
- Some transplant centers have policies requiring a defined period of abstinence, and **all require the willingness of a patient with a history of heavy drinking to remain abstinent after transplantation**.
- Outcomes of LT in AH are **similar to those of other transplanted patients with similar MELD scores**, and **relapse after transplantation appears to be no more frequent than in patients with alcoholic cirrhosis who do not have AH**.
- Abstinence beyond **90 days** is particularly important to emphasize — the period when many patients are regaining normal functioning. A team approach to achieve this goal is optimal.

**Long-term outcome**

- 1-year mortality in STOPAH was **56%**.
- **4-year mortality** in the VA Cooperative Study for AH superimposed on cirrhosis was **65%**, worse than for many common forms of cancers such as colon and breast.
- A potential reason for the high long-term mortality is **return to drinking**.

## Relevance to Wiki

- **`[[alcohol-associated-hepatitis]]`** — primary target. The AGA 2017 CPU supplies the AH-specific content that the tier-1 ALD guidelines cover more thinly: SIRS-on-admission → MOF → death as the organizing pathophysiology; the mandatory blood/urine/ascites culture regardless of fever; fever and WBC as unreliable infection markers; iodinated contrast and diuretics as AKI risks; the 1–1.5 g/kg protein and 30–40 kcal/kg nutritional prescription; explicit management advice for **mild-to-moderate** AH (MELD <20 and MDF <32 → abstinence counseling, high-protein diet with B vitamins and folic acid); and the MELD >26 transplant-referral threshold.

- **`[[liver-transplantation]]`** — MELD >26 referral threshold; LT outcomes in AH equal to other recipients at similar MELD; relapse no more frequent than in alcoholic cirrhosis.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Pentoxifylline — superseded.** BPA 10 offers PTX 400 mg three times daily to patients with a contraindication to glucocorticoids, calling the efficacy data "conflicting." [[aasld-2020-ald]] GS-19 (*"Pentoxifylline is no longer recommended in the treatment of AH"*) and [[acg-2024-ald]] Rec 18 (*recommend against*, strong/moderate) both abandon it. The newer, higher-priority guidelines govern: **do not use pentoxifylline**.
- **Steroid agent and dose expressed differently.** BPA 9 specifies **methylprednisolone 32 mg daily**; AASLD 2020 and ACG 2024 specify **prednisolone 40 mg/day orally**. The update itself states the two are equivalent (STOPAH used prednisolone 40 mg, described as "equivalent of 32 mg methylprednisolone"), so this is a units difference, not a disagreement. The guideline formulation (prednisolone 40 mg) is the one to prescribe.
- **Duration of glucocorticoids.** This update calls 28 days "a subject of controversy" and notes no survival benefit beyond 28 days. AASLD 2020 and ACG 2024 both specify a **28-day** course with day-7 Lille reassessment. The newer guidelines resolve the controversy; the day-7 stopping rule is the operative one.
- **MELD >20 for steroids.** BPA 9 gives *either* MDF >32 *or* MELD >20 as the treatment threshold, as does AASLD 2020. [[acg-2024-ald]] makes **MELD >20** the primary criterion and demotes MDF after the STOPAH post-hoc analysis found MDF inferior (ROC 0.67 vs 0.704–0.726 for MELD/GAHS/ABIC).
- **AH diagnostic criteria are stated two ways inside this one document.** The narrative cites the NIAAA consensus statement — jaundice within **60 days**, **>50 g/day** for ≥6 months — while Table 2 states jaundice within **8 weeks** of the last drinking period and **>40 g/day** for >6 months. AASLD 2020's consensus criteria (jaundice within prior 8 weeks; >40 g/day female or >60 g/day male for ≥6 months with <60 days abstinence) are the newer and operative version.
- **Lille cut-point not printed.** The update instructs stopping glucocorticoids in patients who "fail to improve on the basis of Lille criteria" without stating the number. The **Lille >0.45 = non-response** threshold comes from [[aasld-2020-ald]] and [[acg-2024-ald]].
- **NAC.** Presented here as promising but unconfirmed (single trial plus network meta-analysis). [[acg-2024-ald]] Rec 20 now recommends IV NAC as an adjuvant to corticosteroids (strong, moderate).
- **Transplant referral threshold.** BPA 11's **MELD >26** is specific to this update. AASLD 2020 GS-20 uses **CPT class C or MELD-Na ≥21** for decompensated alcohol-associated cirrhosis, and GS-22 handles severe AH non-responders separately without a MELD number. The two thresholds are answering different questions (whom to refer with AH vs whom to refer with cirrhosis) and are not in direct conflict.
- **Nutritional target.** BPA 5's **30–40 kcal/kg and 1–1.5 g/kg protein** and [[acg-2024-ald]] Key concept 24's **35 kcal/kg/day with 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day protein** are consistent; ACG's figure sits inside this range.
