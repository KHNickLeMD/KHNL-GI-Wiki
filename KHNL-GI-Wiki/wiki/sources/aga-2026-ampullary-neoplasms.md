## Summary

The first AGA Clinical Practice Update dedicated to ampullary neoplasms, covering the whole arc of care for an ampullary adenoma — diagnosis, staging and patient selection, the technical conduct of endoscopic papillectomy, and post-resection surveillance. Ampullary neoplasia is rare (**0.6%–0.8% of gastrointestinal cancers**, prevalence **<0.1%**) but its incidence among young adults has risen over the past 20 years, and ampullary cancer carries a **5-year survival of 20% to 75%** depending on stage at diagnosis. The authors' stated aim is that high-quality endoscopic assessment and complete resection lower recurrence risk and catch patients earlier.

The clinical core is patient selection. **20%–40% of ampullary adenomas already harbor malignancy**, so sampling must be adequate (**at least 6 biopsies**) and performed through a side-viewing duodenoscope, which also lets the endoscopist avoid the pancreatic orifice. Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) then stages the lesion, above all for **intraductal extension**, which is the single measurement that most often moves a patient to surgery.

Technique advice is unusually concrete and in two places runs against reflex practice: **avoid prepapillectomy biliary and pancreatic sphincterotomy** (electrocautery artifact degrades the histopathologic assessment and bleeding obscures the field), and **do not routinely inject submucosally** — injection does not aid resection and is associated with higher rates of incomplete resection, reserved instead for laterally spreading components along the duodenal wall.

Surveillance is anchored on a **3-month first examination with biopsy of the scar even when it looks normal**, then 6 and 12 months and annually for at least 5 years, with an intensified every-2-to-3-month track for large piecemeal resections and positive margins. The update is explicit that **these Best Practice Advice statements carry no formal ratings** of evidence quality or strength, because no systematic review was performed.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Epidemiology and risk

- Ampullary neoplasia represents **0.6%–0.8% of gastrointestinal cancers**; prevalence **<0.1%**. Incidence among **young adults has risen over the past 20 years**.
- Most ampullary tumors are sporadic, but **genetic polyposis syndromes raise the risk about 3-fold**.
- **20%–40% of ampullary adenomas harbor malignancy** — the figure that drives the ≥6-biopsy rule.
- Ampullary cancer **5-year survival 20%–75%**, by stage at diagnosis.
- Early lesions are frequently asymptomatic; as they grow they cause biliary obstruction, cholangitis or pancreatitis. Those symptoms should prompt cross-sectional imaging — **ideally magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)**.

### Selection for endoscopic papillectomy

| Favors endoscopic papillectomy (BPA 4) | Against / requires further consideration |
|---|---|
| Well-defined margins | **Definitive contraindications (BPA 6):** metastatic spread · invasive cancer extending beyond the mucosal layer · **>1 cm intraductal extension in a surgically fit patient** |
| **Size less than 4–5 cm** | **Relative contraindications (BPA 6):** lesions **over 5 cm** · early-stage/T1 cancers · concerns about follow-up adherence · inadequate pancreaticobiliary expertise |
| Nonfriable lesion | Notable weight loss and jaundice; substantial ductal dilation with an abrupt cutoff at the ampulla on cross-sectional imaging |
| Soft to endoscopic probing | Lesions **larger than 4 cm that are friable, ulcerated, or fixed**; signs of dilated ducts, particularly with intraductal extension |
| **Intraductal extension limited to 1 cm or less** | Irregular or nonstructured villi on narrow-band imaging (indicative of adenocarcinoma) — sample those areas |

- **Intraductal extension is the pivotal measurement (BPA 5):** **>1 cm** → suggest consultation for surgical resection; **<1 cm** → amenable to endoscopic treatment with close surveillance. Limited intraductal extension (**≤10 mm**) may still be managed endoscopically in expert centers, whereas **>10 mm generally favors surgery in a surgically fit patient**.
- **The document acknowledges a competing threshold:** the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) permits a **more liberal threshold of up to 20 mm** in selected expert-center cases.
- **Maximum size remains controversial** — ESGE guidelines and some studies support a **4–4.5 cm** threshold; others have suggested lesions up to **one-half the duodenal circumference** may be amenable.
- **Robust evidence-based criteria for when endoscopic papillectomy is appropriate are not yet established** — the decision is individualized on lesion characteristics, clinical status, preference and available expertise.
- The narrow-band imaging appearance of an ampullary adenoma is characterized by **pinecone/leaf-shaped villi**.

### Surveillance schedule

| Track | Trigger | Schedule |
|---|---|---|
| **Default** | No residual adenoma at the 3-month examination | **3 months → 6 months → 12 months → annually for at least 5 years** |
| **Low-risk option** | Adenoma **≤3 cm** resected en bloc or piecemeal with clear margins, and a negative 3-month biopsy | Re-evaluation every **6–12 months for at least 2 years**, then every **3–5 years** |
| **Intensified eradication** | Adenomas **>3 cm** resected piecemeal · positive or indeterminate margins · any residual adenoma on surveillance biopsy | Adjunct thermal ablation and ductography; **repeat every 2–3 months until eradication is confirmed histologically** |
| **Immediate escalation** | High-grade dysplasia (HGD) · incomplete resection · suspected malignancy | Multidisciplinary team (gastroenterology, surgery, oncology, radiology, pathology); restage with EUS and/or MRCP when depth, intraductal extension or operative fitness is uncertain |
| **Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)** | — | Individualized by **ampullary, gastric and duodenal disease burden**, not a fixed interval; **6–12 months** for advanced polyposis, **3–5 years** for minimal disease |

- **Biopsy the resection scar even when it appears endoscopically normal**, plus any abnormal mucosa, at every surveillance examination.
- **Beyond 5 years is individualized** on recurrence risk, comorbidity and life expectancy. Routine surveillance beyond 5 years is **not suggested for average-risk patients after durable negative exams**, but should be extended (annual or biannual) for **piecemeal resection, positive/indeterminate margins, intraductal extension, HGD, or FAP**.
- Most recurrences are identified within the **first 2–3 years**; late recurrence >5 years is reported but rare.
- Where the ampulla is difficult to visualize, **cap-assisted forward-view endoscopy can aid inspection, but a duodenoscope remains preferred** for papillary evaluation.

## Best Practice Advice statements

*All 15, at the document's own numbers. **Ungraded** — the document assigns no evidence quality or strength. Wording condensed where the published statement is long; direction, population and every numeric qualifier unchanged.*

### Diagnosis

1. Assess ampullary adenomas with a **side-viewing duodenoscope** — ampulla visualization and biopsy access with a standard forward-viewing gastroscope is limited, and a duodenoscope allows more careful biopsy **avoiding the pancreatic orifice** to minimize postprocedure pancreatitis risk.
2. Because **20%–40%** of ampullary adenomas harbor malignancy, obtain **at least 6 biopsies** to assess histopathology, **especially from ulcerated or indurated areas**.
3. **EUS** is valuable for staging and for determining the extent (if any) of **intraductal extension**, and should be considered for each patient with an ampullary adenoma potentially amenable to endoscopic resection — **barring lesions smaller than 1 cm with no worrisome clinical or endoscopic features**.

### Staging and assessment

4. Endoscopic characteristics suggesting a benign lesion most amenable to endoscopic papillectomy: **well-defined margins · size less than 4–5 cm · nonfriable · soft to endoscopic probing · intraductal extension limited to 1 cm or less**.
5. For ampullary adenomas with **significant (>1 cm) intraductal extension**, consultation for consideration of **surgical resection** is suggested. Lesions with **minimal (<1 cm) intraductal involvement** may be amenable to endoscopic treatment **with close surveillance**.
6. Three **definitive** contraindications to endoscopic papillectomy: **metastatic spread**, **invasive cancer extending beyond the mucosal layer**, and **>1 cm intraductal extension in a surgically fit patient**. **Relative** contraindications or factors warranting further consideration: lesions **over 5 cm**, **early-stage/T1 cancers**, and concerns about patients with **limited follow-up adherence**.

### Technical aspects

7. Where endoscopic resection is not feasible and biliary obstruction is causing **significant jaundice**, or when **cholangitis** is present, **biliary drainage with or without sphincterotomy and self-expandable metal stent (SEMS) placement** may be considered at the discretion of the surgeon.
8. **Avoid prepapillectomy biliary and pancreatic sphincterotomies** — they risk incomplete histopathologic assessment from electrocautery artifact, and bleeding prior to resection.
9. Perform **en bloc snare resection when feasible**. **Submucosal injection is generally not required** but may be beneficial for **laterally spreading components of the lesion along the duodenal wall**.
10. Use **prophylactic pancreatic duct stenting, rectal nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and high-volume hydration with lactated Ringer's solution** to minimize post-papillectomy pancreatitis.
11. Standard hemostasis techniques — **epinephrine injection, electrocoagulation, hemoclips, argon plasma coagulation (APC), and hemostatic sprays** — may all be carefully employed for bleeding from endoscopic papillectomy.

### Surveillance and follow-up

12. Begin surveillance at **3 months** with a side-viewing duodenoscope and **biopsy of the resection scar and any abnormal area**. If there is no residual adenoma, repeat at **6 months and 12 months, then annually for at least 5 years**. Surveillance beyond 5 years is individualized by recurrence risk, comorbidity and life expectancy. **In FAP, individualize intervals according to ampullary, gastric and duodenal disease burden.**
13. For adenomas **>3 cm resected piecemeal** or those with **positive margins**, consider **adjunct thermal ablation and ductography**, and perform this follow-up **every 2–3 months until eradication is confirmed via biopsy**.
14. Recurrence or cancer **more than 5 years** after resection is reported but rare; the decision to continue endoscopic surveillance beyond 5 years should be individualized on overall health and life expectancy. **If high-risk features are present, long-term endoscopic monitoring is required.**
15. Patients with **high-grade dysplasia, incomplete resection, or suspected malignancy** should be discussed by a **multidisciplinary team including gastroenterologists, surgeons and oncologists**.

## Supporting commentary (condensed)

- **Why no prepapillectomy sphincterotomy** — a preresection cholangiogram and pancreatogram can be useful to exclude intraductal extension and give fluoroscopic guidance for accessing the pancreatic duct post-papillectomy, and may be performed at the endoscopist's discretion; but **sphincterotomy** itself causes cautery artifact that impairs histopathologic assessment and can hinder en bloc resection.
- **Why not inject** — in a prospective multicenter study of 50 patients with ampullary adenomas undergoing snare papillectomy, the **injected group achieved complete resection in 50% versus 80% without injection**. Injection is helpful only for lateral extension along the duodenal wall, where it facilitates resection and reduces perforation risk.
- **Bleeding** — pooled bleeding rate after papillectomy **10.6%**, with endoscopic treatment effective in **96%**. A recent randomized trial found **hemostatic gel more effective than epinephrine injection** for primary hemostasis.
- **Biliary drainage** — SEMS are favored over plastic stents (longer patency, fewer cholangitis events, less reintervention, fewer stent-related hospitalizations). A large national-database analysis found **ERCP associated with lower adverse events, lower hospitalization cost and shorter length of stay than percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage**; EUS-guided biliary drainage is also safe and effective, either after failed ERCP or as primary therapy, and mitigates pancreatitis risk.
- **FAP is a special case** — patients with FAP have **higher adverse event rates** after endoscopic papillectomy and **high recurrence rates**, and removing an ampullary adenoma **does not eliminate or substantially reduce** the recurrence or development of new upper gastrointestinal malignancy. The rate of progression of ampullary adenomas in FAP is **low relative to other patients (11%–14%)**. Because of this, **some experts recommend surveillance endoscopy with biopsies instead of excision** in FAP patients with ampullary adenomas, provided there is no rapid growth or evidence of HGD — accepting the risk of missing unsampled HGD or carcinoma. Duodenal surveillance intensity is set by **Spigelman stage** plus ampullary status, and gastric phenotype (fundic gland polyposis or gastric adenomas) also informs intensity.
- **Intramucosal malignancy on biopsy is unsettled** — spread via lymph nodes and lymphatic channels, and vascular invasion, have been reported in these lesions, and it is difficult to determine whether there is tumor invasion into the sphincter of Oddi. The authors advise **referral for surgical consultation and joint decision-making**.
- **Intraductal radiofrequency ablation** for limited ductal extension achieves **~76% clinical success** at short-term follow-up in observational data and a 2024 systematic review/meta-analysis, but with nontrivial adverse events (**biliary strictures ~22%**) — use in expert centers with prophylactic stenting when indicated. Adjunct **APC** for mucosal remnants on the papillary surface is associated with improved local control in cohort studies.
- **Ductography (ERCP cholangiography/pancreatography)** during surveillance can detect subtle intraductal extension, a major risk factor for persistent/recurrent dysplasia.
- **Multidisciplinary review aligns with international guidance** — French Intergroup 2024 and Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society 2022, which emphasize team review before definitive therapy and whenever histology or imaging raises concern for carcinoma or significant intraductal spread.
- A **5-year structured surveillance program** demonstrated high adherence and diagnostic yield, with most clinically relevant findings emerging in the early years. ESGE recommends at least 5 years of annual surveillance after the initial 3-, 6- and 12-month checks, and a Delphi consensus similarly favored **≥5 years** of follow-up.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Intraductal-extension threshold: AGA 2026 vs ESGE.** AGA sets the surgical-referral threshold at **>1 cm (>10 mm)** intraductal extension; the update itself records that **ESGE permits up to 20 mm** in selected expert-center cases. AGA 2026 is the newer and (for this wiki) higher-priority document, so the page asserts the 1 cm rule and names the ESGE threshold beside it.
- **Maximum lesion size is explicitly unsettled** — the document gives **4–5 cm** in BPA 4, **over 5 cm** as a relative contraindication in BPA 6, and **>4–5 cm** in the Figure 1 contraindication node, while noting ESGE's 4–4.5 cm and a competing one-half-duodenal-circumference proposal. The page states the range rather than a single cut-point, because the document does not resolve it.
- **No evidence ratings at all.** These are ungraded Best Practice Advice statements, drawn from a review of published literature and expert opinion without systematic review. Treat them accordingly, and do not attach grades.
- **"Robust evidence-based criteria... are not yet established"** for when endoscopic papillectomy is appropriate — the document says this in its own words.
- **Management of intramucosal malignancy found on biopsy remains controversial** — the document advises surgical consultation and joint decision-making rather than taking a position.
- **FAP management is deliberately left individualized** — the document presents surveillance-with-biopsy versus excision as a genuine choice made "on an individual patient level," and does not recommend one.
