## Summary

The antimicrobial companion volume of the Tokyo Guidelines 2018 (TG18) family. Its three sibling papers grade severity and set the drainage flowcharts; this one is the **only TG18 paper that names agents, routes, and durations**, which is why both [[acute-cholangitis]] and [[acute-cholecystitis]] previously had to defer all regimen questions to it.

Agents are listed **by antimicrobial class, by TG18 severity grade (I / II / III), and by setting (community-acquired vs healthcare-associated)** in Table 3 — the document's central decision table, carried forward unchanged from TG13 after systematic re-review found no new evidence to modify the agent list. Crucially, the authors repeatedly subordinate the table to **local antibiogram data**: empiric coverage for a resistant organism is advised once that organism exceeds roughly 10–20% of community isolates, and they recommend empiric coverage of resistant isolates occurring in more than 20% of patients. Agents with high resistance prevalence among *Enterobacteriaceae* are separately tabulated (Table 4) as agents that may still be used safely **once susceptibility is proven**.

The genuinely new material in TG18 versus TG13 is **duration** (Table 5), which is systematically reviewed for the first time. The headline figures are short: **4–7 days for acute cholangitis once the source is controlled**, and for Grade I/II acute cholecystitis **no postoperative antibiotics at all** — therapy limited to before and at the time of surgery. Separate, longer rules govern Gram-positive bacteremia (minimum 2 weeks, for endocarditis risk), liver abscess (until radiologic resolution), and residual stones or unrelieved obstruction.

A deletion is also notable: **prophylactic antimicrobial use for elective [[ercp|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]] is no longer recommended, and that section was removed from TG18 entirely.**

**Grading vocabulary (the document's own):** the GRADE system is used, with **strength of recommendation graded 1 (strong) or 2 (weak)** and **quality of evidence graded high (level A), moderate (level B), low (level C), very low (level D)**. The paper labels each boxed statement as "Recommendation *N*, level *X*". It does not number its recommendations sequentially; the Q1–Q4 grouping below is the document's own clinical-question structure.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Named recommendations — all seven, as the document grades them

**Q1. What specimen should be sent for culture to identify the causative organisms in acute cholangitis and cholecystitis?** *(Q1 and Q2 and their answers are endorsed from TG13.)*

| Statement | Grade |
|---|---|
| **Bile cultures** should be obtained **at the beginning of any procedure performed**. **Gallbladder bile should be sent for culture in all cases of acute cholecystitis except those with Grade I severity.** | Recommendation 1, level C |
| Suggest cultures of **bile and tissue** when **perforation, emphysematous changes, or necrosis of the gallbladder** are noted during cholecystectomy. | Recommendation 2, level D |
| **Blood cultures are not routinely recommended for Grade I community-acquired acute cholecystitis.** | Recommendation 2, level D |

**Q2. What considerations should be taken when selecting antimicrobial agents?**

| Statement | Grade |
|---|---|
| Consider **targeted organisms, pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, local antibiogram, history of antimicrobial usage, renal and hepatic function, and a history of allergies and other adverse events.** | Recommendation 1, level D |
| Suggest **anaerobic therapy if a biliary-enteric anastomosis is present.** | Recommendation 2, level C |

**Q3. Optimal duration and route for acute cholangitis?**

| Statement | Grade |
|---|---|
| Once the source of infection is controlled, antimicrobial therapy for acute cholangitis is recommended for **4 to 7 days**. | Recommendation 1, level C |

**Q4. Optimal duration for acute cholecystitis?**

| Statement | Grade |
|---|---|
| **Grade I and II acute cholecystitis: antimicrobial therapy only before and at the time of surgery.** | Recommendation 1, level B |
| **Grade III acute cholecystitis: once the source of infection is controlled, 4 to 7 days.** | Recommendation 2, level D |

**Antimicrobial therapy for special conditions**

| Statement | Grade |
|---|---|
| In **pericholecystic abscess or perforation of the gallbladder**, treat with a Table 3 regimen; **continue until the patient is afebrile, with a normalized white count, and without abdominal findings.** | Recommendation 1, level D |

Footnote rules that change the cell contents:

- **Vancomycin** is recommended to cover *Enterococcus* spp. **for Grade III community-acquired** infection and for healthcare-associated infection. **Linezolid or daptomycin** if vancomycin-resistant *Enterococcus* (VRE) is known to be colonizing the patient, if previous treatment included vancomycin, and/or if VRE is common in the community. **Ampicillin** may be used if the isolate is susceptible.
- **Ampicillin/sulbactam has little activity left against *E. coli*** and was removed from treatment in the North American guidelines. TG18 does not recommend it as empiric therapy where local susceptibility is **<80%**; it is reasonable as **definitive** therapy once susceptibility is known.
- **Anti-anaerobic therapy** (metronidazole, tinidazole, or clindamycin) **is warranted if a biliary-enteric anastomosis is present.** The carbapenems, piperacillin/tazobactam, ampicillin/sulbactam, cefmetazole, cefoxitin, flomoxef and cefoperazone/sulbactam already have sufficient anti-anaerobic activity. **Clindamycin resistance among *Bacteroides* spp. is significant and it is no longer recommended** in other intra-abdominal infections; **cefoxitin is no longer recommended** by SIS-NA/IDSA for the same reason.
- **Fluoroquinolone use is recommended only if the susceptibility of cultured isolates is known**, because resistance among Gram-negative isolates is rising significantly.

### Other decision-relevant content

- **Timing of the first dose: in septic shock, antimicrobials within 1 h of recognition.** In less acutely ill patients, within **6 h of diagnosis** — as long as 6 h may be spent on definitive diagnostics first. **Therapy must be started before any drainage procedure**, percutaneous, endoscopic or operative.
- **30-day all-cause mortality by TG13 severity grade: 2.4% (Grade I), 4.7% (Grade II), 8.4% (Grade III).**
- **Renal function must be estimated before dosing**, using the equation the guideline prints: serum creatinine clearance = (140 − age) × optimum body weight (kg) / 72 × serum creatinine (mg/dL).
- **De-escalation** (called "definitive" or "targeted" therapy) is expected once susceptibilities return; the Table 4 high-resistance agents can then be used safely.
- **Bile penetration is no longer a selection criterion.** As obstruction develops, secretion of antimicrobials into bile stops; well-designed trials comparing agents with and without good biliary penetration are lacking.
- **Oral conversion** is possible in patients who tolerate oral feeding. Table 6 lists the agents with good bioavailability: **amoxicillin/clavulanic acid; cephalexin ± metronidazole; ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin ± metronidazole; moxifloxacin.**
- **Highly resistant organisms:** extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing *E. coli* is highly susceptible to carbapenems and tigecycline; for ESBL-producing *Enterobacteriaceae*, carbapenems, piperacillin/tazobactam and aminoglycosides are recommended. For *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* with ceftazidime resistance **>20%**, use carbapenems, piperacillin/tazobactam or aminoglycosides pending susceptibilities. **Colistin** is the salvage agent for multidrug-resistant Gram-negative epidemic strains — toxic, dosing uncertain, involve infectious-diseases consultation.
- **Microbiology (Table 1, bile cultures):** *E. coli* 31–44%, *Klebsiella* spp. 9–20%, *Enterococcus* spp. 3–34%, anaerobes 4–20%, *Pseudomonas* spp. 0.5–19%, *Enterobacter* spp. 5–9%, *Streptococcus* spp. 2–10%. *Bacteroides fragilis* group: 1.1% of blood cultures, 1.6% of bile cultures.
- **Culture yield:** bile cultures positive in **28–93%** of acute cholangitis and **29–54%** (bile or gallbladder) in acute cholecystitis; blood cultures positive in **21–71%** of cholangitis (15.2% / 21% / 25.7% by TG13 Grade I / II / III) and **7.7–15.8%** in cholecystitis.
- **Grade I cholecystitis is at best prophylaxis.** In early and non-severe cholecystitis it is not obvious that bacteria play a significant role in the pathology; antimicrobial therapy becomes therapeutic only with a systemic inflammatory response.
- **Antibiotic irrigation of the surgical field** may be as effective as systemic agents and the effects may be additive — but the additive effect is lessened if the same agent is used topically and systemically.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **No disagreement found with the ingested TG18 siblings or with [[asge-2021-cholangitis]]** — this paper occupies the regimen space the others explicitly left empty.
- **Agent list is TG13's, unchanged.** The authors state that systematic re-review found "no new significant evidence to modify the list of agents," so Table 3 is 2013 content republished in 2018. Local antibiogram data is repeatedly given precedence over the table.
- **Grade III cholecystitis duration is the weakest statement in the paper** (Recommendation 2, level D). The guideline records that in the consensus meeting a member stated there were insufficient data to support this duration and that it was difficult to recommend.
- **Non-inferiority of no postoperative antibiotics was not formally proven.** The two randomized trials integrated in Figure 1 (Loozen 2017, Regimbeau 2014) gave a risk difference for postoperative infection of **0.01 (95% CI −0.04 to 0.06)**; non-inferiority was not proven in either trial, but no clinically significant difference was seen, and the recommendation rests on that plus the costs of extended therapy.
- **Duration for bacteremic cholangitis is unsettled even among the authors.** Polled at the 6th Asian Pacific Hepatobiliary Pancreatic Surgery Conference (June 2017) on duration for Gram-positive-cocci bacteremic cholangitis after successful drainage, the expert panel split: 14 days 9%, 10 days 3.8%, 7 days 26.9%, 4–5 days 32.1%, ≤3 days 26.9%.
