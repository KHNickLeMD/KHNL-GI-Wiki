## Summary

This ACG guideline provides evidence-based recommendations for nutrition therapy in adult hospitalized patients, particularly those who are critically ill or nutritionally at risk. The guideline emphasizes the importance of the gut as an immune and barrier organ, preferring enteral nutrition (EN) over parenteral nutrition (PN) whenever feasible, with EN associated with reduced infectious complications, preserved gut barrier function, and improved outcomes.

A key practical framework is the stratification of patients by nutritional risk (using NRS-2002 or NUTRIC Score) before deciding on the aggressiveness and timing of nutrition support. In low-risk, well-nourished patients expected to resume oral intake within 5–7 days, artificial nutrition is not needed. In high-risk or malnourished patients, early EN within 24–48 hours is recommended.

The guideline provides guidance on enteral access, volume targets, protein dosing, monitoring for feeding intolerance (away from routine gastric residual volume monitoring), and the appropriate use of supplemental PN. Specific populations are addressed including patients with ARDS, obesity, and end-of-life care.

## Key Findings / Claims

- Malnutrition prevalence in hospitalized patients: up to 30–55% in medical/surgical inpatients; associated with increased complications, length of stay, and mortality
- Nutritional risk scoring: NRS-2002 and NUTRIC Score are preferred validated tools; albumin/prealbumin/transferrin reflect inflammation, not nutritional status, and should not be used as nutrition markers
- Indirect calorimetry is the gold standard for measuring caloric needs; when unavailable, 25–30 kcal/kg/day is commonly used
- Protein requirements: 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day for most hospitalized patients; up to 2.5 g/kg ideal body weight in burns/trauma
- Enteral access: NGT/OGT for initial EN; post-pyloric access (NJT, surgical J-tube) reserved for gastric intolerance or high aspiration risk
- Radiologic confirmation of feeding tube position is required before initiating EN
- Early EN (within 24–48h) in high-risk patients reduces infectious complications; supported by systematic reviews
- Permissive underfeeding (60–70% of caloric target) is acceptable in early ARDS and in obese patients (BMI >30)
- Gastric residual volume (GRV) monitoring: no benefit over clinical assessment; daily abdominal exam preferred
- Prokinetics (metoclopramide, erythromycin): can improve gastric emptying; use when EN intolerance detected
- PN: associated with increased infections if given too early in low-risk patients; reserving PN for the first week in low-risk patients who cannot tolerate EN reduces infection rates
- Supplemental PN (added to EN) should be deferred until EN provides <60% of goal for ≥7–10 days
- End-of-life nutrition: no clinical evidence that artificial nutrition improves quality of life or survival in most EOL situations; patient/family autonomy guides decision-making
- Semi-elemental and elemental formulas: reserved for documented malabsorption; not routinely superior to polymeric formulas

## Recommendations

| Recommendation | Strength | Evidence Quality |
|---|---|---|
| **Indications for Nutrition Support** | | |
| EN is preferred over PN when the gastrointestinal tract is functional | Conditional | Low |
| EN is not indicated in low-risk, well-nourished patients expected to resume oral intake within 5–7 days | Conditional | Very Low |
| PN should be reserved for situations where EN is not feasible, tolerated, or adequate | Conditional | Very Low |
| **Nutritional Assessment** | | |
| Nutritional risk screening using NRS-2002 or NUTRIC Score should be performed on admission for all hospitalized patients | Conditional | Very Low |
| Albumin, prealbumin, and transferrin should not be used as nutritional markers due to acute-phase reactant behavior | Conditional | Very Low |
| Indirect calorimetry is the gold standard for measuring caloric needs; use when available | Conditional | Very Low |
| Protein requirements of 1.2–2.0 g/kg/day should be targeted in most critically ill patients | Conditional | Low |
| Higher protein targets (up to 2.5 g/kg IBW/day) should be considered in burns, trauma, and open abdomen | Conditional | Low |
| **Enteral Access** | | |
| Nasogastric or orogastric tube is the first-line enteral access device | Conditional | Very Low |
| Radiologic confirmation of feeding tube position is required before initiating EN | Strong | Moderate |
| Post-pyloric access (nasojejunal or surgically placed jejunostomy) should be used for patients with gastric intolerance or high aspiration risk | Strong | Moderate–High |
| Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) or jejunostomy should be considered when EN is expected >4 weeks | Conditional | Very Low |
| **Initiation and Advancement of EN** | | |
| EN should be initiated within 24–48 hours of admission in high-risk patients | Conditional | Low |
| EN should be initiated at low rate (10–20 mL/h) and advanced to goal over 48–72h | Conditional | Very Low |
| Trophic or permissive underfeeding (≤500 kcal/day or 10–20 mL/h) is acceptable in the first week of critical illness in ARDS | Strong | Moderate |
| Permissive underfeeding (60–70% caloric target) is acceptable in obese patients (BMI >30) to minimize metabolic complications | Conditional | Very Low |
| **Monitoring and Feeding Intolerance** | | |
| Daily physical exam is the preferred method for monitoring feeding tolerance | Conditional | Low |
| Routine gastric residual volume (GRV) monitoring is not recommended; GRV alone should not determine EN continuation | Conditional | Very Low |
| Prokinetic therapy (metoclopramide or erythromycin) should be used when EN intolerance is detected | Conditional | Low |
| Feeding protocols (nurse-driven titration algorithms) should be implemented to optimize EN delivery | Strong | Moderate–High |
| **Parenteral Nutrition** | | |
| PN should be withheld in the first week if EN is not feasible in low-risk, well-nourished patients | Conditional | Very Low |
| PN should be initiated as soon as possible in high-risk or severely malnourished patients when EN is not feasible | Strong | Moderate |
| Supplemental PN should not be added to EN until EN has provided <60% of caloric goal for ≥7–10 days | Strong | Moderate |
| Hypocaloric PN (≤20 kcal/kg/day) is acceptable in the first week of PN in obese patients | Conditional | Very Low |
| **Formula Selection** | | |
| Polymeric formulas are appropriate for most patients; semi-elemental/elemental formulas are reserved for documented malabsorption | Conditional | Very Low |
| Immune-modulating formulas (arginine, glutamine, omega-3 fatty acids) are not recommended for routine use in ICU | Conditional | Low |
| Fiber-containing formulas may be used in hemodynamically stable patients to improve bowel function | Conditional | Very Low |
| **End-of-Life Nutrition** | | |
| Nutrition decisions at end of life should be guided by patient autonomy and expressed patient preferences | Conditional | Very Low |
| Clinicians are not obligated to initiate or continue artificial nutrition support in patients at end of life when it is not desired | Conditional | Very Low |
| Prognosis and goals of care should guide nutrition decisions; withholding/withdrawing nutrition is ethically equivalent | Conditional | Very Low |

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Optimal caloric target in ICU remains controversial (permissive underfeeding vs. full feeding in late phase)
- GRV threshold for intervention and the decision to stop vs. continue EN during high GRV remains variable in practice
- Role of immune-modulating formulas (glutamine, arginine, omega-3) remains uncertain outside specific settings
