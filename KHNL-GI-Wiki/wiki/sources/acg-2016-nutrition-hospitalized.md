## Summary

American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) guideline on nutrition therapy for the adult hospitalized patient. Target population: the adult hospitalized patient unable to sustain volitional intake, expected to remain in hospital >3 days — ward or intensive care unit (ICU), though most of the evidence derives from ICU management.

Central framework: **nutritional risk** (disease severity + nutritional status), not malnutrition alone, decides how aggressively to feed. High-risk patients gain most from early, adequate enteral nutrition (EN); low-risk, well-nourished patients are unlikely to have their outcome changed by either EN or parenteral nutrition (PN).

Operational thrust: attain enteral access and start EN within 24–48 h in the high-risk patient; feed the stomach first and divert post-pylorically only for demonstrated intolerance or high aspiration risk; abandon gastric residual volume (GRV) as a routine monitor in favour of daily physical exam and percent-of-goal delivery; use nurse-driven feeding protocols; reserve PN for high nutritional risk when EN is infeasible, or after the first week in low-risk patients.

**Grading scheme (Tables 1–3).** Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methods. Strength is **Strong** (desirable effects clearly outweigh undesirable, or clearly do not) or **Conditional** (tradeoffs less certain). Evidence quality is **High / Moderate / Low / Very low**. Recommendations are **numbered 1–38** (sub-lettered a/b/c) and collected in **Table 6**; every recommendation carries both a strength and an evidence level.

## Key Findings / Claims

- Butterworth's "The Skeleton in the Hospital Closet" indicated **nearly 50%** of patients in an urban US hospital setting were malnourished
- Nutritional risk incorporates **both** nutritional status and disease severity; the two validated tools are **NRS-2002** (Nutritional Risk Score 2002) and the **NUTRIC Score**. Component scoring: Table 5, reproduced on [[nutrition-in-hospitalized-patients]]
  - Elective-surgery cohort (>1,000 patients): NRS-2002 **≥5** → nutrition therapy gave a **50% reduction** in nosocomial infection and total complications; NRS-2002 **≤3** → no outcome change regardless of therapy
  - ICU observational cohort: NUTRIC **6–10** = high risk, mortality fell the closer delivery came to goal; NUTRIC **0–5** = low risk, no association
  - Randomized controlled trial (RCT) of 132 patients at NRS-2002 **>3**: aggressive therapy → 30% greater receipt of calories and protein, fewer total complications and re-hospitalizations
- **Albumin, pre-albumin, transferrin are negative acute-phase proteins** — markers of inflammation and risk, not of nutritional adequacy; they normalize only as inflammation abates
- Indirect calorimetry is the most accurate method for caloric needs; when unavailable, a weight-based equation (**25–30 kcal/kg/day**) or a published predictive equation is used. Predictive equations match calorimetry in only **40–70%** of cases
- Protein: **1.2–1.5 g/kg/day** was historically thought sufficient; recent data suggest **1.5–2.0 g/kg/day** to optimize outcome. Needs "may be even higher" in trauma or large wounds — **no number is given for that group**. Nitrogen balance from a 24-h urine urea nitrogen (UUN) collection: protein g/day = [(UUN+4)×6.25]
- Gastric feeding succeeds in the vast majority; gastrointestinal (GI) dysfunction occurs in 30–70% of ICU patients but most still tolerate gastric feeds. Bowel sounds are **not** a sensitive marker of GI function and are not required to start EN
- Small-bowel feeding benefit is confined to sicker patients — RCT in Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II **>20**: shorter length of stay, fewer total complications, more EN delivered; **no difference** at APACHE II **<20**
- Nasoenteric access carries sinusitis in ~11.4–13.0%; the **4-week** cutoff for percutaneous access is acknowledged as arbitrary, based on nares erosion, aspiration pneumonia, sinusitis, esophageal ulceration/stricture
- Permissive underfeeding data: acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)/acute lung injury trial — **trophic 20 mL/h (25% of requirements) for 6 days** was equivalent to full feeds (80% of caloric requirements); obese critically ill (body mass index [BMI] >30) — **2.0–2.5 g protein/kg ideal body weight/day with 60–65%** of estimated or measured energy expenditure; PN in the first week — **80% of caloric requirements with full protein at 1.5–2.0 g/kg/day**
- Routine use of immune-modulating, elemental/semi-elemental, disease-specific (diabetes) and organ-specific (hepatic, renal, pulmonary) formulas is **discouraged**; the arginine + fish-oil immune-modulating formula is the exception, for the post-major-surgery surgical ICU patient
- GRV is a poor marker of gastric volume, emptying, aspiration risk and pneumonia; sensitivity for aspiration **<2–8%**; mandating no-GRV showed no difference in clinical outcome, while GRV use increases tube clogging **tenfold**
- Aspiration risk factors: age **>70**, altered mental status, endotracheal or nasoenteric tube, prolonged supine position, bolus infusion
- Refeeding syndrome risk: **BMI <20 kg/m²**, recent weight loss before admission, or prolonged nil per os; monitor electrolytes and volume for up to **5 days** after starting feeds
- Diarrhea on EN: incidence 2–95%; commonest etiology is **sorbitol-containing medication (55% of cases)**; *Clostridium difficile* accounts for **17–20%** of diarrhea in tube-fed patients
- PN complications drove its historic disadvantage; with moderate glucose control, better line care and avoidance of overfeeding, the outcome benefit of EN over PN **may be narrowing** — a 2,400-patient multi-ICU RCT in England found no difference in infection, organ failure, length of stay or mortality between routes

## Recommendations (Table 6 — verbatim, with the guideline's own numbering)

### A. Indications for nutritional therapy
*Which hospitalized patients should be considered for specialized nutrition therapy and by which route (enteral or parenteral) should it be provided?*

| # | Recommendation | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Specialized nutrition therapy in the form of EN should be initiated promptly in the hospitalized patient who is at high nutritional risk and is unable to maintain volitional oral intake | Conditional | Low |
| 2 | EN should be used preferentially over PN in hospitalized patients who require non-volitional specialized nutrition therapy, and do not have a contraindication to the delivery of luminal nutrients | Conditional | Low |
| 3 | Specialized nutrition therapy (EN or PN) is not required for hospitalized patients who are at low nutritional risk, appear well nourished, and are expected to resume volitional intake within 5 to 7 days following admission | Conditional | Very low |
| 4 | PN should be reserved for the hospitalized patient under specific circumstances, when EN is not feasible or sufficient enough to provide energy and protein goals | Conditional | Very low |

### B. Nutritional assessment
*How should the hospitalized patient be assessed prior to initiation of specialized nutrition therapy, and how are energy and protein requirements determined?*

| # | Recommendation | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Prior to initiation of specialized nutrition therapy (either EN or PN), a determination of nutritional risk should be performed using a validated scoring system such as the NRS-2002 or the NUTRIC Score on all patients admitted to the hospital for whom volitional intake is anticipated to be insufficient | Conditional | Very low |
| 6a | An additional assessment should be performed prior to initiation of nutrition therapy of factors which may impact the design and delivery of the nutrition regimen | Conditional | Very low |
| 6b | Use of "traditional" nutrition indicators (albumin, pre-albumin, transferrin, and anthropometry) should be avoided | Conditional | Very low |
| 6c | Surrogate markers of infection or inflammation should not be used for nutritional assessment | Conditional | Very low |
| 7a | Caloric requirements should be determined and then be used to set the goal for delivery of nutrition therapy | Conditional | Very low |
| 7b | One of three strategies should be used to determine caloric requirements: indirect calorimetry; simple weight-based equations; published predictive equations | Conditional (each) | Very low (each) |
| 8 | Protein requirements should be determined independently of caloric needs, and an ongoing assessment of protein provision should be performed | Conditional | Very low |

### C. Enteral access
*How should enteral access be achieved, and at what level of the GI tract should EN be infused?*

| # | Recommendation | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9a | A nasogastric or orogastric feeding tube should be used as the initial access device for starting EN in a hospitalized patient | Conditional | Very low |
| 9b | Radiologic confirmation of placement in the stomach should be carried out prior to feeding (except with use of electromagnetic transmitter-guided feeding tubes). Repeated periodic radiologic confirmation of correct tube position in the GI tract is **not** required unless there is concern for tube displacement because of nausea/vomiting, regurgitation, coughing, retching, or overt displacement | Conditional | Very low |
| 10a | Conversion to a post-pyloric feeding tube should be carried out **only** when gastric feeding has been shown to be poorly tolerated or the patient is at high risk for aspiration | Strong | Moderate-to-high |
| 10b | Simultaneous aspiration/decompression of the stomach with jejunal feeding may be accomplished by using a dual lumen aspirate/feed nasoenteric tube, a combined percutaneous gastrojejunostomy (GJ) tube, or the use of both gastrostomy and jejunostomy tubes | Conditional | Very low |
| 11 | When long-term enteral access is needed in a patient with [[gastroparesis]] or [[chronic-pancreatitis|chronic pancreatitis]], a jejunostomy tube should be placed | Conditional | Very low |
| 12 | A percutaneous enteral access device should be placed, either via the gastric or the jejunal route, if enteral feeding is anticipated to be required for greater than 4 weeks duration | Conditional | Very low |
| 13 | A percutaneous gastrostomy should be placed preferentially in the gastric antrum in order to facilitate conversion to a GJ tube in the event that the patient is intolerant to gastric feeding | Conditional | Very low |
| 14 | For the patient at high risk for tube displacement, steps should be taken proactively to secure the access device at the time of placement | Conditional | Very low |

### D. Initiating enteral nutrition
*How soon, at what dose, and with which formula should EN be initiated in the hospitalized patient?*

| # | Recommendation | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 | In the patient at high nutritional risk unable to maintain volitional intake, EN should be initiated within 24–48 h of admission to the hospital | Conditional | Low |
| 16a | Although early EN should be initiated within 24–48 h of admission, the timing by which to advance to goal is unclear. When tolerated, feeding should be advanced to goal within 48–72 h | Conditional | Very low |
| 16b | With reduced tolerance, feeding should be advanced with caution to goal by 5 to 7 days | Conditional | Very low |
| 17(i) | Permissive underfeeding (hypocaloric feeding) is acceptable in **acute lung injury / ARDS** | **Strong** | **High** |
| 17(ii) | Permissive underfeeding is acceptable in **obesity with BMI >30** | Conditional | Very low |
| 17(iii) | Permissive underfeeding is acceptable with **placement on PN over the first week** of nutrition therapy | Conditional | Low |
| 18a | A standard polymeric formula or a high-protein standard formula should be used routinely in the hospitalized patient requiring EN | Conditional | Very low |
| 18b | An immune-modulating formula containing arginine and omega-3 fish oil **should be used** for patients who have had major surgery and are in a surgical ICU setting | Conditional | Very low |
| 18c | An immune-modulating formula containing arginine and omega-3 fish oil should **not** be used routinely in patients in a medical ICU | Conditional | Very low |

### E. Monitoring tolerance and adequacy of EN

| # | Recommendation | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 19a | Hospitalized patients on EN should be monitored daily by physical exam | Conditional | Very low |
| 19b | Patients on EN should be monitored for adequacy of provision of EN as a percent of target goal calories, cumulative caloric deficit, and inappropriate cessation of EN | Conditional | Very low |
| 20 | In the patient at high risk for refeeding syndrome, feeding should be ramped up slowly to goal over 3 to 4 days, while carefully monitoring electrolytes and volume status | Conditional | Very low |
| 21a | Enteral feeding protocols should be used in hospitalized patients in need of nutrition therapy | **Strong** | **Moderate-to-high** |
| 21b | A validated protocol should be used, such as a volume-based feeding protocol or a multi-strategy (bundled) top-down protocol | Conditional | Very low |
| 22 | GRV should **not** be used routinely as a monitor in hospitalized patients on EN | Conditional | Very low |
| 23a | Patients on EN should be assessed for risk of aspiration | Conditional | Very low |
| 23b(i) | For patients at high aspiration risk: use a prokinetic agent | Conditional | Low |
| 23b(ii) | For patients at high aspiration risk: divert the level of feeding lower in the GI tract | **Strong** | **Moderate-to-high** |
| 23b(iii) | For patients at high aspiration risk: switch to continuous infusion | Conditional | Very low |
| 23b(iv) | For patients at high aspiration risk: use chlorhexidine mouthwash twice daily | Conditional | Very low |
| 24a | For the patient receiving EN who develops diarrhea, an evaluation should be initiated to identify an etiology and direct management | Conditional | Very low |
| 24b(i) | Diarrhea on EN: use of fermentable soluble fiber as an adjunctive supplement to a standard EN formula | Conditional | Very low |
| 24b(ii) | Diarrhea on EN: switching to a commercial mixed fiber (soluble and insoluble) formula | Conditional | Low |
| 24b(iii) | Diarrhea on EN: initiating a small peptide / medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil formula | Conditional | Very low |

### F. Complications of enteral access

| # | Recommendation | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | The percutaneous enteral access site should be monitored by cleaning daily with mild soap and water and maintaining correct positioning of the external bolster | Conditional | Very low |
| 26a | Prevention of tube clogging is important and may be achieved by frequent water flushes delivered every shift and each time medications are given | Conditional | Very low |
| 26b | When a clogged tube is encountered and water flushes are unsuccessful, a de-clogging solution comprising a nonenteric-coated pancreatic enzyme tablet dissolved in a sodium bicarbonate solution should be used | Conditional | Very low |
| 26c | If still unsuccessful, a mechanical de-clogging device should be considered prior to exchanging the tube for a new one | Conditional | Very low |
| 27a | A patient who inadvertently dislodges a **recently placed** percutaneous gastrostomy tube (<7–10-day old) should be brought back immediately to the endoscopy or radiology suite and a new tube placed endoscopically or radiologically through the same site on the abdominal wall | Conditional | Very low |
| 27b | If a percutaneous gastrostomy tube in place long enough for a partially formed tract to develop (**>7–10 days**) becomes dislodged, a tube of similar diameter should be placed blindly as expeditiously as possible to maintain patency and prevent closure of the tube tract; radiologic confirmation should be carried out prior to feeding if there is any question of inappropriate location | Conditional | Very low |
| 28a | For a patient with deterioration, breakdown, increased drainage/leakage, or enlarging stoma around the percutaneous tube site, an evaluation should be performed to determine etiology and appropriate management | Conditional | Very low |
| 28b | Placement of a larger tube should **not** be used to manage leakage caused by an enlarging stoma around the percutaneous access device | Conditional | Very low |
| 29 | A percutaneous enteral access device that shows signs of fungal colonization with material deterioration and compromised structural integrity should be replaced in a non-urgent but timely manner | Conditional | Very low |

### G. Parenteral nutrition
*When and how should PN be utilized in the hospitalized patient?*

| # | Recommendation | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30a | If early EN is not feasible and the patient is at **low** nutritional risk upon admission, no specialized nutrition therapy should be provided and PN should be withheld for the first week of hospitalization | Conditional | Very low |
| 30b | If a patient is at **high** nutritional risk on admission and EN is not feasible, PN should be initiated as soon as possible | **Strong** | **Moderate** |
| 31 | Supplemental PN should be considered for the patient already on enteral tube feeding **only after 7 to 10 days**, when unable to meet greater than 60% of energy and protein requirements by the enteral route alone. Initiating supplemental PN prior to this 7–10-day period in those patients already receiving EN does not improve outcomes and may be detrimental | **Strong** | **Moderate** |
| 32 | In hospitalized patients receiving PN, mild permissive underfeeding (delivery **80%** of energy requirements with full protein provision) should be considered initially for the first 7 to 10 days. Following this first week (if long-term PN is required), energy provision should be increased to meet energy goals | Conditional | Low |
| 33 | Peripheral PN should not be used, as it leads to inappropriate use of PN, has a high risk of phlebitis and loss of venous access sites, and generally provides inadequate nutrition therapy | Conditional | Very low |
| 34a | Careful transition feeding should be used in the patient on PN, for whom EN is now being initiated. As tolerance to EN improves and volume of delivery increases, PN should be tapered to avoid overfeeding | Conditional | Very low |
| 34b | PN should be stopped when the EN provides **>60%** of energy and protein goals | Conditional | Very low |

### H. Nutritional therapy at end-of-life

| # | Recommendation | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 35a | The decision to place a gastrostomy tube in an end-of-life situation should be determined by patient autonomy and the wishes of that patient and their family, even though the nutrition therapy may do little to change traditional clinical outcomes | Conditional | Very low |
| 35b | Regardless of prognosis, placement of a gastrostomy device should be based on whether achieving enteral access and initiating EN successfully meet the goals of the patient and/or their family. Percutaneous gastrostomy placement should be considered even if the only benefit is to provide improvement in the quality of life for the family, increased ease of providing nutrition, hydration, and medications, or to facilitate transfer out of the hospital setting to a facility closer to home | Conditional | Very low |
| 36 | The clinician is **not obligated** to provide hydration and nutrition therapy in end-of-life situations. The decision to initiate nutrition therapy is no different than the decision to stop therapy once it has started (thus, clinicians are not obligated to provide therapy that is unwarranted) | Conditional | Very low |
| 37a | If requested, nutrition therapy in end-stage malignancy should be provided by the enteral route | Conditional | Very low |
| 37b | Use of PN in this setting may cause net harm and should be highly or aggressively discouraged | Conditional | Very low |
| 38 | The clinician who has ethical concerns of his own in a difficult end-of-life situation should excuse himself from the case, as long as he can transfer care to an equally qualified and willing health-care provider | Conditional | Very low |

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Optimal caloric target in the ICU is unresolved** — permissive underfeeding vs full feeding; the guideline grades ARDS trophic feeding Strong/High but the obesity and PN scenarios only Conditional
- **Immune-modulating formulas cut both ways within the same guideline**: recommended for the post-major-surgery surgical ICU patient (Rec 18b) but not for the medical ICU (Rec 18c); no recommendation is made for non-arginine anti-inflammatory lipid formulas in ARDS/acute lung injury because the seven RCTs conflict
- **Protein in trauma and large wounds** — the guideline states needs "may be even higher" but **gives no target**. The 2.0–2.5 g/kg ideal body weight/day figure applies to the obese hypocaloric regimen only
- **The 4-week cutoff for percutaneous access is explicitly called arbitrary** by the guideline
- **EN-vs-PN advantage may be narrowing** — the 2,400-patient English multi-ICU RCT found no outcome difference between routes, which sits uneasily with the guideline's preferential EN stance
