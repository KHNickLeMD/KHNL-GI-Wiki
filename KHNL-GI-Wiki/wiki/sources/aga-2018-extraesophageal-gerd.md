## Summary

An AGA Clinical Practice Update arguing that reflux causation of extraesophageal symptoms has been **overestimated**, and that the pendulum has swung away from aggressive empiric anti-reflux therapy. The extraesophageal attribution is strongest for asthma, cough, and hoarseness; weaker (association only) for sore throat, sinusitis, ear pain, and pulmonary fibrosis. Symptoms are usually **multifactorial or not esophageal in origin**, so non-GI evaluation (ENT, pulmonary, allergy) is positioned as an early — often initial — step, and the gastroenterologist's job is narrowed to assessing whether a gastroesophageal etiology contributes.

Two mechanisms are set out: direct caustic contact of refluxate with pharynx/larynx/bronchial tree, and neurogenic stimulation/hypersensitivity (esophageal distention activating neurons innervating bronchial constriction), the latter not requiring proximal reflux at all.

Every diagnostic test is reviewed and found wanting. Laryngoscopic signs occur in up to 86% of asymptomatic subjects with poor intra- and interobserver reproducibility; endoscopy has low yield (silent esophagitis in up to 20% of asymptomatic subjects) and does not predict antisecretory response; distal pH monitoring is abnormal in only 30–50% of suspected LPR and reflux-related asthma and does not predict PPI response; pharyngeal/airway pH measurement "cannot be recommended," since >90% of pharyngeal pH drops between 4 and 5 are swallow-related; salivary pepsin and mucosal impedance remain unestablished. Testing's **main role is to document the absence of GERD**.

Therapeutically, high-quality data are lacking. Placebo-controlled trials of PPI in poorly controlled asthma and in suspected LPR were negative; meta-analyses in chronic cough found insufficient evidence. The update's practical position: a short empiric course of PPI (BID, 6–8 weeks) focused on extraesophageal symptom response, with responders tapered to the lowest effective dose, non-responders taken to objective testing rather than more PPI, and surgery strongly discouraged unless a mechanical defect plus objectively significant off-PPI reflux is present.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Best Practice Advice statements (verbatim, as numbered by the document)

**Best Practice Advice 1:** The role of a gastroenterologist in patients referred for evaluation of suspected extra esophageal symptom is to assess for gastroesophageal etiologies that could contribute to the presenting symptoms.

**Best Practice Advice 2:** Non-GI evaluations by ENT, pulmonary and/or allergy are essential and often should be performed initially in most patients as the cause of the extraesophageal symptom is commonly multifactorial or not esophageal in origin.

**Best Practice Advice 3:** Empiric therapy with aggressive acid suppression for 6-8 weeks with special focus on response of the extraesophageal symptoms can help in assessing association between reflux and extraesophageal symptoms.

**Best Practice Advice 4:** No single testing methodology exists to definitively identify reflux as the etiology for the suspected extra esophageal symptoms.

**Best Practice Advice 5:** Constellation of patient presentation, diagnostic test results and response to therapy should be employed in the determination of reflux as a possible etiology in extra esophageal symptoms.

**Best Practice Advice 6:** Testing may need to be off or on proton pump inhibitor (PPI) depending on patients' presenting demographics and symptoms in assessing the likelihood of abnormal gastroesophageal reflux.
- **A.** On therapy testing may be considered in those with high probability of baseline reflux (those with previous esophagitis, Barrett's esophagus or abnormal pH).
- **B.** Off therapy testing may be considered in those with low probability of baseline reflux with the goal of identifying moderate to severe reflux at baseline.

**Best Practice Advice 7:** Lack of response to aggressive acid suppressive therapy combined with normal pH testing off therapy or impedance-pH testing on therapy significantly reduces the likelihood that reflux is a contributing etiology in presenting extraesophageal symptoms.

**Best Practice Advice 8:** Surgical fundoplication is discouraged in those with extra esophageal reflux symptoms unresponsive to aggressive PPI therapy.

**Best Practice Advice 9:** Fundoplication should only be considered in those with a mechanical defect (e.g., hiatal hernia), moderate to severe reflux at baseline off PPI therapy who have continued reflux despite PPI therapy and have failed more conservative non-GI treatments.

### Test performance data

- **Laryngoscopy** — laryngeal signs present in up to **86% of asymptomatic subjects**; intra- and interobserver reproducibility poor. Reflux Finding Score validity questioned: in 77 children, RFS did not predict abnormal gastroesophageal reflux. Other causes of laryngeal symptoms (vocal fold paresis, benign mucosal lesions, muscle tension dysphonia) are underdiagnosed in this population.
- **EGD** — low diagnostic yield. Esophagitis in **40% of patients with asthma** and **20% with primary extraesophageal symptoms**, but also in up to **20% of asymptomatic subjects** (silent esophagitis). Only a minority of patients with erosive esophagitis have LPR symptoms. Presence of esophagitis does not predict response to antisecretory therapy.
- **Distal ambulatory pH monitoring** — abnormal distal acid exposure does **not** predict response of extraesophageal symptoms to PPIs, though it is demonstrable in **30%–50% of patients with suspected LPR** and reflux-related asthma. Symptom-association analysis is unreliable because laryngeal symptoms lack sudden onset; even for cough, up to **90% of cough events** were not adequately reported with the patient event marker (vs acoustic/manometric detection).
- **Proximal/pharyngeal pH** — >**90% of pharyngeal pH drops between 4 and 5** in healthy subjects were swallow-related, only a minority true reflux. Pathologic pharyngeal acid events occur even after total gastrectomy. International consensus quoted: "there is no clear evidence that dual probe pH monitoring is of additional value above distal pH probe measurement alone," and "measurement of airway and pharyngeal pH cannot be recommended to diagnose gastro-esophageal reflux episodes extending to the pharynx."
- **pH-impedance** — best tool to characterize reflux (acidic, weakly acidic, weakly alkaline) and proximal extent. In PPI-refractory patients: **30%–40%** of symptoms associated with nonacid reflux, ~**10%** with acid reflux, and **50%–60%** not associated with any reflux. About **40%** of patients with suspected extraesophageal manifestations have an abnormal pH-impedance study. In laryngeal symptoms including globus, the added value of impedance over pH alone is limited; after antireflux surgery for extraesophageal manifestations, only heartburn and abnormal pH parameters predicted favorable outcome — impedance parameters had no value.
- **Combined esophageal and pharyngeal pH-impedance** — swallows and air trapping create many pharyngeal artifacts, poor interobserver reproducibility; in PPI-refractory extraesophageal reflux, pharyngeal and esophageal reflux patterns were identical to healthy subjects both on and off PPI, so the technique cannot detect abnormal pharyngeal reflux.
- **Salivary pepsin** — sensitivity **78.6%**, specificity **64.9%** for diagnosis of reflux-related symptoms; specificity rises to **98.2%** when a higher pepsin concentration is required. Another study: PPV **81%**, NPV **78%** for GERD. One-third of control subjects had a positive pepsin sample. In 50 children, 42% were positive with no significant relationship to extraesophageal symptoms or pH-impedance parameters. Role remains to be established.
- **Mucosal impedance** — catheter/balloon-based measurement of epithelial integrity during endoscopy; ~1 minute added to the exam, 5 seconds per measurement. Distinguishes GERD from non-GERD and eosinophilic esophagitis from GERD without biopsies; role in extraesophageal reflux requires future study.

### Off vs on therapy testing

- **"Off" therapy** documents baseline reflux; a negative pH or pH-impedance study off therapy in a PPI non-responder significantly reduces the likelihood that reflux is contributing. Preferred in patients **without previously documented or suspected GERD**.
- **"On" therapy** gives information about reflux control and temporal association with persisting symptoms. Better option in patients with **pathologic reflux — previous abnormal pH testing, significant esophagitis, large hiatal hernia, or Barrett's esophagus**.
- No consensus exists on the definition of an abnormal pH-impedance study performed **on** therapy (ie, high number of reflux events, low baseline impedance value, positive symptom association analysis).
- Even when pathologic reflux is demonstrated, no single testing methodology can definitively identify reflux as the cause.

### Treatment evidence

- **Lifestyle** — high-quality data lacking; systematic reviews found weak or absent evidence that cessation of tobacco, alcohol, chocolate, caffeine/coffee, citrus, mint, or spicy food improves symptoms or physiologic reflux, and no association of carbonated beverages with GERD. Weight control is supported: weight gain increases GERD symptoms/complications, weight loss reduces reflux symptoms; esophageal acid exposure rose with BMI and plateaued in the obese. In one study, lifestyle modification alone was **less effective** than PPI therapy in extraesophageal reflux. The update's prudent position: head-of-bed elevation only in patients with concomitant regurgitation or nocturnal heartburn; avoid trigger foods only if the patient reports symptom worsening after certain food intake; weight loss program in overweight/obese patients with concomitant heartburn and regurgitation.
- **H2RAs** — heal esophagitis in about **50%** in standard divided doses; most useful for intermittent or mild reflux disease. Role in extraesophageal reflux less well established for maintaining nocturnal gastric pH >4. Pre-PPI RCTs in asthma showed conflicting results — some subjective improvement and a trend toward improved pulmonary function with a minimum of 4 weeks of treatment.
- **PPI in asthma** — American Lung Association Asthma Clinical Research Center randomized **412 patients** with poor asthma control to esomeprazole **40 mg twice daily** or placebo; **no benefit at 24 weeks**. A pediatric RCT of lansoprazole in asthma without overt GERD showed no benefit. A controlled trial did suggest benefit in the subgroup with **both nocturnal respiratory and typical reflux symptoms**.
- **PPI in LPR** — open-label response ranges **47% to 90%**. One RCT of rabeprazole **20 mg twice daily** improved symptom index score without a difference in laryngeal signs. The largest study — **145 patients**, esomeprazole **40 mg twice daily for 4 months** — showed **no benefit vs placebo**. Three meta-analyses suggest no overall benefit, with a possible benefiting subgroup.
- **PPI in chronic cough** — one open-label study reported 79% improvement; a meta-analysis of 5 placebo-controlled studies found insufficient evidence; 2 further RCTs found no benefit vs placebo in adults. Recent expert panel guidelines have deemphasized acid suppression in unexplained chronic cough. Benefit may exist in those with chronic cough **and concomitant objective reflux**.
- **Prokinetics** — 4 RCTs of itopride, domperidone, or tegaserod added to PPI: 3 of 4 showed symptom reduction but no change in laryngeal findings in most; a meta-analysis concluded the current body of evidence is inadequate to make a recommendation.
- **Baclofen** — GABA-B agonist, reduces transient LES relaxations. Case series of 3 with cough: improvement/resolution in all 3. **20 mg three times daily** as an 8-week add-on to omeprazole improved cough in **56%** of patients who responded with a reduction in acid reflux episodes. No controlled studies of benefit in extraesophageal reflux.
- **Alginates** — form a pH-neutral raft near the gastroesophageal junction. In typical GERD, reduced severity and frequency of heartburn and regurgitation in patients with continued symptoms despite once-daily PPI. In LPR, liquid alginate suspension significantly improved symptoms at 2, 4, and 6 months and laryngeal signs at 6 months vs placebo. Role in asthma not assessed.
- **Duration/dose position** — a short course of PPI (likely twice daily for **1–2 months**) is sufficient to address the question of reflux association; likely responders have concomitant typical GERD symptoms or pathophysiologic reflux. Data on twice-daily vs once-daily in this group are limited and based on open-label trials, but twice-daily PPI ensures near-complete esophageal reflux control in **95%–99%** of patients. If twice-daily PPI does not help and there is no regurgitation to suggest increased nonacid reflux, GERD can be excluded and other etiologies sought. Among responders, **taper to once daily and then to the minimum effective dose**.
- **Surgery** — no randomized trials compare surgical antireflux therapy with PPI in this population. One study of Nissen fundoplication vs H2RAs in GERD + asthma showed 75% improvement at 2 years surgically vs 9% with H2RAs. Most surgical data in chronic cough, LPR, and asthma are uncontrolled, retrospective, small. Patients with extraesophageal syndrome are **less likely** than those with esophageal syndrome to improve after fundoplication, and the **best predictor of response in both groups is prior symptom response to PPI therapy**. In laryngeal symptoms refractory to PPI, a concurrent controlled study showed no benefit of surgery at 1 year — only **10%** (surgery) and **7%** (continued medical therapy) improved. Fundoplication might be beneficial in those with known response of extraesophageal symptoms to PPI who are (1) unwilling to take PPIs chronically, (2) have side effects with PPI therapy, or (3) have continued regurgitation with a mechanical defect (hiatal hernia) despite PPI therapy.
- **Endoscopic antireflux procedures** — not discussed, given limited available data in those with extraesophageal reflux.

### Figure 1 — recommended algorithm

Suspected extraesophageal reflux **with** typical GERD symptoms → PPI twice daily × 6–8 weeks. **Without** typical GERD symptoms → ENT, allergy, pulmonary evaluation first; if negative, then the PPI trial. After the trial: symptoms improve → titrate to the lowest dose of acid suppression. Symptoms persist → split by pretest probability — high suspicion of GERD / BMI >25 (and, where there is asthma/lung disease and/or concomitant heartburn/regurgitation) → MII/pH monitoring **on** therapy; low suspicion of GERD / BMI <25 → pH testing **off** therapy and look for other diagnoses.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Superseded by [[aga-2023-extraesophageal-gerd|AGA 2023]] where they differ.** 2018 makes an empiric twice-daily PPI trial (6–8 weeks) the routine first step in patients with typical GERD symptoms and treats the response as diagnostically informative; 2023 starts at a **single** daily dose titrated up to twice daily, allows **up to 12 weeks** before declaring failure, advises considering objective testing **before** any PPI when typical GERD symptoms are absent, and states that symptom improvement on PPI must **not** be regarded as confirmation of GERD. The 2023 positions govern the wiki page.
- 2018 favors **on**-therapy testing when baseline reflux probability is high; 2023 defaults to **off**-therapy monitoring unless previous objective evidence of GERD already exists. Both reach the same destination — do not repeat an off-therapy study in someone already proven to have GERD — but the default differs.
- 2018 leaves salivary pepsin and mucosal impedance as unresolved ("role remains to be established"); 2023 and [[acg-2021-gerd|ACG 2021]] are firmer that salivary pepsin and oropharyngeal pH testing are not recommended.
- 2018 does not define "moderate to severe reflux at baseline" (BPA 9) numerically, and gives no acid-exposure-time cutoff; the operative thresholds come from the reflux-monitoring literature on [[ambulatory-reflux-monitoring]].
- The BMI >25 / <25 split in Figure 1 is presented as a pretest-probability aid only; no outcome data are given for it, and it does not appear in the Best Practice Advice statements.
