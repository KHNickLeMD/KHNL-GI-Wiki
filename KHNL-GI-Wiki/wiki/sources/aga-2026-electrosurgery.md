## Summary

An AGA Institute Clinical Practice Update reviewing the physics and practical operation of electrosurgery across therapeutic GI endoscopy, framed as 13 Best Practice Advice statements. The core argument is that **generator settings are only one of several determinants of tissue effect** — device geometry, operator technique, tissue characteristics, and the operational environment (gas vs fluid) act through the same final common pathway of **current density**, so dialling the electrosurgical unit (ESU) alone will not reliably produce the desired effect.

The physics section defines the terms (current, resistance, voltage, power, Ohm's law), distinguishes **monopolar** (current runs device → tissue → patient → dispersive pad → generator) from **bipolar** (current confined between two electrodes on the instrument; no pad needed), and explains the cut–coagulation continuum through **duty cycle** (fraction of time energy is actively delivered; higher = more cut) and **crest factor** (peak-to-average voltage; higher voltage spikes push current through high impedance → deeper effect). Tissue effect follows temperature: **>100°C** vaporizes intracellular fluid and ruptures membranes (cutting); **<100°C** denatures protein → desiccation (coagulation). Circuit resistance is *not* constant — it varies by GI segment (lower in esophagus, higher in colon), by wall layer (submucosa < mucosa < muscularis propria), and rises during the procedure as tissue desiccates. Older analog ESUs held power output fixed and therefore let voltage and tissue effect fluctuate; **microprocessor-controlled generators adjust output to impedance**, holding voltage constant for a more consistent effect.

The applications section covers polypectomy (cut- vs coagulation-predominant current are equivalent for serious adverse events, complete resection, and recurrence; hot snare strongly preferred for large pedunculated polyps because of the stalk vessel), post-EMR margin thermal ablation with snare-tip soft coagulation (STSC), the underwater environment (reduced contact impedance → energy dissipation and a predominantly coagulation effect, so higher power is needed), the deep-injury risk of soft coagulation, hot forceps avulsion for non-lifting fibrosis, argon plasma coagulation as non-contact monopolar delivery, ERCP sphincterotomy, and third-space endoscopy (ESD/POEM).

Safety is treated as a team process: a **pre-procedure timeout dedicated to electrosurgery**, deliberate ESU placement in the room for visibility and operation, and closed-loop communication before activation and during troubleshooting. Two hardware issues get separate treatment — dispersive pad placement (as close to the treatment site as possible, over muscle with good blood supply, avoiding scars/bone/hair/tattoos/implants; dual pads for large patients or high power) and implantable cardiac devices (identify manufacturer, model, location, and device dependence; route current away from device and leads; prefer multipolar electrocautery when feasible).

## Key Findings / Claims

### Best Practice Advice statements (verbatim, all 13)

1. **BPA 1:** "Endoscopists should have a thorough understanding of fundamental electrosurgical principles."
2. **BPA 2:** "Endoscopists should have knowledge of the specific electrosurgical unit that they are using and the related electrosurgical settings."
3. **BPA 3:** "Endoscopists should understand the concept of current density and that multiple factors influence tissue effect besides electrosurgical unit settings, and therefore, changing the electrosurgical unit settings alone may not achieve the optimal desired tissue effect."
4. **BPA 4:** "The endoscopist should adopt best safety practices when utilizing electrosurgery, which includes incorporating a pre-procedure timeout to review electrosurgery safety, optimal positioning of the electrosurgical unit in the room for both visibility and operation, and the use of closed-loop communication among team members prior to electrosurgical unit activation and troubleshooting."
5. **BPA 5:** "Either a cut- or coagulation-predominant electrosurgical current can be considered for the resection of colorectal polyps without clear differences in the risk of serious adverse events, complete resection rate, or polyp recurrence."
6. **BPA 6:** "Hot instead of cold snare polypectomy should be strongly considered for the removal of large pedunculated polyps as these lesions can often contain thick blood vessels prone to bleeding when transected."
7. **BPA 7:** "Following piecemeal endoscopic mucosal resection of colorectal polyps, thermal ablation of the resection margins should be considered to reduce the risk of recurrence."
8. **BPA 8:** "Endoscopists should recognize that the operational environment (gas vs fluid) affects the tissue effect."
9. **BPA 9:** "Endoscopists should be aware that, although the application of soft coagulation current is an effective hemostatic and ablative technique, it can also cause deep tissue injury and the potential for delayed perforation."
10. **BPA 10:** "Hot forceps avulsion using a cutting current can be an effective adjunct technique for removing focal areas of gastrointestinal lesions complicated by non-lifting fibrosis."
11. **BPA 11:** "The endoscopist should understand the principles of argon plasma coagulation as a non-contact method of delivering electrosurgical energy and how gas flow and power parameters can be adjusted to achieve the desired tissue effect."
12. **BPA 12:** "Endoscopists should understand how to optimally deliver electrosurgical energy to achieve the desired sphincterotomy at the time of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography while minimizing the risk of immediate/delayed bleeding and pancreatitis."
13. **BPA 13:** "Endoscopists performing third-space endoscopy should have in-depth understanding of how to optimize the desired tissue effects while minimizing any off-target tissue damage."

### Terminology and basic principles (Table 1, verbatim definitions)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Current (I) | "Defined as the flow of electrons through a circuit. Measured in amperes (A)." |
| Resistance (R) | "Describes the ease by which electrons flow through the tissue. Measured in ohms (Ω)." |
| Voltage (V) | "Defined as the force that pushes current through the circuit. Measured in volts." |
| Power (P) | "Defined as the energy transferred in a circuit per unit of time. Measured in watts (W). Power = V × I or V²/R" |
| Ohm's law | "Current is directly proportional to voltage and inversely proportional to resistance (I = V/R)." |

### Factors influencing current density and tissue effect (Table 3)

| Variable | Effect |
|---|---|
| ESU settings and waveforms | Work with the ESU manufacturer; preset choices may not suit all circumstances |
| Duration of ESU activation | Aim for the shortest activation time that achieves the desired effect |
| Pressure/tension of electrode on tissue | More pressure → **lower** current density (less cutting, more coagulation) |
| Speed of electrode movement | Slower movement (e.g. slow snare closure) → more coagulation, less cutting |
| Material/structure of electrode | Monofilament → more cutting; braided → more coagulation |
| Surface area of electrode | Thin electrode → more cutting; thicker → more coagulation |
| Coagulum/eschar on electrode | Increases resistance → lower current density, less precision; sparking and flaming of eschar can occur |
| Patient variables | Increasing age, increasing body weight, and decreased hydration all increase tissue impedance |
| Distance of electrode from return electrode (monopolar) | Return electrode should be as close to the active electrode as possible — for GI, the patient's flank |

### Physics and generator claims

- Current density **D = intensity (amperes) / area (cm²)**; current density is **inversely proportional to surface area of contact**.
- Thermal effect by temperature: **>100°C** → instantaneous vaporization of intracellular fluid, membrane rupture → **cutting**; **<100°C** → protein denaturation → desiccation → **coagulation**.
- **Duty cycle** = fraction of time energy is actively delivered per waveform; **higher duty cycle → more cutting, less coagulation**.
- **Crest factor** = peak voltage relative to average voltage; higher voltage spikes force current through high impedance → **deeper tissue effect**.
- ESU pedals: **yellow left pedal = "cut"; blue right pedal = "coagulation."** Cutting wants **high** current density; coagulation wants **lower** current density.
- Tissue resistance varies by segment (**lower in the esophagus vs higher in the colon**) and by wall layer (**least → highest: submucosa, mucosa, muscularis propria**), and rises intraprocedurally with desiccation.
- Analog ESUs hold selected power output constant regardless of tissue change → voltage fluctuation, variable tissue effect, higher collateral thermal injury risk. **Microprocessor-controlled ESUs adjust output to impedance**, maintaining constant voltage and a more consistent, precise effect.
- Larger electrode surface area → lower current density and slower heating; helps **coapt vessels** and prevent **heat sink**, but raises the risk of adjacent thermal injury because heat generated is proportional to power dissipated by the tissue.

### Safety

- **Dispersive pad:** place as close as possible to the treatment site (shortest circuit); over **muscular areas with good blood supply** (thigh, flank, upper arm); away from **scars, bony protuberances, excessive hair, tattoos, implants**. Pad size chosen by patient size/weight; **dual-pad system** for larger patients or high-power settings.
- **Implantable cardiac devices** (pacemakers, ICDs) may respond to ESU signals with **pacing inhibition, mode switching, device inactivation, or inappropriate shocks**. Mandatory preoperative assessment: **manufacturer, model, location of device, and whether the patient is device-dependent**. There is **no GI societal consensus**; general recommendations are to place the pad so current avoids the device and leads and to **preferentially use multipolar electrocautery when feasible**.
- Most ESU-associated adverse events are **operator- or device-related**; ESU malfunction itself is uncommon.

### Application-specific claims

- **Polypectomy current (BPA 5):** Two older retrospective studies suggested cut-predominant current → more immediate bleeding, coagulation current → more delayed bleeding. The subsequent RCT (blended cut = EndoCut Q effect 2 duration 1 interval 4, ERBE vs coagulation = forced coagulation effect 2, 25 W, ERBE) found **intraprocedural bleeding more common with cut (17% vs 11%; P = .006)** but **no difference in severe adverse events (7.2% vs 7.9%; P = .76) or delayed bleeding (5.0% vs 5.7%; P = .84)**. Caveat: performed on a newer microprocessor-controlled ESU, so may not generalize to older generators.
- **Pedunculated polyps (BPA 6):** coagulation-predominant waveform may be preferable to seal vessels across the thick stalk; the snare should be positioned **across the mid-stalk and not too close to the base**, to avoid transmitting excessive thermal injury to the colonic wall.
- **Post-EMR margin ablation (BPA 7):** high-quality trials over the past 5 years show thermal ablation of the **normal-appearing** post-EMR margin reduces recurrence to **<5% at first follow-up**. STSC and APC were **equally effective in a randomized trial**, but STSC **may be more cost-effective in most practice environments**. Ablate only normal-appearing margin; any **visible residual neoplasia must be resected** (additional cold snaring or adjunct technique). Role at other GI sites (e.g. upper GI) not well established, though preliminary duodenal data suggest safety and reduced recurrence.
- **Underwater environment (BPA 8):** immersion in conductive fluid **significantly reduces contact impedance → energy dissipation**; the profound drop in peak voltage yields a **predominantly coagulation effect**, because relatively high voltage is needed to generate the spark for cutting under water/saline. Designated settings — **usually higher power output** — are typically needed. **Saline is preferred over water** to mitigate **water intoxication syndrome** (dilutional hyponatremia) during prolonged procedures or large resections.
- **Soft coagulation (BPA 9):** continuous, **low-voltage (<200 Vp)** sinusoidal waveform that **does not produce sparks**; suited to coagulating large vessels with reduced risk of inadvertent cutting. Effect is contact-based, localized, and superficial *at first*, but coagulum and rising impedance cause uneven current distribution, prompting the ESU to maintain or increase power — so **prolonged application or grasping excessive tissue can produce deep thermal injury and delayed perforation despite the low voltage**. Mitigations: treat only the target vessel/bleeding point with coaptation; **gentle traction away from underlying layers** after grasping; for STSC ablation, place the **snare tip only on the mucosal surface**, avoiding the muscularis propria, and stop once the mucosal surface is ablated.
- **Hot forceps avulsion (BPA 10):** for non-lifting submucosal fibrosis (from prior biopsy, incomplete resection, or tattoo). Grasp target tissue with hot forceps while applying **predominant cutting current (e.g. EndoCut I, effect 3, duration 1, interval 3; ERBE)** and mechanically pull the tissue off. Fibrotic tissue has **poor conductivity and high impedance**, so **target a small surface area** to keep current density high. Pull tissue away from the GI wall with **simultaneous short bursts of current (tapping the pedal)**. **Avoid prolonged continuous activation** — cutting current can cause deep tissue injury → bleeding, post-coagulation syndrome, delayed perforation. Efficacy/safety outside the colon undetermined.
- **APC (BPA 11):** argon gas is ionized at the catheter tip by a high-voltage waveform; the plasma conducts current without mechanical contact along the path of least resistance to the nearest tissue, in a **monopolar circuit** exiting through the dispersive electrode. **Power** determines current conducted into tissue — higher power and duration → deeper ablation. **Argon flow rate** determines the volume of plasma available; flow must be sufficient to reach the target, but **if flow is too high the plasma disperses the current → low current density at the target**. **Typically 0.8–1.0 L/min is sufficient for most clinical settings.** Argon gas is **not absorbed** → luminal distension and perforation risk, so track total volume used. Poor bowel prep leaves higher **hydrogen and methane** concentrations which, with oxygen and a heat source, risk the rare but **fatal complication of colonic explosion**; **no consensus threshold exists**, but a thorough **complete oral (as opposed to local colonic) bowel preparation is highly advised** when APC is anticipated.
- **Sphincterotomy (BPA 12):** minimize sphincterotome wire contact with mucosa and sphincter to **achieve high current density**; the wire should **not be "buried"** in the sphincter but gently applied to the surface with **minimal tension**. An older study suggested "pure cut" causes less post-procedure pancreatitis than blended current, but **more recent studies with newer blended-current settings have not shown increased pancreatitis risk and may show reduced post-sphincterotomy bleeding**. Conclusion: **pancreatitis risk is primarily technique-related rather than energy-setting-related**.
- **Third-space endoscopy (BPA 13):** for **fibrotic tissue (low water content, high resistance) use a cut current**, reducing excessive thermal injury from high-peak-voltage coagulation current. Submucosal dissection can be achieved with **either cutting or coagulation current** — choice depends on endoscopist preference, GI location, and tissue characteristics. **Preemptive hemostasis of smaller-caliber vessels** can often be achieved with the **shaft of the ESD knife at a low-power setting (10 W forced coagulation)**, which lowers current density and the risk of cutting through the vessel; the vessel should first be **"skeletonized"** (isolated from surrounding tissue to improve knife contact), then **gentle pressure applied with the widest portion of the knife (the shaft)** to promote vessel sealing and reduce blood flow (avoiding heat sink). For vessels **wider than the widest portion of the ESD knife, and especially arteries, coagulation forceps are preferred**. Underwater: **higher cutting settings** are required for dissection and **higher-power coagulation** for preemptive hemostasis, because current disperses underwater → lower current density than in air or CO₂.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Hot avulsion current — contradicts [[usmstf-2020-endoscopic-removal]] / [[asge-2020-endoscopic-removal]].** The 2020 USMSTF/ASGE description on [[endoscopic-mucosal-resection]] was "apply coagulation current while avulsing"; this 2026 CPU specifies a **predominant cutting current (EndoCut I, effect 3, duration 1, interval 3)** and warns that fibrotic tissue's high impedance requires a small contact area for cutting. Same tier, **newer publication date → the 2026 cut-current guidance is what the wiki asserts**; the older coagulation-current wording is flagged rather than deleted.
- **Cut vs coagulation for polypectomy — older retrospective data vs the RCT.** Two retrospective studies linked cut current to immediate bleeding and coagulation current to delayed bleeding; the RCT found no difference in severe adverse events or delayed bleeding. The CPU resolves toward equivalence (BPA 5) but notes the RCT used a microprocessor-controlled generator, so **the result may not transfer to older analog ESUs** — an explicit generalizability limit, not a settled question.
- **STSC vs APC for post-EMR margin ablation.** Consistent with [[usmstf-2020-endoscopic-removal]]'s "insufficient evidence to pick APC vs snare-tip soft coagulation," but this CPU adds a tiebreaker: **equally effective in a randomized trial, STSC likely more cost-effective in most practice environments**.
- **Pure cut vs blended for sphincterotomy is unresolved.** The older trial favoring pure cut for pancreatitis prevention is contradicted by newer studies of blended currents; the CPU's position is that **technique, not waveform, dominates** the pancreatitis risk.
- **No consensus threshold for bowel-preparation adequacy before APC** — the CPU says so explicitly while still advising a complete oral preparation.
- **Implantable cardiac device management has no GI societal consensus**; the CPU's advice derives from the HRS/ASA perioperative consensus statement, not a GI document.
- **Open questions the CPU names:** optimal ESU settings for underwater/immersion techniques are unstudied ("studies are needed"); optimal settings and their safety across diverse endoscopic procedures are "currently underway"; hot forceps avulsion outside the colon is undetermined; margin thermal ablation outside the colorectum is not well established.
