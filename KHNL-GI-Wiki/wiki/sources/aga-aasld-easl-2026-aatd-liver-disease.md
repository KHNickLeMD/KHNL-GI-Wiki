## Summary

A multidisciplinary expert panel convened by the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) in collaboration with the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD), the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL), and the Alpha-1 Foundation proposes a unified nomenclature, case definition, diagnostic algorithm, and staging framework for the hepatic manifestation of alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) in **adults**.

The proposed term is **alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency–associated liver disease (AATD-LD)**, defined as **adults with the PiZZ genotype who exhibit recurrently elevated liver enzymes (aspartate aminotransferase [AST], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], or γ-glutamyl transferase [GGT]) and/or evidence of significant liver fibrosis (stage ≥2 on a 0–4 scale)**. The problem the definition addresses is that liver enzymes are frequently normal in PiZZ adults — elevated in only 11%–24% across three cross-sectional studies — while 20%–36% have clinically significant fibrosis.

The diagnostic pathway is a four-step algorithm: identify at-risk patients, screen with a **serum AAT concentration**, confirm by **genotyping** (phenotyping by isoelectric focusing where genotyping is unavailable), and stage fibrosis **non-invasively**. Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) by vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) is the preferred staging method, with **≥8 kPa** marking clinically significant fibrosis; blood-based scores (fibrosis-4 index [FIB-4] <1.3, AST-to-platelet ratio index [APRI] <0.5) are endorsed for rule-out but their sensitivity is explicitly described as limited. **Liver biopsy is not required** for diagnosis, and non-invasive tests (NITs) are preferred over biopsy for monitoring. Monitoring interval is set by the LSM risk stratum: 2–3 years (low), 1–2 years (intermediate), annually (high).

The remainder of the document addresses clinical trial design — inclusion/exclusion criteria, endpoints, and pulmonary monitoring. The consensus primary efficacy endpoint for phase 3 trials is **≥1-stage improvement in fibrosis over 2 years** by METAVIR using paired biopsies.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Grading

The document gives **no evidence grades and no recommendation numbers** — it is expert consensus guidance presented as a narrative with a summary recommendation table (Table 4). Any category headings below are the document's own (Table 4); the ordering is the wiki's.

### Nomenclature and definition (Table 4)

- **Nomenclature:** *alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency–associated liver disease (AATD-LD)*.
- **Definition:** AATD-LD is proposed to be defined as **adults with the PiZZ genotype who exhibit recurrently elevated liver enzymes (AST, ALT, or GGT) and/or evidence of significant liver fibrosis (fibrosis stage ≥2 on a 0–4 scale)**.
- The term *alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency–associated liver disease* would usually be applied to adults with the **PiZZ genotype**, because the risk of fibrosis is much higher there. Patients with PiMZ and PiSZ genotypes often have additional comorbid conditions — diabetes, obesity, or excessive alcohol use — noted at the time of clinical liver disease diagnosis.
- **LSM cutoff of 8 kPa by VCTE** was chosen for the fibrosis limb, in line with current non-invasive fibrosis guidelines and with a Delphi panel that recommended LSM as the preferred method of liver fibrosis evaluation in AATD.
- **APRI <0.5 and FIB-4 <1.3** might be used to rule out AATD-LD, although values below these cutoffs were associated with a very low risk of major adverse liver outcomes (MALOs) over a median 3.5 years of follow-up, **an FIB-4 <1.3 was seen in approximately 50% of the cohort, which limits its utility as a triage tool**.
- **Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) 3 kPa** has been associated with clinically significant fibrosis in other liver diseases and is likely to be appropriate in AATD.

### Genetics and risk

- AATD is a rare inherited disorder caused by sequence variations in the **SERPINA1** gene, **autosomal codominant** inheritance, resulting in reduced serum levels and dysfunctional forms of the AAT protein.
- The **Z allele** (**Glu342Lys** substitution) is the most common pathogenic variant.
- Prevalence by genotype in White individuals, and risk of liver disease from the genetic mutation alone (Figure 1):

| Genotype | Prevalence | Liver disease risk from mutation alone |
|---|---|---|
| **PiZZ** | **1:3000** | **Highest** — lowest prevalence but highest risk |
| PiSZ | 1:500 | Intermediate |
| PiMZ | 1:30 | Lower |
| PiMM | 9:10 | Normal |

- PiZZ carries an approximately **20-fold increased risk of advanced liver fibrosis** vs wild type; **PiMZ ~2× and PiSZ ~3× elevated risks**.
- Approximately **10% of adults with the PiZZ genotype develop clinically significant liver disease**, including cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).
- **Pathogenesis is a toxic gain-of-function**: accumulation of misfolded AAT protein within the endoplasmic reticulum of hepatocytes → cellular stress, inflammation, and subsequent fibrosis. This mechanism distinguishes hepatic involvement from the protease–antiprotease imbalance underlying pulmonary disease.
- Modifiers of progression: **age, male sex, metabolic syndrome, alcohol use, and other coexisting liver insults (e.g., viral hepatitis)**.
- Individuals with an AAT serum level **<57 mg/dL with severe AATD-LD generally have the PiZZ genotype**; approximately **5% have non-Z alleles** and may not have misfolded AAT production in the liver. **Null variants produce no protein and have no liver risk.**

### Why enzymes alone miss the disease

- Cross-sectional studies describe clinically significant liver fibrosis in **20%–36% of PiZZ genotypes**, yet serum transaminases often remain within the normal range.
- Three cross-sectional studies reported **elevated liver function tests in only 11%–24% of patients** (AST 13%–15%; ALT 11%–19%; GGT 13%–24%), with abnormal findings more common in patients with comorbid conditions such as obesity or diabetes.
- Among liver function tests, **GGT correlated best with histologic fibrosis and MALO development**; AST and ALT appear somewhat less accurate. Persistently elevated liver function tests are associated with an increased risk of MALOs.

### Case detection and diagnostic testing (Table 4)

- **The diagnostic evaluation of chronic liver disease should include testing for AATD by means of determining the serum AAT level.** It is an inexpensive test that is typically abnormal in severe deficiency genotypes (e.g., ZZ).
- **Genotyping is necessary to comprehensively determine carrier genotypes (MZ)**, because AAT is an **acute-phase reactant** and serum levels can be in the normal range under physiologic stress. Genotyping is more expensive but embraced by many liver centers.
- The diagnostic approach for any patient who may potentially have AATD-LD **should begin with levels <11 μM (57 mg/dL)**.
- For case finding, low serum levels should be followed by **confirmatory testing by genotyping (preferred)** or alternatively **phenotyping by isoelectric focusing**, depending on local availability.
- **Optimally, AAT levels should be determined in the absence of systemic inflammation (i.e., normal C-reactive protein)** because it is an acute-phase protein. Some patients with chronic liver disease have a persistent slight elevation of C-reactive protein reflective of systemic inflammation. A patient with MZ may have a normal serum level of AAT and require a genotype to diagnose.
- **Suspected or confirmed evidence of steatotic liver disease does not preclude testing for AATD**, as both conditions overlap, with the Z allele amplifying liver fibrosis progression.
- **Case finding is also indicated** in patients with a **family history of AATD**, or an individual history of **neonatal cholestasis**, or associated extrahepatic conditions: **emphysema, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma with airflow obstruction that is incompletely reversible, unexplained bronchiectasis, necrotizing panniculitis, or granulomatosis with polyangiitis**.

**Four-step algorithm (Figure 2).** Framing statement: liver specialists (gastroenterologists and hepatologists) should detect and manage AATD-LD early; pulmonologists should evaluate AATD patients for liver disease.

- **Step 1 — identify patients at risk** (any of): recurrent elevated AST/ALT/GGT · hepatic fibrosis ≥F2 (including cirrhosis — anyone with cirrhosis or being evaluated for liver transplantation should be tested for AATD) · family history of AATD · history of neonatal cholestasis · history of AATD-associated extrahepatic conditions.
- **Step 2 — screen for deficient AAT by serum concentration:**

| Serum AAT | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **>20 μM (>90 mg/dL)** | Minimal risk of severe AATD |
| **11–20 μM (57–90 mg/dL)** | Mild/intermediate AATD |
| **<11 μM (<57 mg/dL)** | At risk for severe AATD, likely ZZ |

- **Step 3 — genotype to confirm and stratify risk** (phenotyping by isoelectric focusing if genotyping is unavailable):

| Result | Action |
|---|---|
| **PiSS and null alleles** | Counsel on lung risk or refer |
| **PiSZ or PiMZ** | Consider cofactors; counsel on liver/lung risk |
| **PiZZ** | Referral to genetic counseling and pulmonology |

- **Step 4 — if hepatic fibrosis stage is unknown, use non-invasive testing to stage AATD-LD:**
  - **Blood-based NIT:** low risk = **FIB-4 <1.3 or APRI <0.5** → repeat blood-based NIT **annually during pulmonary monitoring**. Requires second-line NIT if **FIB-4 ≥1.3 or APRI ≥0.5**.
  - **LSM by VCTE (preferred) or MRE**, depending on local availability:

| LSM (VCTE) | Risk stratum | Repeat LSM |
|---|---|---|
| **<8 kPa** | Low (significant fibrosis unlikely) | **2–3 years** |
| **8–15 kPa** | Intermediate (likely significant to advanced fibrosis) — *consider liver biopsy for definitive diagnosis* | **1–2 years** |
| **≥15 kPa** | High (likely cirrhosis) and of MALOs | **Annually** |

  - **Decompensated cirrhosis** → monitor for portal hypertension and HCC; **evaluate early for liver transplantation**.
  - **Cofactors** (obesity, diabetes mellitus, excessive alcohol consumption) increase liver risk and should be considered for individualization of surveillance intervals.

### Staging and monitoring (Table 4)

- Patients with the PiZZ genotype should undergo liver fibrosis staging using **non-invasive means, including blood-based and imaging-based approaches (VCTE preferred)**.
- **Longitudinal reassessment using NIT for liver fibrosis during routine pulmonary follow-up** may offer a practical approach to ongoing liver disease monitoring.
- Patients with **VCTE LSM <8 kPa are deemed low risk and should be re-evaluated every 2–3 y**, those with **LSM 8–15 kPa are intermediate risk and should be re-evaluated every 1–2 y**, and those with **LSM ≥15 kPa are high risk and should be monitored yearly**.
- In those with **LSM >15 kPa (advanced chronic liver disease)**, **yearly LSM assessment by VCTE as well as surveillance for HCC and management of portal hypertension are recommended**.
- Intervals should be **individualized** considering risk factors for fibrosis — in particular **obesity** and potentially diabetes. Obesity is an important target for lifestyle intervention in AATD-LD.
- **Alcohol:** analogous to steatotic liver disease, patients with AATD-LD should be cautioned regarding the risks associated with consuming alcohol, and **those who are candidates for treatment should completely and permanently stop alcohol consumption**.
- Shear-wave elastography and MRE may serve as alternatives to VCTE, depending on local availability and cost; not specifically validated in this population.
- The **enhanced liver fibrosis test has not been validated** against liver biopsy or clinical outcomes in AATD-LD; its role in staging remains uncertain.
- **Ultrasound** may be considered at the time of diagnosis of AATD-LD and is **currently only recommended for HCC screening and surveillance in those with cirrhosis**.

### Liver biopsy (Table 4)

- **Liver biopsy is not required for establishing the diagnosis of AATD-LD**, although it may be indicated in select cases to establish or exclude additional factors contributing to liver disease.
- Histologic staging of liver fibrosis is currently considered an **imperfect reference standard**. For the purpose of staging, **NITs that have been validated vs histologic fibrosis and/or MALOs are preferable over liver biopsy in AATD-LD**, as the latter is impractical for monitoring the longitudinal evolution of this genetic liver disease.

### HCC surveillance, portal hypertension, and liver transplantation (Table 4)

- The indication for **HCC surveillance** and **portal hypertension management** should be based on published recommendations for the management of cirrhosis of any etiology.
- After first hepatic decompensation (clinically evident ascites, variceal bleeding, or overt hepatic encephalopathy), patients with decompensated AATD-LD have a **higher risk of further decompensation, acute-on-chronic liver failure, requirement for liver transplantation, or death compared with other etiologies**.
- **Early consideration for liver transplantation is warranted in patients with decompensated AATD-LD.** Liver transplantation is the **only curative option** for decompensated AATD-LD.
- These patients may have **higher risks of post-transplantation complications** compared with other etiologies; **early referral to a center with expertise in both transplant hepatology and AATD-LD is strongly recommended**.
- **Any patient with decompensation, including ascites, variceal bleeding, or hepatic encephalopathy, should be evaluated promptly** to facilitate timely transplant listing and optimize pretransplantation care.

### Therapy

- **No regulatory guidance for AATD-LD drug development is available, and no approved therapies exist for liver disease associated with AATD.** Liver transplantation is the only option for decompensated cirrhosis and is challenging in patients with pulmonary disease.
- Investigational agents in human trials (Table 1) — liver and lung: **BEAM-302** (DNA editing, phase 1/2b), **WVE-006** (RNA editing, phase 1/2b), **KRRO-110** (RNA editing, phase 1/2a). Liver: **fazirsiran** (small interfering RNA, phase 3), **BMN-349** (chaperone, phase 1). Lung: **Kamada-AAT** (AAT protein replacement, phase 3), **INBRX-101** (AAT protein replacement, phase 2/3), **alvelestat** (neutrophil elastase inhibitor, phase 2), **KB-408** (gene therapy, phase 1).
- Phase 2 studies of fazirsiran showed promise.

### Clinical trial design (Table 4; Tables 2, 3)

- **The ideal study population would be patients with PiZZ genotype with significant liver fibrosis (F2–F4).**
- **When investigational therapies targeting the liver move into phase 2b or phase 3 studies, the trial design should consider a stepwise approach toward including patients with significant liver disease.**
- **A 1-stage improvement in fibrosis stage over a 2-y period should be sufficient as a primary endpoint in phase 3 clinical trials** (METAVIR Histologic Scoring System, paired biopsies; recommended trial duration 2 y).
- **Inclusion criteria (all required):** PiZZ genotype · clinically significant liver fibrosis **F2–F4** by any method (FIB-4, APRI, VCTE, MRE, or biopsy) · **forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV₁) >50% predicted** for therapies that inhibit Z-protein production and require long-term treatment.
- **Exclusion criteria:** decompensated liver disease. Clinically significant portal hypertension should be excluded in early-phase studies and may be less amenable to improvement in later phase trials.
- **Secondary endpoints:** reduction in hepatocyte Z-AAT accumulation · reduction in hepatic globule burden · increase in functional AAT level · 1-stage improvement in fibrosis using NIT. **Exploratory:** reduction in circulating Z-polymer.
- **Preservation of pulmonary function was not deemed appropriate for inclusion in trials of therapies that increase AAT levels via DNA or RNA editing.**
- Fewer than 50% of patients with the PiZZ genotype would be willing to participate in a trial with more than 2 biopsies, which may limit study participation.

### Pulmonary monitoring (Table 4)

- **If lung function is used as a stopping rule, a significant decline in FEV₁ or transfer coefficient of carbon monoxide (KCO) should require a reduction of ≥20%, confirmed on at least 2 consecutive tests.**
- **Low FEV₁% predicted inclusion criteria should be considered**, particularly if patients are on augmentation therapy and carefully monitored.
- **Lung function measurements are an imperfect tool to use as stopping rules in clinical trials.** Spirometric tests should be spaced at least 3 months apart; the typical AATD patient has 2 exacerbations per year.
- Death in AATD-associated lung disease increases significantly only when **FEV₁% falls below 50% of predicted**; patients with FEV₁% ≥50% may be safely included in RNA interference trials provided they are closely monitored.

### Pediatric considerations

- The document is focused on adults, who tend to have presentations and disease courses different from most children with ZZ.
- The diagnostic path and the role of AAT in the differential diagnosis of liver abnormalities can be strongly considered to apply in children as well as adults.
- **Continued follow-up for liver abnormalities throughout life, regularly or annually, including the use of NITs, is reasonable to apply to children** — with the understanding that not all LSM modalities are readily available at all pediatric centers.
- Lung disease monitoring is not generally needed in children unless they have asthma or are symptomatic; it seems wise to refer patients with ZZ for a **baseline adult pulmonary evaluation at age 18 years**.
- At present, none of the AATD liver treatment trials are open to enrollment of participants younger than 18 years.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No conflict with existing wiki content** — AATD-LD had no page and no ingested source before this document. Nothing it states is contradicted by an ingested guideline.
- **The ≥8 kPa VCTE threshold is consistent** with the general significant-fibrosis cutoffs already on `[[noninvasive-liver-disease-assessment]]`; the AATD-specific element is the **monitoring interval attached to each stratum**, which no other ingested source gives for this disease.
- **Acknowledged limitations (the document's own):** recommendations rest on available evidence and expert consensus and will require validation in large prospective or interventional studies across diverse patient populations. Direct evidence linking histologic liver fibrosis to MALOs in adults with the PiZZ genotype is limited. Data on intra-subject variability of LSM in AATD-LD are still being accumulated.
- **Unmet research needs named by the panel (Table 5):** natural history of AATD-LD · global incidence and prevalence across geographies and ethnic groups · mechanism of injury in liver and lung · role of gene silencing and gene editing · performance of NITs in detecting AATD-LD · which patients develop liver disease and why · predictors of treatment response · NITs that predict long-term clinical outcomes · a uniform approach to assessing metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease in trial participants, with defined permissible alcohol limits.
- **Enhanced liver fibrosis test** — unvalidated in AATD-LD; staging role uncertain.
- **Whether PiMZ and PiSZ patients fall within the AATD-LD definition** is explicitly left open: future trials showing efficacy coincident with modification of the Z protein will be needed to determine how often these genotypes improve and fall within the nomenclature boundaries.
