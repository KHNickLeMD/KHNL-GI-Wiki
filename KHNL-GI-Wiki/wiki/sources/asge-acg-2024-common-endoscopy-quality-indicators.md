## Summary

The **base layer** of the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) / American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) quality series: **19 indicators that apply to every gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopic procedure**, regardless of which procedure it is. The procedure-specific documents — [[asge-acg-2024-colonoscopy-quality-indicators|colonoscopy]], [[acg-asge-2026-ercp-quality-indicators|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]], upper endoscopy, endoscopic ultrasound — sit **on top of** this one; the task force calls these indicators the "building blocks" on which the others stand. It is the **third iteration**, after 2006 and 2015, with a literature search from January 2014 through **December 2022**.

Almost the whole set is **process** measures graded **3 (expert opinion only)** with a target of **>98%**, and the reason is stated explicitly: for most of these indicators the task force considered **failure to perform them a "never event"**, so the target was set where only very unusual circumstances would excuse a miss. That framing is the document's defining feature — unlike the colonoscopy and ERCP sets, **variation in practice and outcomes was deliberately *not* the basis for inclusion**, because these activities are expected to happen almost universally with minimal variability.

**Six priority indicators** are named: **appropriate indication** (#1), **prophylactic antibiotics** (#5), **antithrombotic management** (#6) and **adverse-event documentation** (#17) are the four described in the Priority Quality Indicators section as reliably measurable in a manageable number of examinations and very likely to improve care. (The table also marks #1, #5, #6 and #17 as priority indicators.)

The substantive clinical content sits in the discussions rather than the indicator statements: the **antithrombotic** section, the **sedation risk-assessment and monitoring** section, the **adverse-event** definitions and rates, and the **minimal elements of an endoscopy report**.

The document states the same guardrail as its companions: the indicators and targets **do not reflect the standard of care, credentialing requirements or training standards, and applying them that way is a misuse of the document**.

### Strength-of-recommendation scheme (Table 2)

Identical to the one used by the colonoscopy and ERCP documents — the task force's own 8-level scheme (1A, 1B, 1C+, 1C, 2A, 2B, 2C, 3), adapted from Guyatt, **not GRADE**. **3 = expert opinion only.** Fifteen of the 19 indicators here carry it.

### Sedation (indicators 4, 10, 11)

- **The sedation plan must specify the intended level** — no sedation, minimal, moderate, deep (monitored anesthesia care), or general anesthesia — and be documented **before sedation starts**.
- **Risk stratification:** the two most commonly used instruments are the **American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score** (1 = normal, 5 = critically ill and at substantial risk of death within 24 hours) and the **Mallampati score**. Large database studies show **ASA scores predict adverse events during endoscopy, primarily those related to sedation**. **Mallampati has not been rigorously validated as a risk-stratification tool for endoscopy** but has gained clinical relevance with the widespread use of deep sedation and the possible need for urgent airway management.
- **Even when an anesthesiologist delivers the sedation, input from the endoscopist can be critical** to clarify the risk-to-benefit ratio and inform the optimal anesthesia approach.
- **Rescue capability is the operative rule:** sedation is a continuum and individual response is unpredictable, so **those giving moderate sedation must be able to rescue a patient who enters deep sedation, and those giving deep sedation must be able to rescue a patient who enters general anesthesia**.
- **Monitoring:** oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry, heart rate and blood pressure in **all** sedated procedures; **blood pressure and heart rate recorded at intervals of ≤5 minutes**. **Electrocardiographic monitoring is indicated for deep sedation and general anesthesia.** **Capnography** is associated with reduced apnea and hypoxemia in randomized trials of endoscopy under propofol deep sedation and **can be considered**.
- **Fasting (ASA practice guidelines):** no clear liquids for **2 hours**, milk **6 hours**, a light meal **6 hours**, a meal with fried or fatty food **8 hours** before sedation. **Gastroparesis, achalasia and bowel obstruction may require a longer fast** to minimize aspiration risk.
- **Patients may take essential medications**, with the possible exception of anticoagulation — including bowel preparation before endoscopic procedures. Prospective studies found **minimal residual gastric fluid (<25 mL)** after colonoscopy preparation, **whether the prep was split or taken entirely the evening before**.
- **Nothing-by-mouth and medication policy for patients on glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and other weight-loss drugs that may delay gastric emptying will evolve as data emerge** — the document does not set one.

> [!pitfall] Do not turn indicator 11 into a punitive metric
> Reversal agents and premature termination are **potentially life-saving decisions and can represent excellent clinical judgement**. The task force states that penalizing clinicians for using them could have devastating unintended consequences; such events must be reviewed **non-punitively**, within process improvement, and **not as an indirect measure of outcome**. The goal is to reduce the number of events requiring interruption — **not to discourage reversal or termination in a potential emergency**.

### Antithrombotic management (indicator 6)

- **Diagnostic procedures, including those with mucosal biopsy sampling, are low risk for procedure-related bleeding and do not require cessation of antiplatelet or anticoagulant agents.**
- **Some therapeutic procedures are high risk**, and most such cases without extenuating circumstances **require cessation of anticoagulants**.
- **Some interventions traditionally thought to require interruption may not** — the document names **cold snare resection of small polyps in patients on clopidogrel** — and this should be considered in the plan.
- **Patients at high thromboembolic risk** may need bridging, deferment of endoscopy, and/or specialist consultation. The high-risk conditions named: **atrial fibrillation with other cardiac conditions** (heart failure, valvular disease), **history of thromboembolism**, **mechanical mitral valve**, **coronary artery stent placed within a year**, **acute coronary syndrome**, and **nonstented percutaneous coronary intervention after myocardial infarction**.
- **In most nontherapeutic procedures, antithrombotics may be resumed immediately if they were discontinued.** After a high-bleeding-risk intervention, timing of resumption is individualized on the type of endoscopic therapy and the thromboembolic risk.
- **Almost all endoscopic procedures can be performed safely on 81–325 mg of daily aspirin**, and recent guidelines recommend **against periprocedural interruption of aspirin prescribed for secondary cardiovascular prevention**. Evidence suggests **aspirin is frequently stopped inappropriately** before endoscopy, and education reduces this — a potentially important quality-improvement initiative for an endoscopy unit.

### Antibiotic prophylaxis (indicator 5)

**GI endoscopy is not considered a significant risk factor for clinically important bacteremia.** Prophylaxis is **not** recommended for:

- Prevention of bacterial endocarditis or other infectious adverse events, **even in those at highest risk for these conditions**.
- **Cardiac conditions, synthetic vascular grafts or other nonvalvular cardiovascular devices** undergoing any endoscopic procedure.
- **Biliary obstruction in the absence of cholangitis** undergoing ERCP **with anticipated complete drainage**.
- **Solid lesions along the upper GI tract and colon** undergoing endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle sampling.
- **Prosthetic joints** undergoing any endoscopic procedure.

It **is** recommended for: **ERCP with anticipated incomplete biliary drainage** (e.g., primary sclerosing cholangitis); **ERCP after orthotopic liver transplantation**; **ERCP with cholangioscopy**; **any endoscopic procedure in patients with cirrhosis and acute GI hemorrhage** (high incidence of concurrent bacterial infectious adverse events); **percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube placement in all patients**; **colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis**; and **endoscopic ultrasound-guided fine-needle aspiration of a mediastinal or pancreatic cystic lesion** — the last of which **remains controversial**.

> Antibiotic management is classified as a **preprocedure** indicator while recognizing that antimicrobial decisions are also made **during** the procedure as appropriate indications arise. The document is explicit that **judicious use to minimize unnecessary administration is an equally important** aim of this indicator, not just giving antibiotics where indicated.

### Adverse events (indicators 17 and 18)

**Definition.** An adverse event is one that **prevents completion of the planned procedure** or results in **admission to the hospital, prolongation of the existing hospital stay, another invasive procedure, or subsequent medical consultation**.

**Timing classification:** preprocedure · intraprocedure · **postprocedure (up to 14 days)** · **late (any time after 14 days)**.

**What to document:** the **level of certainty** that the event is attributable to the procedure — **definite, probable, possible or unlikely** — and the **severity**, graded by the degree of consequent disturbance to the patient and any change in the plan of care — **mild, moderate, severe or fatal**. Preprocedure and intraprocedure events evident at completion go in the endoscopy report; later-recognized events should be documented too, **ideally linked to the original report as an addendum**.

> [!pearl] Relying on the patient to call you misses most delayed adverse events
> **15% to 45% of adverse events go unrecognized or unreported.** Active efforts to contact patients, **ideally 2 weeks after the procedure**, are what capture them. The task force acknowledges the added effort and cost of 14-day follow-up remain real obstacles.

**Rates the document prints:**

| Event | Rate |
|---|---|
| Cardiopulmonary adverse events, general endoscopic procedures | **0.01%–1.1%** |
| Cardiopulmonary adverse events, ERCP | **up to 5%** |
| Perforation, diagnostic upper endoscopy | **~1 in 25,000** |
| Perforation, therapeutic upper endoscopy | **up to 1%** |
| Perforation, screening colonoscopy | **generally <0.1%** |
| Perforation, endoscopic ultrasound and ERCP | **0.1%–0.6%** |
| Bleeding after ERCP **with sphincterotomy** | **0.3%–2%** |
| Bleeding, screening and diagnostic colonoscopy | **well under 1%** |
| Bleeding, colonoscopy **with polypectomy across the full range of lesion sizes** | **~1%** |
| Bleeding when **cold snare resection is the primary tool for lesions ≤10 mm** | **well under 1%** |

Delayed bleeding is **increased after endoscopic mucosal resection, endoscopic submucosal dissection and other complex therapeutic interventions**. Because of their clinical importance, **cardiopulmonary events, perforation (not identified and treated intraprocedurally) and major hemorrhage** are the three the document names as worth tracking against these benchmarks.

### Minimal elements of an endoscopy report (indicator 16)

Twenty required elements: date · patient identification · **endoscopist(s), including trainees, participating in the procedure** · **assistant(s) participating** · relevant history and physical examination (if not separately documented) · confirmation of informed consent **and team pause** · endoscopic procedure **both planned and performed** · indication(s) · **type of endoscopic instrument(s) used** · **medications administered, including dose and route** · anatomic extent of examination · **quality of visualization** (bowel preparation for colonoscopy, retained food for upper endoscopy) · **limitation(s) of examination, or the absence of limitations** · tissue or fluid samples obtained · findings · diagnostic impression · results of therapeutic intervention (if any) · adverse events (if any) · disposition · recommendations for subsequent care.

### Photodocumentation (indicator 9)

- **Cecal intubation rates approaching 100% are achievable in healthy adults**, and photodocumentation of the cecum is the simplest and most practical way to verify a complete colonoscopy — it is **fundamental to calculating the cecal intubation rate indicator**. Capture the **appendiceal orifice with the surrounding cecal strap fold** and a **view of the cecum with the ileocecal valve**; alternatives are the **ileocecal valve orifice** or the **terminal ileum** showing villi, circular valvulae conniventes, or lymphoid hyperplasia.
- **At ERCP:** endoscopic photos of the **papilla before and after any intervention** (sphincterotomy, stent placement) and of any pathologic findings; plus a **fluoroscopic scout film before starting** and representative images of important pancreaticobiliary findings.

### Other indicator content worth carrying

- **Discharge (13):** every endoscopy unit should have a **written policy** of criteria the patient must meet — generally a prespecified level of consciousness, certain hemodynamic parameters, and freedom from excess pain or other untoward symptoms — and satisfaction of those criteria must be **documented**.
- **Patient instructions (14):** written, and covering dietary restrictions, **resumption or change in medications including antithrombotics and new prescriptions**, return to activities such as driving, contact information for questions or emergencies, **signs and symptoms of delayed adverse events and exactly whom to contact**, how biopsy results will be communicated, and follow-up appointments or the lack of need for them.
- **Credentialing (8):** ASGE credentialing guidelines recommend **objective criteria and direct observation**, not simply the number of procedures performed, to define competency. Competence measures should be **universal and not vary by provider specialty**; **competency in one procedure does not imply competency in another**; and competency requires the endoscopist to perform the **minimum therapeutic maneuvers specific to that procedure** — standard polypectomy for colonoscopy, stent placement for distal biliary obstruction at ERCP.
- **Open-access endoscopy** (nongastroenterologists scheduling without prior consultation) has been widely practiced for over two decades and most studies show these procedures are usually done for appropriate indications — but quality-improvement efforts should include **feedback to referring physicians on indication appropriateness** and processes to triage some open-access referrals to clinic.
- **Specimen verification (12):** specimen mishandling causes missed diagnoses, delayed or improper treatment and increased cost; the most frequent errors are **mislabeling and lack of standardization**. A policy on **reconciliation of specimen requisition** so that physician and staff agree on labeling is the meaningful, feasible measure, and verification **must be performed for every specimen container**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Internal inconsistencies, not contradictions with other sources:** indicator 1 is graded 1C+ in the table and 1C in the text; indicator 6's target is >95% in the table and 95% in the text; indicator 19 is an outcome measure in the table and a process measure in the text. Recorded as published — do not silently pick one.
- **Priority indicators: four.** Table 1 marks #1, #5, #6 and #17, and the *Priority Quality Indicators* section discusses the same four. The companion ERCP document's reprint of this table marks the same four. Any later claim of a different number needs a source.
- **Mallampati is used but not validated for endoscopy** — the document says so plainly. It should not be presented as a validated endoscopic risk tool.
- **No nothing-by-mouth rule for GLP-1 receptor agonists.** The document explicitly defers, saying policy "will evolve as clinical data emerge". Any such rule on the wiki must come from a different source.
- **Patient satisfaction (indicator 19) has no performance target**, and the document says true outcome indicators of patient experience may become feasible only as benchmarks are defined. Nothing to assert.
