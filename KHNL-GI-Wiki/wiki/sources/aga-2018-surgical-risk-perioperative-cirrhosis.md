## Summary

An expert review addressing the patient with cirrhosis who needs an **invasive non–liver-transplant surgical procedure**. It covers why cirrhosis raises operative risk (synthetic dysfunction, portal hypertension, malnutrition/sarcopenia, thrombocytopenia and rebalanced hemostasis, renal dysfunction, altered drug handling), how to quantify that risk with existing tools, the procedure-specific data, and how to manage the patient before, during, and after the operation.

Risk prediction rests on the **CTP score**, the **MELD score**, the **ASA Physical Status Classification**, and the **Mayo Postoperative Mortality Risk Score** (a calculator combining MELD score, ASA class, age, and etiology). The update is explicit that **no single system is definitive** and recommends using multiple methods. Surgical risk is **continuous — there are no absolute cutoff values** for exclusion — but Child-Pugh class C (CTP score >10) or MELD score >20 marks a high-risk group in whom all but the most urgent and life-saving procedures should be avoided or delayed until after liver transplantation.

Procedure type and anatomic site matter independently: hepatobiliary surgery (especially primary liver resection), other intra-abdominal procedures, thoracic surgery, and cardiovascular procedures carry the highest risk. The document assembles the procedure-specific thresholds reported in the literature (Table 3) and reviews hepatic resection, cholecystectomy, abdominal wall hernia repair, cardiovascular surgery, and bariatric surgery individually.

Perioperative management emphasizes: aggressive preoperative control of ascites, variceal bleeding risk, and encephalopathy; avoidance of volume excess or depletion; **no protocol transfusion to a target INR** (INR does not predict procedural bleeding); viscoelastic-testing–directed transfusion where available; careful analgesia and sedation choices; and preoperative **liver transplant evaluation** when predicted postoperative 3-month mortality exceeds 15% or the MELD score is ≥15. The authors note the data are limited in scope, usually lack risk-matched controls, and are largely single-center — so evidence-based recommendations are "mostly lacking."

## Best Practice Advice (verbatim, BPA 1–15)

Reproduced as printed in **Table 1**. No evidence grades accompany these statements in the source.

> **BPA 1:** Due to the profound effects of hepatic synthetic dysfunction and portal hypertension, patients with cirrhosis are at increased risk of death after all invasive surgical procedures compared with the healthy general population. It is not entirely clear how much the risk attributed to cirrhosis is additive to traditional cardiopulmonary risk factors studied in the general population. Patients with cirrhosis undergoing all but the most emergent surgical procedures should be risk stratified and counseled on the magnitude of that risk.

> **BPA 2:** In patients considered for surgery, use the CTP score (Child-Pugh class), MELD score, Mayo Postoperative Mortality Risk Score, or another validated risk stratification system. There is no single definitive risk stratification system to determine operative risk in all patients with cirrhosis and we recommend using multiple methods.

> **BPA 3:** The type and anatomic site of the proposed surgical procedure are important in risk stratification. The clinical teams must incorporate the surgical procedure itself into discussions of risk with patients. Procedures associated with higher surgical risk include hepatobiliary surgery, such as primary liver resection, other intra-abdominal procedures, thoracic surgery, and cardiovascular procedures. Avoid elective cholecystectomy if possible and if required, it should be carried out in centers with expertise in this population.

> **BPA 4:** Surgical risk is continuous and there are no absolute cutoff values for excluding patients with cirrhosis from surgical procedures. The patient and the surgical and medical teams caring for the patient must weigh the potential benefits and risks collaboratively. Patients should be referred to a surgical team with experience in the care of patients with cirrhosis and portal hypertension whenever possible. Patients with Child-Pugh class C (CTP score >10) or a MELD score >20 pose a high risk of postoperative decompensation and death. Avoid or delay until after liver transplantation, if possible, all but the most urgent and life-saving procedures in this population.

> **BPA 5:** TIPS is not routinely recommended before surgical procedures in patients with cirrhosis and portal hypertension with abdominal collaterals. Small uncontrolled case series have demonstrated the ability to decompress collateral vessels with TIPS preoperatively in patients requiring deep pelvic and colonic resections; however, the absolute benefit of TIPS over conservative management is not established.

> **BPA 6:** The special case of primary liver resection in a patient with cirrhosis, usually for malignancy, has been studied more thoroughly than other general surgical procedures. Data support the safety profile of segmental liver resections in patients without clinically significant portal hypertension, as indicated by an HVPG <10 mm Hg. A validated surrogate measure of lack of clinically significant portal hypertension is the absence of venous abdominal collaterals on imaging or of esophageal varices on endoscopy, peripheral blood platelet count >100,000/µL, or hepatic transient elastography values of <23kPa.

> **BPA 7:** There are no established preoperative safety thresholds for common laboratory values related to bleeding and clotting. The INR is not predictive of procedural bleeding risk in patients with cirrhosis. We do not recommend protocol transfusions to a target INR in patients with cirrhosis. In vitro studies suggest that a platelet count >50,000/µL is adequate to generate thrombin and provide stable clot formation and retrospective clinical studies show an increased bleeding tendency in patients with a platelet count lower than this threshold. The literature provides no evidence to support protocol transfusions, but critically ill patients with plasma fibrinogen levels <100 mg/dL have more bleeding events. Handle coagulation management on a case-by-case basis preferably using viscoelastic testing directed therapy and avoid needless transfusions or volume overload.

> **BPA 8:** Involve a skilled medical team with experience in treating patients with cirrhosis in the postoperative management of patients with cirrhosis, and obtain early consultation to help avoid progressive complications. Achieve optimal control of ascites, variceal bleeding risk, and hepatic encephalopathy before surgery, if possible. Monitor renal and hepatic function at least daily in the postoperative period.

> **BPA 9:** Aggressively avoid exacerbations of portal hypertension and associated complications in the postoperative period. Appropriate measures include close monitoring of renal function and avoidance of volume excess or depletion. Excess volume can increase the risk of variceal and other portal hypertensive bleeding.

> **BPA 10:** Due to disturbed metabolism and elimination, patients with cirrhosis are at especially high risk for medication-related complications. Generally, use opiates at lower doses and with longer dosing intervals than in the general population. Use only short-acting benzodiazepines. Avoidance of constipation should be a priority in the management of these patients to minimize flares of hepatic encephalopathy in the postoperative period. In patients who can take medications orally, rifaximin generally causes less bowel distension than nonabsorbable disaccharides (lactulose).

> **BPA 11:** Avoid medications that might be toxic in patients with portal hypertension and cirrhosis. Do not use NSAIDs because they can impair renal blood flow. Patients with cirrhosis, especially those with heavy alcohol use, can be susceptible to acetaminophen toxicity at doses lower than those that may cause toxicity in the general population. Do not prescribe combination opiate/acetaminophen pain relievers to take home after surgery because the patient may be unaware of the presence of acetaminophen in various over-the-counter products and unintentional acetaminophen overdose and hepatotoxicity can result.

> **BPA 12:** The gallbladder wall may appear thickened on imaging, which may lead to the erroneous diagnosis of acute cholecystitis. A diagnosis of acute cholecystitis should be made only in the appropriate clinical setting, usually in the presence of biliary pain. There is a significant risk of complications after cholecystectomy in patients with cirrhosis. Avoid elective cholecystectomy in a patient with confirmed cirrhosis. Refer patients who require cholecystectomy to a surgical team with experience in invasive procedures in the cirrhotic population.

> **BPA 13:** Except for incarceration that cannot be manually reduced or suspected strangulation with bowel ischemia or gangrene, abdominal hernia surgery should be avoided in the patient with cirrhosis and ascites unless the ascites is completely controlled. Wound dehiscence, peritonitis, and poor outcomes frequently occur when ascites recurs after hernia surgery.

> **BPA 14:** Patients who experience hepatic decompensation after surgery may become candidates for liver transplantation. The MELD score cutoff value for selecting patients for a liver transplant evaluation before elective surgery is not clear. We recommend preoperative liver transplant evaluation when the predicted postoperative 3-month mortality rate is greater than 15% as reflected by surgical risk stratification models or when the MELD score is ≥15.

> **BPA 15:** Centers with expertise in surgery in cirrhotic patients may perform bariatric surgery in this population but clinically significant portal hypertension is a contraindication to the operation. In highly selected patients with obesity and decompensated cirrhosis undergoing liver transplantation, a sleeve gastrectomy at the same time as liver transplantation is an option.

## Scope, Target Audience, and Baseline Risk Factors (Table 1)

| Field | Content as printed |
|---|---|
| **Target audience** | Gastroenterologists, hepatologists, general surgeons, surgical subspecialists, anesthesiologists, critical care physicians, and other clinicians seeing patients with cirrhosis |
| **Target population** | Patients with cirrhosis undergoing invasive non–liver transplant surgical procedures |
| **Baseline factors contributing to surgical risk** | Significant malnutrition; portal hypertension, raising the risk of bleeding complications; thrombocytopenia, coagulation, and fibrinolytic abnormalities; renal dysfunction; impaired hepatic drug metabolism and elimination; increased susceptibility to infection; decreased effective intravascular volume and susceptibility to acute renal injury; potential for a thrombophilic state and predisposition to venous thromboembolism |
| **Preoperative mortality risk assessment tools** | CTP score, MELD score, ASA Physical Status Classification, Mayo Postoperative Mortality Risk Calculator in Patients with Cirrhosis |
| **Postoperative complications more common in / unique to cirrhosis** | Hepatic decompensation and worsening of liver synthetic function; acute worsening of portal hypertension and associated complications, including ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and portal hypertensive bleeding; wound healing difficulties and dehiscence; pulmonary complications — pleural effusions and pneumonia; other infections, including bacterial peritonitis; increased risk of intraoperative and perioperative bleeding; multiple organ failure |

## Risk Scores — Components and Definitions (Table 2)

**CTP score**

| Parameter | 1 point | 2 points | 3 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Encephalopathy grade | None | Stage 1–2 | Stage 3–4 |
| Ascites level | Absent | Slight | Moderate or severe |
| Serum albumin (g/dL) | >3.5 | 2.8–3.5 | <2.8 |
| Total bilirubin (mg/dL) | <2 | 2–3 | >3 |
| INR | <1.7 | 1.7–2.3 | >2.3 |

Total points: **5–6 = class A; 7–9 = class B; 10–15 = class C.**

**ASA Physical Status Classification** (as printed here, classes 1–6)

| Class | Description |
|---|---|
| 1 | Normal healthy patient |
| 2 | Mild systemic disease |
| 3 | Severe systemic disease that is not life-threatening |
| 4 | Severe systemic disease that is a constant threat to life |
| 5 | Moribund and not expected to survive without the operation |
| 6 | Brain-dead patient/organ donor |

**MELD score** (formula as printed)

> MELD = 3.78 × ln (serum bilirubin in mg/dL) + 11.2 × ln (INR) + 9.57 × ln (serum creatinine in mg/dL) + 6.43

- Final score is **rounded to the nearest whole number**
- Patients on **renal replacement therapy are assigned a serum creatinine value of 4.0 mg/dL**

**Mayo Postoperative Surgical Risk Score** — an online calculator combining **MELD score, ASA class, and age** (with etiology of liver disease), predicting mortality at **7 days, 30 days, 90 days, 1 year, and in the long term**. Its advantage over MELD alone is the inclusion of age and ASA class, giving greater granularity and predictive value. **MELD-Na has not been studied as a predictor of surgical outcomes in cirrhosis.**

| Lumbar spine surgery | CTP score <6 |
| Intracranial neurosurgery | CTP score (continuous risk) |
| Head and neck cancer resections | Child-Pugh class A; MELD score <10 |

## Key Findings / Claims

**Risk prediction**

- Severity of liver disease is **the most important single factor** determining postoperative mortality risk; the others are comorbidities, type of procedure, and expertise of the management team.
- **Futility** must be part of the decision: patients categorized preoperatively as **ASA class V** had a median survival of **2 days, 90% mortality at 14 days, and 100% mortality at 90 days**.
- **CTP:** class A patients may generally undergo surgery **in the absence of thrombocytopenia or clinically significant portal hypertension**; **selected** class B patients are elective candidates; **class C patients are not considered candidates for elective surgery**. Modern analyses show **less** correlation between Child-Pugh class and surgical outcomes, partly because few class C patients now undergo surgery.
- The CTP score has been **largely supplanted by MELD** for surgical risk stratification because MELD uses objective variables; however, **MELD is less effective in the patient with severe portal hypertension but preserved synthetic function — there CTP may be the more useful tool.** Ascites (a CTP component absent from MELD), especially massive ascites, correlates with poor surgical outcomes.
- Preoperative **MELD <16** is associated with lower postoperative mortality than higher scores; mortality rises **linearly** with MELD. Higher **ASA class and age >70 years** were also predictive.
- **Emergency surgery** carries at least a **2-fold increased relative risk** of morbidity and mortality that is **not directly represented** by most MELD-based predictive models.
- **No studies have compared anesthesia techniques** in cirrhosis; the agent is chosen case-by-case, apart from strict avoidance of outdated hepatotoxic inhalational agents. Spinal and epidural anesthesia reduce hepatic blood flow, but no comparative data exist.

**Procedure-specific**

- **Hepatic resection for HCC** is typically confined to **BCLC stage 0 (early) HCC** — tumor **<2 cm**, excellent performance status, and normal liver function (**Child-Pugh class A**) without portal hypertension. Surrogates for absent CSPH: **platelet count >100,000/µL**, **HVPG <10 mm Hg**, absence of venous collaterals on cross-sectional imaging plus absence of esophageal varices on endoscopy. **Liver stiffness <22 kPa** was the optimal cutoff in one series (≥22 kPa predicted postoperative decompensation); **much lower cutoffs, 12–15 kPa, have also been proposed**, and optimal values are not validated. **MELD <9** also selects patients with excellent post-resection prognosis.
- **Cholecystectomy:** a **thickened gallbladder wall** in cirrhosis with ascites usually reflects fibrotic thickening, not acute cholecystitis — misdiagnosis is common. Elective cholecystectomy should be **delayed** in transplant candidates. **Percutaneous cholecystostomy is generally not an option** in CTP class C or refractory ascites (ascites, infection, and procedural complication risk); **endoscopic transpapillary gallbladder drainage** may be used in the noncritically ill if technically feasible and expertise is available. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is associated with **higher mortality in cirrhosis than in noncirrhotic patients**, and with an increased need for conversion to open.
- **Abdominal wall hernia:** ascites control is the key to successful repair. **A single large-volume paracentesis before surgery does not provide adequate preoperative control** — use **medical control with diuretics** before elective repair in decompensated cirrhosis. Selected candidates may benefit from **preemptive TIPS**. Postoperatively, avoid recurrent ascites with frequent paracentesis and careful diuretics while avoiding renal injury; in selected patients, **transient pigtail catheter drainage** may prevent pressure on the repair. **Incarcerated hernia is the most common indication for emergency surgery** in this population and a major risk factor for poor outcomes.
- **Cardiovascular surgery:** elective CABG mortality ranges **4%–70%**, correlating with MELD and Child-Pugh class. **A MELD score ≥13.5 or a CTP score >7 is considered a contraindication to cardiac surgery.** Transcatheter vs surgical aortic valve replacement showed **no significant mortality difference** in one small study. Coronary angiography and cardiac catheterization appear relatively safe. **Transfusion of plasma to prophylactically "correct" the INR before cardiac catheterization cannot be recommended.** Dual antiplatelet therapy after coronary stenting is associated with **increased variceal and nonvariceal bleeding**.
- **Bariatric surgery:** may be carried out at expert centers in **compensated cirrhosis without CSPH**; complication risk is still increased versus the general population. Patients with **decompensated cirrhosis or CSPH are at highest risk and are not candidates**. **Sleeve gastrectomy at the time of liver transplantation** in highly selected patients produces durable weight loss and lower risk of post-transplant metabolic syndrome; it adds no significant morbidity to the transplant and, unlike bypass, **causes no intestinal malabsorption** (which would threaten immunosuppression absorption) and **preserves endoscopic access to the biliary tree**.

**Perioperative management**

- **Elective operations should generally be avoided until the liver disease can be optimized.** Progressive decompensation after surgery is well described even in previously fully compensated cirrhosis.
- For chronic viral hepatitis and autoimmune hepatitis, **strict adherence to antiviral/immunosuppressive therapy is required**; elective or semielective surgery may be delayed until therapy is completed to avoid interrupting it. Alternative routes (e.g. **IV corticosteroids** in autoimmune hepatitis) may be needed when oral intake is not possible.
- **Ascites:** in symptomatic ascites, perform **large-volume total abdominal paracentesis preoperatively with IV albumin 6–8 g for each liter of ascitic fluid removed**. Once oral medications are tolerated, **resume daily antibiotic prophylaxis** in patients with a history of SBP and in those with a low ascitic total protein concentration; when oral intake is not possible, give a **third-generation cephalosporin such as ceftriaxone 1 g every 24 hours intravenously** until oral prophylaxis can resume. **Preoperative thoracentesis only for moderate-to-large pleural effusions that compromise respiratory function.** **Limit IV fluid and blood products perioperatively** to avoid increasing extracellular volume, ascites, and portal venous pressure.
- **Preemptive TIPS before surgery cannot be recommended as routine practice** — unproven efficacy, uncontrolled published evidence, potentially high complication rates, significant added expense. A misplaced TIPS (right atrium, IVC, or mesenteric veins) can **adversely affect future liver transplantation**.
- **Hemostasis:** protocol transfusion strategies based on preoperative platelet count and INR are **generally ineffective** at reducing intra- and postoperative bleeding. Cirrhosis is a **"rebalanced"** state; progressive protein C deficiency creates a **thrombophilic** state in some patients. **Prophylactic preoperative FFP is not recommended.** **Platelet counts above 50,000/µL are adequate for clot formation in most patients**; raising platelets higher is unlikely to help and exposes the patient to transfusion complications, volume overload, or unexpected thrombosis. **Fibrinogen <100 mg/dL** is associated with inhibition of clot formation; **cryoprecipitate** (low volume) is the physiologically reasonable replacement before high-risk procedures, although no controlled trials exist.
- **TPO receptor agonists:** only **avatrombopag and lusutrombopag** are approved for thrombocytopenia related to liver disease; both raised platelets above 50,000/µL in phase 3 trials before invasive procedures. **Bleeding event rates were equivalent between drug and placebo arms** in both pivotal studies, raising questions about the need to elevate the platelet count for most invasive procedures. **Thrombotic events (including portal vein thrombosis and DVT) have occurred** with these agents in liver disease. There is **no clear guidance on preoperative use**, but it is reasonable to consider them for an **elective procedure with a high bleeding risk when the baseline platelet count is <50,000/µL**.
- **Viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM)** decreases red blood cell and plasma transfusion in the general surgical population and specifically in cirrhosis; if available, consider it before and during surgical procedures to guide a rational transfusion strategy.
- **Pain control:** opiates at **lower doses and longer dosing intervals**; **hydromorphone and transdermal fentanyl may be preferred**; only short-acting benzodiazepines (**midazolam**). **Tramadol**, which has favorable hepatic metabolism, or **acetaminophen in doses of no more than 2 g daily**, is reasonable — acetaminophen is **not** contraindicated in cirrhosis even in small doses, despite the misconception. **Avoid routine NSAIDs** (impair renal blood flow). **Oral combination products containing acetaminophen or NSAIDs, especially when combined with an opioid, should not be used.**
- **Constipation:** avoid it to minimize postoperative HE flares. **Polyethylene glycol solution may be used daily** to maintain bowel regularity. Absent comparative trials, **rifaximin may be preferable to lactulose in patients with HE undergoing primary bowel surgery** because it causes less bowel distension.
- **Rescue liver transplantation:** determine **before** surgery whether the patient is a future transplant candidate, and make that position clear to family and team. Elective operations (e.g. orthopedic) may be best deferred while the patient is waitlisted. **If postoperative mortality risk is calculated to be >15% at 3 months (using an appropriate risk calculator) or the MELD score is >15, preoperative liver transplant evaluation is recommended.**

**Internal inconsistencies within this document** (both quoted as printed):

- **Liver stiffness cutoff for hepatic resection.** BPA 6 gives "hepatic transient elastography values of **<23 kPa**" as the surrogate for absent CSPH, while Table 3 and the body text both give **<22 kPa** (with ≥22 kPa predicting postoperative decompensation). The body text also notes that **much lower cutoffs of 12–15 kPa have been proposed** and that **optimal values have not been validated**.
- **MELD threshold for preoperative transplant evaluation.** BPA 14 states "when the MELD score is **≥15**"; the *Rescue Liver Transplantation* section states "if the MELD score is **>15**."

**Open questions acknowledged by the authors**

- No prospective trials guide evaluation or treatment of the surgical patient with cirrhosis; the literature is limited in scope, usually lacks risk-matched controls, and is largely single-center — so evidence-based recommendations are "mostly lacking."
- The MELD cutoff for selecting patients for preoperative transplant evaluation "is not clear."
- How much cirrhosis-attributable risk is **additive** to traditional cardiopulmonary risk factors is not established.
- No studies compare anesthesia techniques, or minimally invasive versus open approaches with risk-adjusted controls, in cirrhosis.
- Whether nonhepatic comorbid risk factors are additive or multiplicative with liver disease severity has not been studied.
