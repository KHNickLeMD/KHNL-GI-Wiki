## Summary

Expert commentary on **gastric peroral endoscopic myotomy (G-POEM**, also called peroral endoscopic pyloromyotomy**)** for medically refractory [[gastroparesis]]. Medically refractory gastroparesis = "persistent symptoms in the context of objectively confirmed gastric emptying delay, despite the use of dietary adjustment and metoclopramide as a first-line therapeutic agent." G-POEM — a natural extension of [[endoscopic-submucosal-dissection|ESD]] and esophageal [[poem|POEM]] (first described in a porcine model by Kawai 2012; first human case by Khashab 2013) — is efficacious for **diabetic, post-surgical, and idiopathic** gastroparesis.

The core selection advice: offer G-POEM to adults with refractory gastroparesis who have (1) EGD excluding mechanical gastric-outlet obstruction, (2) solid-phase gastric emptying scan (GES) confirming delay — preferably **retention >20% at 4 hours**, and (3) **moderate-to-severe symptoms** (GCSI total >2), preferably **nausea/vomiting-dominant**. Baseline GCSI >2.6 and baseline 4-hour retention >20% independently predict 12-month clinical success; pain-predominant patients respond less well; most **post-infectious** gastroparesis should *not* be offered G-POEM; nonselective use is discouraged. Durable 1-year clinical success is in the **50%–60% range** (pooled 1-year 61%) — more modest than earlier pooled short-term estimates of 71%–82% — but a European sham-controlled RCT showed clear superiority of G-POEM over sham (71% vs 22% at 6 months, ITT). AEs are uncommon (6.2%–14%; most mild). The commentary also covers technique (greater-curvature approach, full-thickness pyloromyotomy sparing serosa, closure), post-procedural care (diet ladder, 8 weeks PPI), follow-up (GCSI each visit; GES no sooner than 4–8 weeks), the double-myotomy variation, and the lack of an effective salvage strategy after failed G-POEM.

## Key Findings / Claims

*No numbered BPA statements in this CPU format; the following captures every "we suggest/advise" near-verbatim, grouped by section.*

**Patient selection:**

- G-POEM should be considered for patients with **medically refractory gastroparesis** (persistent symptoms + objectively confirmed gastric-emptying delay despite dietary adjustment and metoclopramide first-line).
- Efficacious for gastroparesis from **diabetes, prior surgery, and idiopathic** causes.
- **We suggest offering G-POEM to adult patients with refractory gastroparesis who:** (1) have undergone esophagogastroduodenoscopy to confirm **no mechanical gastric-outlet obstruction**; (2) had a **solid-phase gastric emptying scan (GES)** confirming delayed gastric emptying, preferably with **retention >20% at 4 hours**; and (3) have **moderate-to-severe symptoms**, preferably with **nausea and vomiting as the dominant symptoms**.
- Normal gastric retention at 4 hours is **<10%** (scintigraphy with low-fat [2%] chicken-egg-white meal). Retention >20% at 4 h suggested as the offer threshold — baseline retention >20% at 4 h was an independent predictor of clinical success in a large prospective study.
- **GCSI**: scale 0 (none)–5 (very severe), 2-week recall, 9 symptoms in 3 subscales — nausea/vomiting (3 items), postprandial fullness/early satiety (4 items), bloating (2 items); total score = average of the 3 subscale means. Moderate-to-severe symptoms often correspond to **GCSI total >2**.
- Patients who failed gastric electrical stimulation, pyloric stenting, or botulinum toxin injection should also be offered G-POEM — **failure of these therapies is not a prerequisite**. Published studies do not inform whether improvement after botulinum toxin or transpyloric stenting predicts G-POEM success.
- **Opioid dependence:** wean off opioids whenever possible and re-evaluate gastric emptying first.
- Most patients with **post-infectious gastroparesis should not be offered G-POEM** (usually self-limited).
- The ultimate decision should be based on the **full clinical picture**, not GES results or GCSI score alone. Nausea/vomiting-predominant patients are more likely to respond; **abdominal-pain-predominant patients are less likely to respond**.
- Pyloric **cross-sectional area and distensibility index** on [[flip-panometry|impedance planimetry (FLIP)]] may have a role in patient selection (data limited). **Nonselective use of G-POEM should be discouraged.**
- **Pediatrics:** insufficient evidence for G-POEM in children with gastroparesis (though used for congenital hypertrophic pyloric stenosis). **Post-lung-transplant** severe gastroparesis: emerging evidence, promising minimally invasive option.

**Table 1 — Potential causes of gastroparesis:**

| Cause | Details |
|---|---|
| Endocrine | Diabetes accounts for ~25% of gastroparesis cases |
| Post-surgical | Typically partial/complete vagotomy, intended or unintended (esophagectomy, Billroth II gastrectomy, fundoplication or hernia repair); symptomatic gastroparesis is a common adverse event after lung or heart–lung transplantation (may promote microaspiration into the allograft → graft failure) |
| Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty | May delay gastric emptying by constraining the middle and distal stomach |
| Medications | Anticholinergics, GLP-1 receptor agonists; opioids induce pyloric dysfunction and gastric stasis — a common, iatrogenic, potentially reversible cause |
| Post-viral | Usually self-limited; course can be prolonged in some |
| Idiopathic | The most common form |

**Procedural considerations:**

- G-POEM should **only be performed by interventional endoscopists with expertise or training in third-space endoscopy**; ESD experience not mandatory but shortens the learning curve; E-POEM + ESD expertise immensely beneficial.
- Prep: **liquid diet ≥24 hours** pre-procedure; **periprocedural antibiotics** with efficacy against enteric pathogens; **general anesthesia**, supine or left-lateral (authors favor **supine** — easier examination for capnoperitoneum; tension pneumoperitoneum managed with percutaneous needle decompression).
- Equipment: high-definition gastroscope with waterjet + clear distal cap; **low-flow CO₂ insufflation required — room air completely avoided**; modern electrosurgical generator modulating power by tissue resistance/impedance.
- Steps: submucosal bleb **4–5 cm proximal to the pylorus**, typically **greater curvature** (saline + ~0.25% indigo carmine/methylene blue) → longitudinal or transverse **1.5–2 cm mucosal incision** → submucosal tunnel by repeated injection/dissection until the **pyloric ring is exposed** (blended-cutting current for incision, blended-coagulation for tunnel; hemostatic forceps for large vascular complexes; intermittent stomach deflation reduces looping) → **single myotomy** extended **1–3 cm proximally into the antrum**; insulated-tip knife may increase the safety margin (difficult situations / early learning curve). **At minimum incise the inner circular muscularis; authors advise full-thickness pyloromyotomy of the circular and oblique bundles with preservation of the serosa** (do not breach serosa) → close mucosal incision with **endoscopic clips or suturing**. Fluoroscopy optional, not mandatory.
- **Variations:** lesser-curvature approach described with similar outcomes but pyloromyotomy is more challenging. **Double myotomy** (2nd incision near the 1st) superior to single myotomy at 6 months in a single study; technically easy, adds little time — but additional long-term comparative data required before deeming it routinely necessary.

**Post-procedural care:**

- Often admitted overnight. If no AEs → clear-liquid diet. Optional upper-GI study next day to exclude contrast leak → advance to full liquids. **Considerable abdominal pain, tachycardia, and/or fever → CT or upper-GI study.**
- Generally: **full-liquid diet 5–7 days**, then **5–6 small meals/day of a low-fiber, low-fat solid gastroparesis diet**.
- **8 weeks of PPI** (mucosal healing) + a short course of oral antibiotics suggested.
- **Same-day discharge** possible at experienced centers for reliable patients with ED access if: high-confidence mucosal closure, no intraoperative AEs, no major comorbidities, pain controlled with oral meds, liquids tolerated without nausea/vomiting.
- **Aspirin** with strong indication may be continued irrespective of G-POEM; resumption of clopidogrel/warfarin/other antithrombotics individualized.

**Follow-up and efficacy definitions:**

- Clinic follow-up ~**1–3 months**; **repeat solid-phase GES no sooner than 4–8 weeks** post-G-POEM; obtain **GCSI before G-POEM and at each visit**.
- **Clinical success** (recent definition): decrease in total GCSI ≥1, typically with **>25% decrease in ≥2 GCSI subscales**. **Recurrence**: clinical return of gastroparesis symptoms + GCSI back to baseline or ≥3 after initial response. Desirable signs: relief of nausea/vomiting, increased meal size, weight gain.

**Efficacy data:**

- Pooled meta-analyses (largely short-term): clinical success **71%–82%**; recent meta-analysis: **1-year pooled clinical success only 61%**; French multicenter retrospective: **66% at 1 year**; long-term retrospective (4–5 y): only **50% sustained efficacy**. Durable clinical success is in the **50%–60% range** — still a major benefit given the paucity of effective gastroparesis therapies.
- **International prospective study (n=80,** all failed prokinetics, 70% also failed botulinum toxin and/or transpyloric stenting; mean 4-h retention 39%; etiology idiopathic 41%, postsurgical 35%, diabetic 24%**):** greater-curve G-POEM, **100% technical success**, median 43 min; at 12 months clinical success **56%**, GCSI <2 in 68%; total GCSI 2.8 → 1.5 (P < .001); success consistent across etiologic subtypes. Follow-up GES (66% of pts, 3 mo): improved 64%, **normalized 47%**. Multivariable **independent predictors of 12-month success: baseline GCSI >2.6 (OR 3.23; P = .04) and baseline 4-h retention >20% (OR 3.65; P = .029)**.
- Single-center retrospective (n=97): 81.1% response at 3–6 months but **loss of response 12.9% per year**.
- **Sham-controlled RCT** (European, single-blinded, n=41: 17 diabetic, 13 post-surgical, 11 idiopathic; terminated early for G-POEM superiority): success (GCSI decrease ≥50% at 6 mo) **71% G-POEM vs 22% sham** (P = .001; OR 9.0 favoring G-POEM, P = .005); by GCSI decrease ≥1 definition, **95% vs 37%**. **Diabetic gastroparesis had the highest success (89%)**. 4-h retention fell 22% → 12% after G-POEM (no change post-sham). 12 sham patients crossed over; 75% achieved success.
- Mexico City retrospective (n=374; 102 completed 48 months): **4-year clinical success 77.5%**; diabetic gastroparesis had the best long-term outcomes and fewest recurrences.
- A US sham-controlled trial (NCT04869670) in diabetic/idiopathic gastroparesis is planned.

**Adverse events:**

- Uncommon when performed by trained/experienced endoscopists, but serious AEs occur.
- International prospective (n=80): AEs **6.2%, all mild** — capnoperitoneum ×3 (needle decompression), mucosotomy ×1 (stented), thermal mucosal injury ×1 (clipped).
- Retrospective n=216: AEs **14%** (77% mild, 16% moderate, 6% severe); most common abdominal pain (n=16), inadvertent mucosotomy (n=5), capnoperitoneum (n=4); the 2 severe = post-procedural pain → negative exploratory laparoscopy, and a 2-mm anterior stomach/pylorus perforation → laparoscopic omental patch.
- Retrospective n=374: AEs **8.6%; bleeding most common**; all managed endoscopically/conservatively. Also reported: gastric ulceration, dumping syndrome.

**After failed G-POEM:**

- A sizable minority will not achieve satisfactory response; **redo G-POEM is very challenging (dense fibrosis)** and data are sparse. Gastric electrical stimulation might be attempted if not already failed (no published guidance). **No proven medical or minimally invasive salvage strategy** → set realistic expectations; refine patient selection; intraprocedural impedance planimetry may help gauge myotomy adequacy.

**Comparative data (supplementary):**

- vs **laparoscopic pyloroplasty** (propensity-matched, 30 vs 30): LP had longer stay (4.6 vs 1.4 d; P = .003), longer operative time (99.3 vs 33.9 min; P < .001), more AEs (16.7% vs 3.3%; P = .086) — comparable functional outcomes, less perioperative morbidity with G-POEM.
- Meta-analysis (332 G-POEM vs 376 surgical pyloroplasty): pooled GCSI-based clinical success **76% vs 77%** (NS); comparable AEs.
- vs **gastric electrical stimulation** (propensity-matched 23 vs 23): G-POEM had better and longer-lasting clinical response.

**Other:**

- Many insurers still consider G-POEM investigational; authors state safety/effectiveness are now well supported and payors should cover it.
- Future: refine selection, prognostic ancillary tests (e.g., intraprocedural FLIP); G-POEM enables gastric muscularis propria biopsy via the tunnel (interstitial-cells-of-Cajal research).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Durability vs earlier pooled estimates:** [[acg-2022-gastroparesis]] cites pooled open-label clinical improvement of 88.2% (n=276), whereas this CPU emphasizes more modest durable success — 1-year pooled **61%**, 4–5-year sustained **50%** (both flagged on [[gastroparesis]]). Not a formal contradiction (different endpoints/time horizons), but expectations should be set with the 50%–60% durable figure.
- **Double myotomy:** superior at 6 months in a single study only — not yet routine.
- No validated predictor from response to prior botulinum toxin/stenting; FLIP-based selection promising but unproven.
