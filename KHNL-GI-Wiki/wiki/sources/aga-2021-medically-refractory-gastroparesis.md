## Summary

The first tier-1 source written specifically about **medically refractory** gastroparesis — the patient who has already failed the first-line pathway. Its central move is to supply the definition that the label had been missing: *"no precise definition or dedicated treatment algorithm for this diagnosis exists in the literature."*

The update's governing idea is that **the diagnosis itself is frequently wrong**, so escalation to invasive therapy is often escalation against a mimic. Two facts carry this. First, gastroparesis and functional dyspepsia are substantially interchangeable over time: on repeat testing, **42% of gastroparesis patients were reclassified as FD and 37% of FD patients as gastroparesis within a year**. Second, **gastric emptying scans are commonly performed incorrectly**, with short measurement times causing misdiagnosis and mismanagement — hence BPA 2's insistence on verifying the methodology of the study before believing it. The clinical consequence is stated bluntly in the Summary: *"Pursuing invasive therapeutic options on the basis of a single GES without clinical context may close the door on potentially effective management options targeting FD and other mimics of gastroparesis."*

From there the update is organized around **symptom-predominant phenotyping** (BPA 4): patients are split into **nausea/vomiting predominant** and **abdominal pain/discomfort predominant**, and treatment follows the predominant symptom rather than the emptying number — justified because *"there are no prospective, randomized controlled studies comparing different management strategies (eg, a central anti-emetic vs a prokinetic agent)."* Table 1 then supplies a complete dose list for both arms.

The two invasive options are permitted but fenced. **GES (BPA 7)** is for refractory/intractable nausea and vomiting in patients who failed standard therapy and are **not on opioids**; persistent abdominal pain is explicitly **not** an indication and opioid use is a **contraindication**. **G-POEM (BPA 8)** is for select patients with **severe** emptying delay, at a center of excellence with a motility-specialist plus advanced-endoscopist team; it *"should not be considered first-line therapy."* Two other pylorus-directed options are pushed away entirely: available data *"argue against use of botulinum toxin in refractory gastroparesis, except in clinical trials,"* and transpyloric stenting *"should be considered investigational."*

## Key Findings / Claims

### The 8 Best Practice Advice statements (verbatim, numbered as in the source)

The source prints these as a single "BEST PRACTICE ADVICE" block of eight numbered items. **No evidence grade or strength accompanies any of them** (see Methodology above).

> **1.** Clinicians should review symptoms and evaluate physical examination findings to exclude disorders that can mimic medically refractory gastroparesis.

> **2.** Clinicians should verify appropriate methodology of the gastric emptying study to ensure an accurate diagnosis of delayed gastric emptying.

> **3.** Clinicians should classify patients with gastroparesis into mild, moderate, or severe based on symptoms and the results of a properly performed gastric emptying study.

> **4.** Clinicians should identify the predominant symptom and initiate treatment based on that symptom.

> **5.** Clinicians should be aware of the multiple treatment options to treat nausea and vomiting.

> **6.** Clinicians should consider the use of neuromodulators to treat gastroparesis associated abdominal pain but should not use opioids.

> **7.** Clinicians can consider gastric electrical stimulation for gastroparesis patients with refractory/intractable nausea and vomiting who have failed standard therapy and are not on opioids.

> **8.** Clinicians can consider G-POEM for select refractory gastroparesis patients with severe delay in gastric emptying, using a thoughtful team approach involving motility specialists and advanced endoscopists at a center of excellence.

### Definition of medically refractory gastroparesis — with the trial durations that make it operative

This is the source's headline contribution, and the **durations are the decision**:

| Component | What the source requires |
|---|---|
| **Symptoms** | **Persistent** symptoms — generally nausea and vomiting predominate, *"although all symptoms should be considered"* |
| **Objective test** | **Objectively confirmed gastric emptying delay** |
| **Failed dietary trial** | **Small particle size, reduced fat diet** for a **minimum of 4 weeks** (based on a single trial that formally studied dietary manipulation in gastroparesis) |
| **Failed drug trial** | **Metoclopramide** — *"a minimum of 10 mg three times daily before meals and at bedtime for at least 4 weeks"*; the only FDA-approved medication for gastroparesis. This duration is *"based on limited data and no agreed upon standards"*; evidence from RCTs to support longer interventions **is not available** |
| **Proviso (exclusionary)** | Symptoms are **not medication induced** — e.g. **opioids, GLP-1 agonists** |

- Metoclopramide black box warning must be known, *"although the risk of tardive dyskinesia from chronic metoclopramide use may be lower than previously estimated by regulatory authorities."*

### BPA 1 — physical examination findings that identify a mimic

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| **Succussion splash** | Delayed gastric emptying **or** gastric outlet obstruction |
| **Bruit on auscultation of the right upper quadrant** | **Celiac artery compression syndrome** |
| **Digital ulcers and telangiectasia** | **Scleroderma** |
| **Ascites, a mass, or enlarged lymph nodes** | **Underlying malignancy** |

- Baseline labs if not recently performed: **complete blood count, liver chemistries, basic metabolic profile.** Correct electrolyte derangements (common with persistent nausea/vomiting). **TSH** if hypothyroidism is a concern. **Hyperkalemia + metabolic acidosis may indicate adrenal insufficiency** → initially evaluated with a **fasting cortisol**.
- **Upper endoscopy should be performed to rule out an organic cause of symptoms.**
- Mimics named in the Figure 2 legend that must be differentiated: **celiac artery compression syndrome** (initially evaluated with a **mesenteric duplex**), **superior mesenteric artery syndrome** (radiologic imaging — small bowel follow-through or CT enterography), **intestinal pseudo-obstruction** (symptoms, labs, imaging), and *"in particular"* **cyclic vomiting syndrome and cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome**.

### BPA 2 — the gastric emptying study must be verified, not just read

- Gastric emptying can be measured by **scintigraphy, ¹³C-spirulina breath test, or wireless motility capsule**; most U.S. centers use **gastric scintigraphy**, *"albeit often incorrectly with short measurement times, leading to misdiagnosis and mismanagement."*
- Joint **American Nuclear Medicine Society / American Neurogastroenterology and Motility Society** guidelines outline key protocol requirements for an accurate **4-hour** test, *"optimally performed off opioid medication."*
- **Refer preferentially to centers that adhere to those guidelines.**
- **A meal-based test is recommended first-line over the wireless motility capsule**, because the capsule — *"an inanimate object"* — identifies the phase III activity front of the migrating motor complex rather than overall gastric emptying.
- **Repeat testing reclassifies the diagnosis in 37%–42% within a year** (gastroparesis ↔ FD, in both directions).

### BPA 3 — severity classification by 4-hour scintigraphic retention

*The source attributes this to "some investigators" and states it is **"not validated in large, prospective studies,"** but it is the only numeric severity banding given and BPA 8 conditions G-POEM on "severe delay."*

| Severity | Gastric retention at 4 hours on scintigraphy |
|---|---|
| **Mild** | **10%–15%** |
| **Moderate** | **15%–35%** |
| **Severe** | **>35%** |

- ⚠ **The bands as printed overlap at 15%** (mild `10%–15%`, moderate `15%–35%`); the source does not state which side 15% falls on. Do not silently resolve this — see *Text-extraction and source ambiguities*.
- Severity should be classified *"based on symptoms **and** the results of a properly performed gastric emptying study"* (BPA 3) — not the scan alone.
- Symptom severity is scored with a validated instrument such as the **GCSI**, *"however, overlap with FD makes this less reliable than previously thought."*

### BPA 4 — phenotype into two arms, then treat the predominant symptom

Figure 2's two categories: **nausea/vomiting predominant** and **abdominal pain/discomfort predominant**. The pathway *"assumes that anatomic/organic causes for symptoms have been ruled out with upper endoscopy and selective imaging."* Management options then depend on *"the degree of patient-reported symptoms and/or the degree of gastric emptying delay (mild, moderate, and severe) on a 4-hour gastric emptying scan."*

Rationale for symptom-first rather than physiology-first: *"Because there are no prospective, randomized controlled studies comparing different management strategies (eg, a central anti-emetic vs a prokinetic agent), initiating treatment based on the predominant presenting symptom is a reasonable first approach."* And: *"Simply accelerating gastric emptying may not improve global gastroparesis symptoms."*

### BPA 5 — agent-by-agent detail for nausea and vomiting

| Agent / class | What the source states |
|---|---|
| **Domperidone** (D2 antagonist) | Does **not** readily cross the blood-brain barrier → **fewer central side effects than metoclopramide**; risks are **QT prolongation and ventricular tachycardia**. US availability **only through an FDA investigational drug application**. Recommended **starting dose 10 mg 3×/day**; escalation to 20 mg 4×/day has been reported but *"should probably be avoided for cardiovascular safety considerations."* Single-center cohort (n = 115): **68% improved symptom scores**, **7% had cardiac side effects requiring drug cessation.** Published efficacy is **modest**, and studied patients were **not defined a priori as refractory** |
| **5-HT3 antagonists** (ondansetron, granisetron) | Block serotonin receptors in the chemoreceptor trigger zone and inhibit vagal afferents. **Similar efficacy across agents — select by price, availability, and mode of delivery.** Ondansetron parenteral + enteral; granisetron liquid, tablet, transdermal patch. **Transdermal granisetron (3.1 mg/24 h) decreased symptom scores by 50%** in refractory gastroparesis symptoms |
| **NK-1 antagonists** (aprepitant, tradipitant, casopitant, rolapitant) | Block substance P in the nucleus tractus solitarius and area postrema. **Aprepitant RCT (n = 126, 125 mg/day vs placebo):** improved nausea and vomiting **by GCSI but not by visual analog score** of nausea intensity. **Tradipitant RCT (85 mg vs placebo, 4 weeks, diabetic or idiopathic):** improved nausea — *"especially in idiopathic gastroparesis"* — vomiting and overall GCSI also improved. ⚠ Symptoms improved **regardless of whether emptying was delayed** (gastroparesis) or not (FD). **Up to one-third of patients with troublesome nausea may benefit, "provided costs are affordable"** |
| **Phenothiazine antipsychotics** (prochlorperazine, chlorpromazine) | Inhibit dopamine receptors in the brain, but **have not been studied in gastroparesis** or compared prospectively with other anti-emetics |
| **Levosulpiride** (substituted benzamide antipsychotic, D2 blocking) | Controlled trial in **40 diabetic gastroparesis patients** showed significant improvement of **symptoms as well as gastric emptying** |
| **Scopolamine** | Muscarinic cholinergic antagonist, used **off-label despite lack of supporting clinical studies** |
| **Synthetic cannabinoids** (dronabinol, nabilone) | Approved for chemotherapy-related nausea/vomiting; **use in gastroparesis has not been formally evaluated, with the potential to slow gastric emptying** |
| **Ginger** | Improves nausea and vomiting but **has not been prospectively evaluated in refractory gastroparesis** |
| **Mirtazapine** | Improved refractory nausea and vomiting in a cohort of **30 gastroparesis patients**; improved weight loss, dyspeptic symptoms and **especially early satiation** in a controlled trial in FD |

**Medications to accelerate gastric emptying:**

- **Erythromycin** — macrolide binding **motilin receptors**, stimulating antral cholinergic activity and initiating phase III MMC contractions. **IV in hospitalized patients: 3 mg/kg every 8 hours.** **Oral in outpatients: 50–100 mg 4 times a day, given 30–45 minutes before each of the 3 main meals and at bedtime.** Limited by **tachyphylaxis**. ⚠ *"Higher oral doses may cause early satiation and pain and may exacerbate nausea and vomiting."*
- **Azithromycin** — also accelerates gastric emptying, but **may prolong QT and increase arrhythmia risk, similar to erythromycin.**
- **5-HT4 agonists** — stimulate peristalsis via acetylcholine release from the myenteric plexus. **Cisapride** was effective in some patients despite lack of RCT data but **was removed from the market for adverse cardiac effects**. **Velusetrag** accelerated emptying in a large phase 2 RCT without apparent cardiac side effects, but **no phase 3 RCTs announced**. **Prucalopride** accelerated emptying and improved symptoms and QoL in both diabetic and idiopathic gastroparesis in a **small** RCT; large multicenter trials needed.
- **Relamorelin** (selective ghrelin agonist) — improved core symptoms and accelerated emptying in an RCT of **393 diabetics** diagnosed by ¹³C-spirulina breath test, **but without improvement in vomiting vs placebo**; *"further prospective trials have been placed on hold"* and whether the small emptying change is clinically meaningful *"remains unanswered."*

### BPA 6 — neuromodulators for pain; opioids prohibited

- **Mechanism basis:** TCAs and SNRIs reduce pain perception at different levels of the brain–gut axis. **Noradrenaline reuptake inhibition, as provided by TCAs and SNRIs, is considered the main mechanism for controlling visceral pain.**
- ⚠ **The direct evidence in gastroparesis is negative.** High-quality evidence is limited to a **single placebo-controlled RCT — the NORIG trial** of **nortriptyline** (a secondary tricyclic amine). Using a strict primary outcome of **50% reduction in 2 consecutive GCSI assessments** vs baseline, **there was no difference** between tailored nortriptyline (adjusted at 3-week intervals up to 75 mg at 2 weeks) and placebo.
- The argument for trying **tertiary** amines anyway: because of the gastroparesis–FD overlap, **amitriptyline and imipramine "may potentially provide greater benefits, particularly in diabetic gastroparesis,"** though **prospective RCT data in gastroparesis are lacking**. In the FD treatment trial, **amitriptyline improved FD patients without slowing gastric emptying** and was **more effective than escitalopram**, especially when **epigastric pain was a relevant symptom and when gastric emptying was normal**.
- **SSRIs** may improve coexisting anxiety and depression but are **unlikely to directly improve visceral pain**, because they do not block reuptake of norepinephrine.
- **Duloxetine** — improved diabetic polyneuropathic pain vs placebo at **60–120 mg daily over 12 weeks** in RCTs; **nausea or constipation can develop or worsen.**
- **Gabapentin** — systematic review gave **second-tier evidence** that more patients treated at **>1200 mg daily in divided doses** for neuropathic pain achieved at least 50% pain reduction vs placebo; ⚠ *"selective outcome reporting by industry sponsored trials for off-label use has called some of this evidence into question."*
- **Pregabalin** — pooled analysis of **7 RCTs, 1510 patients** with neuropathic pain: statistically significant reduction in mean pain scores over **5–13 weeks** at **150 mg, 300 mg, and 600 mg daily** in divided doses. Side effects: **dizziness, somnolence, weight gain, peripheral edema.**
- **Opioids — do not use (the prohibition half of BPA 6).** *"Finally, opioid analgesics (eg, morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone, etc) should not be used to manage chronic visceral abdominal pain, because they further delay gastric emptying, increase the risk of narcotic bowel syndrome, and create the potential for addiction, tolerance, and overdose."*

### BPA 7 — gastric electrical stimulation: the four conditions

- **FDA approved GES (Enterra Therapy) in 2000**, using **high frequency (12 cycles per minute), low energy** stimuli, for **drug-refractory nausea and vomiting due to gastroparesis**. Approved under a **Humanitarian Device Exemption**.
- **Mechanism remains unknown, and GES does not accelerate gastric emptying** — benefit may occur via modulation of the gastric pacemaker, interstitial cells of Cajal, sensory afferents, other myoneural pathways, or peptide release.
- **Benefits reported:** improves refractory nausea and vomiting in some patients; may improve **glycemic control, nutritional status, and quality of life**, while **reducing hospitalizations and medication use**.

**GES could be an option only when all four hold (source's own conjunction):**

| # | Condition |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Refractory/intractable nausea and vomiting** |
| 2 | **Has failed standard therapy** |
| 3 | **Not on opioids** — opioid use is a **contraindication** |
| 4 | **Abdominal pain is not the predominant symptom** — *"persistent abdominal pain is not an indication for GES"* |

- **Refractory symptoms of shorter duration are more likely to respond than prolonged intractable symptoms.**
- Whether **diabetic** gastroparesis responds better than **idiopathic** *"remains controversial."*
- **Temporary electrical stimulation may predict response to GES and should be offered if available.**
- ⚠ Even though one study reported a reduction in self-reported *"severe"* pain, **pain is still not an indication**.

### BPA 8 — G-POEM, and the two pylorus-directed therapies that are *not* endorsed

**G-POEM (gastric per-oral endoscopic myotomy / gastric per-oral pyloroplasty):**

- **Two separate multicenter trials** noted improvement in symptoms and reduction in gastric emptying times. **Pooled analysis including 8 other open-label and retrospective studies** suggests reduced post-procedure GCSI scores and improved gastric emptying, with **6.8% overall adverse events**.
- ⚠ **Randomized, sham-controlled studies do not exist, and long-term follow-up data are not available.**
- *"G-POEM should not be considered first-line therapy and should only be performed at tertiary care centers using a team approach of experts (motility specialists, advanced endoscopists) with extensive experience in treating refractory gastroparesis patients."*
- **Theoretical potential to induce dumping syndrome**, which has a deleterious effect on food tolerance and quality of life.

**Intrapyloric botulinum toxin injection — argued against:**

- Early studies improved symptoms in diabetic patients, but **two larger placebo-controlled studies showed no benefit over placebo**.
- **No studies have focused on patients with severe emptying delay**, *"which may be the population most likely to benefit."*
- One study suggested benefit in gastroparesis with **decreased pyloric distensibility on FLIP**, but this *"requires further confirmation before recommendation as a means to select patients."*
- Verdict: *"although generally safe, available data argue against use of botulinum toxin in refractory gastroparesis, except in clinical trials."*

**Transpyloric stent placement — investigational:**

- *"Transpyloric stent placement should be considered investigational in refractory gastroparesis for the lack of data from prospective, sham-controlled trials and concerns over stent migration, despite limited case reports describing symptom improvement."*

**Pyloric physiology and FLIP (why patient selection is unsolved):**

- Abnormalities of pyloric tone and pressure (*"pylorospasm"*) and **antro-pyloric dyscoordination** may impair emptying. **Deep pyloric biopsies** have shown **pyloric stenosis and reduced numbers of interstitial cells of Cajal**.
- Measuring pyloric basal tone, phasic pressures and relaxation is difficult — **endoscopy, fluoroscopy and antroduodenal manometry all have significant limitations**.
- **FLIP** uses impedance planimetry to estimate sphincter distensibility and compliance; some studies show **diminished pyloric distensibility** in select gastroparesis patients. ⚠ **FLIP "has not been validated to segregate physiological from pathologic changes in pyloric distensibility across all causes of gastroparesis; it is also expensive, invasive, and not widely available."**

### Other endoscopic and surgical interventions

- **Enteral nutrition** may be required when nausea, vomiting, early satiety and weight loss persist **despite adequate trials of medications and endoscopic therapies**. An endoscopic/surgical **transjejunal tube or combined gastrojejunostomy tube should be placed beyond the pylorus**; case series show weight recovery with acceptable morbidity and mortality, **allowing removal after an average of 20 months**.
- **Parenteral nutrition** may improve symptoms and **provide a bridge to other therapies** in the occasional patient with nutritional compromise.
- **Laparoscopic pyloroplasty or sleeve gastrectomy — role unclear**, because of the absence of large, well-designed, sham-controlled trials.
- **Partial or total gastrectomy is rarely required**, carries a **risk of dumping syndrome**, and *"should be considered only after all available therapies have been exhausted, preferably at a tertiary care center."*

### Epidemiology and overlap with functional dyspepsia

- Estimated prevalence **per 100,000 persons: 37.8 for women and 9.6 for men**; approximately **5 million U.S. adults** have gastroparesis-like symptoms; **7.2% of the global population** report FD symptoms.
- Etiology is diverse with **more than 50 recognized causes**. **Diabetes accounts for 25%**; medications (opioids, GLP-1 agonists), vascular disorders, connective tissue disorders and postsurgical causes are other common causes — **but the largest etiologic group is idiopathic**.
- Pathophysiology beyond delayed emptying: **impaired gastric accommodation, electrical dysrhythmias, antroduodenal dyscoordination, pyloric dysfunction, antral hypomotility, vagal nerve injury, disorders of visceral sensation.**
- **Figure 1** (putative factors generating refractory symptoms) names both central and local gastroduodenal mechanisms: **exaggerated visceral perception, altered central processing, learned behaviors including food aversion, and ongoing psychological distress.**
- Management goals: identify and improve the predominant symptom; **reduce potential for complications (reflux esophagitis, malnutrition, weight loss)**; reduce health care utilization; improve quality of life.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- ⚠ **Two different definitions of "refractory" exist, and they do not match.** [[aga-2025-gastroparesis|AGA 2025]] requires cardinal symptoms predominant **+** delayed emptying **+ failure of at least 2 medical treatments including a prokinetic *and* an antiemetic**. This 2022 CPU requires persistent symptoms **+** confirmed delay **+ failure of a ≥4-week small-particle/low-fat diet *and* a ≥4-week metoclopramide trial**, with medication-induced symptoms excluded. **AGA 2025 is newer and governs the gate**; this CPU is the only source that supplies **how long each first-line trial must run** — a threshold AGA 2025 does not give.
- ⚠ **GES: this CPU is more permissive than AGA 2025.** BPA 7 says clinicians **can consider** GES for refractory/intractable nausea and vomiting off opioids; [[aga-2025-gastroparesis|AGA 2025]] recommends **against routine** GES (conditional, very low certainty). **The 2025 position governs the page.** This CPU's contribution is retained as the **selection criteria** to apply *if* GES is being considered — the four conditions, the opioid contraindication, the "pain is not an indication" rule, and temporary stimulation as a response predictor.
- ⚠ **G-POEM: same direction of disagreement.** BPA 8 says clinicians **can consider** G-POEM for select patients with severe delay; [[aga-2025-gastroparesis|AGA 2025]] recommends **against routine** G-POEM (conditional, low). **2025 governs**; BPA 8 supplies the selection and setting requirements. Compare also [[aga-2023-gpoem-gastroparesis]], which sits between them in date.
- ⚠ **Botulinum toxin:** this CPU argues **against use except in clinical trials**; AGA 2025 is **against routine** use. Same direction, slightly different strength — no true conflict, but the CPU is the stronger statement and is the older one, so the page asserts AGA 2025's wording.
- **Nortriptyline:** BPA 6 endorses neuromodulators for pain while the only RCT of a neuromodulator in gastroparesis (NORIG, nortriptyline) was **negative**, and Table 1's own footnote says nortriptyline *"was not found to be effective in idiopathic gastroparesis."* The update resolves this by arguing for **tertiary** amines on FD-overlap grounds — an explicitly **extrapolated** rationale with no gastroparesis RCT behind it. - **Severity banding is unvalidated.** The mild/moderate/severe retention bands are attributed to *"some investigators"* and are *"not validated in large, prospective studies"* — yet BPA 3 asks clinicians to classify and BPA 8 conditions on "severe delay." Reproduce with that caveat attached.
- **Aprepitant dose** conflicts within the source itself (Table 1: 80 mg/day; text: 125 mg/day RCT dose).
