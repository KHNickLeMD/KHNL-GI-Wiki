## Summary

A commentary on, not a restatement of, the 2016 Multi-Society Task Force (MSTF) guideline on reprocessing flexible gastrointestinal endoscopes. Widespread media attention to duodenoscope-related transmission of deadly antibiotic-resistant infections prompted the MSTF to update the 2011 reprocessing guidelines; **the AGA Institute has officially endorsed the updated 2016 guidelines**. The authors trace the size of successive versions — the first guidelines in **2003 included 34 recommendations**, the **2011 guidelines included 39**, and the **2016 guidelines include 41** — and make the central point that, although outbreaks occurred despite adherence to the 2011 guidelines, the 2016 guidelines "are not dramatically different."

The major 2016 changes are two: (1) language to maintain consistency with the 2015 US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) endoscope reprocessing communications, and (2) statements suggesting greater monitoring and tracking of the endoscope throughout the clinical units and cleaning rooms, including the timing of events and who performs the key steps. There are "surprisingly only 2 new recommendations," one of which (**#24**) is a *suggestion* to perform cultures or further disinfection if feasible. Changes between 2003 and 2011 had included precleaning the endoscope (2016 recommendation #2) and transporting the pre-cleaned endoscope covered (2016 recommendation #3). The 2003 guidelines discuss unresolved issues related to endoscope reprocessing, and the majority of those unresolved issues have remained constant in both the 2011 and 2016 guidelines.

The authors organize the piece around MSTF recommendation numbers rather than around advice of their own, pairing each with an "Implications for Practice" paragraph on what endoscopy units will actually have to change: device- and manufacturer-specific (not unit-generic) cleaning protocols, endoscope-specific tracking systems possibly requiring barcode scanning and electronic health record modification, relocation of reprocessing out of patient care areas, and brand-matched cleaning brushes. They are skeptical of the supplemental modalities: per-procedure culture surveillance is not cost-effective unless the unit's transmission probability of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) is 24% or greater, ethylene oxide (ETO) sterilization failed to prevent a CRE-positive endoscope culture in one unit while adding cost and endoscope damage, and 2% glutaraldehyde exposes reprocessing staff to known irritants and can cause colitis if the endoscope is not properly rinsed between uses.

**The document is ungraded.** It issues no numbered recommendations, no Best Practice Advice statements, and no strength or evidence-quality ratings of its own, and describes no systematic evidence-review method. Every number it uses (#2, #3, #5, #6, #8, #12, #16, #24, #27, #28, #31, #32) is a **Multi-Society Task Force 2016 recommendation number**, not a number assigned by this commentary. Recommendation text below is quoted only where the commentary itself quotes the MSTF guideline; the MSTF guideline is not itself ingested, so recommendations it discusses only by number are listed as such.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Recommendation scheme — what this document actually contains

- **No recommendations of its own; no numbering of its own; no grades.** Section headings are MSTF 2016 recommendation numbers. Five discussion blocks, each followed by "Implications for Practice":
  1. **Recommendations #5, #6, #8, #12, #16, #28, #32** (treated as one group)
  2. **Recommendation #24**
  3. **Recommendation #31**
  4. **Recommendation #5** (treated again, for its device-specific content)
  5. **Recommendation #27**
- MSTF guideline counts: **2003 = 34 recommendations · 2011 = 39 · 2016 = 41**; **only 2 new recommendations in 2016** (#24 and #31 are the two discussed as new).

### Recommendations #5, #6, #8, #12, #16, #28, #32 — adherence to FDA and manufacturer information

- "These recommendations have been modified to include specific mention of adhering to information provided by the FDA and manufacturers."
- **Recommendation #5 has been revised to recommend "strict adherence" to manufacturer guidance.** This language change is consistent with the FDA's 2015 interim guidance using the same language. The strict adherence is meant to be **device and manufacturer specific**.
- The 2016 guidelines specifically state that "a **single standard work process within one institution may be insufficient**, given differences among manufacturers' instructions and varied instrument designs."
- **Training requirement (2016 guidelines, quoted):** "All staff involved with the use, cleaning and processing of flexible endoscopes should undergo documented **brand and model-specific training at commencement of employment and at least annually**…. Additional training, along with updated evaluation and documentation of competency, is **required whenever a change in reprocessing guidance is received from the endoscope manufacturer or regulatory agencies, or guidance from professional organizations is incorporated into unit policies and procedures**."
- Training is therefore required **at hire, annually**, and "anytime a breach is identified, when a major technique or new endoscope or reprocessing equipment is introduced, and in the context of local quality control efforts."
- **Implications for practice:** many units use a device-specific but **not manufacturer-specific** cleaning and disinfection protocol that takes the common elements across manufacturers and modifies them for the unit's environment — "The recommendations are not permissive of such a training or cleaning system." Many units will need to revise their cleaning and training protocols, and identify an individual or group to keep up with FDA, CDC, manufacturer, and professional-society changes and update the training of all individuals in the unit. **It is unclear from the guidelines what the minimum time should be between a change in recommendations and updated training**, and whether "all staff involved in the use" includes the endoscopists.

### Recommendation #5 — device-specific content

- Modified to specify compliance with manufacturer documentation, including that "**only model-specific cleaning devices (such as brushes) designed for the endoscope model being cleaned**" should be used.
- Also indicates that "**Manual cleaning should occur within 60 minutes of endoscope use and the intervals from use to cleaning should be monitored.**"
- **Implications for practice:** endoscope-specific tracking will be required to monitor the time between use and cleaning — possibly barcode scanning or another scanner system within the clinical areas and the cleaning/reprocessing rooms. Units will also need a means to indicate which brush brands match which endoscope brands, rather than using the brush that works best for the majority of brands used by the unit.

### Recommendation #24 (new) — supplemental modalities

- Verbatim as quoted in the commentary: "**Beyond the reprocessing steps discussed in these recommendations, no validated methods for additional duodenoscope reprocessing currently exist. However, units should review and consider the feasibility and appropriateness for their practice of employing one or more of the additional modalities *suggested* by the FDA for duodenoscopes: intermittent or per procedure culture surveillance of reprocessing outcomes, sterilization with ethylene oxide gas, repeat application of standard high level disinfection, or use of a liquid chemical germicide.**"
- "This new recommendation includes a suggestion that is consistent with the FDA documents."
- **Implications for practice — each modality's limitation:**
  - **Per-procedure culture surveillance:** "not cost-effective unless the unit's transmission probability of carbapenem-resistant *Enterobacteriaceae* is **24% or greater**." Restated in the conclusions as: "the culture and quarantine process is not cost-effective until the **pre-test probability of transmission is 1 in 4 patients or greater**."
  - **ETO sterilization:** problematic — a unit that took this approach **still encountered an endoscope with CRE detected by culture**; it also incurred extra costs to purchase additional scopes because of the longer reprocessing time, and had a greater number of endoscopes with damage (though the damage was not directly attributable to sterilization).
  - **Repeat high level disinfection:** "has the same potential limitations as sterilization."
  - **Liquid chemical germicides, such as 2% glutaraldehyde:** "will expose the staff responsible for reprocessing to known irritants and **can cause colitis in patients if the endoscope is not properly rinsed between uses**."
  - "These costs and risks to patients and staff should be evaluated by a unit before implementing these suggested modalities."
- **Sterilization versus disinfection:** since 2003 the guidelines have described sterilization versus disinfection for endoscopes. In 2016, **the majority of the FDA panel members recommended reclassifying endoscopes so that sterilization is required instead of high level disinfection.** "Indeed, the FDA Advisory Panel concluded that *duodenoscopes and AERs (automated endoscope reprocessors) do not provide a reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness*." Some panelists held that high level disinfection is adequate when done properly; the majority felt duodenoscopes should be **reclassified as critical devices in the Spaulding Classification**, shifting expectations toward sterilization. However, per the CDC and a laparoscopy textbook, **there is no evidence of differences in infection transmission when laparoscopes undergo sterilization versus high level disinfection** — surgical experience similar to the endoscopy unit that performed disinfection yet still identified CRE in a sterilized endoscope. "It appears that contamination may be unavoidable using the current reprocessing or sterilization paradigm and that at present, **methods to rapidly identify and contain outbreaks are the highest priority for innovation**."

### Recommendation #31 (new) — reprocessing facility design

- Verbatim as quoted: "**Reprocessing facilities should be designed with attention to the optimal flow of personnel, endoscopes and devices to avoid contamination between entering dirty instruments and reprocessed clean instruments. Reprocessing of endoscopes (other than immediate pre-cleaning) should not be performed in patient care areas due to risk of patient exposure to contaminated surfaces and devices.**"
- **Implications for practice:** units currently cleaning endoscopes in patient care areas will require a modification of workflow so that cleaning can occur in a separate area.

### Recommendation #27 — procedure logging and endoscope tracking

- The recommendation to **log each procedure by name, medical record number, the procedure, the serial number of the endoscope, and the automated reprocessor unit** was modified to include **the name of the person who cleaned and disinfected the endoscope** and to **log information about temporarily used/loaner endoscopes**. This information is needed to assist in an outbreak investigation.
- **Implications for practice:** the endoscope-specific tracking system will need to document the entire lifecycle of the endoscope. Electronic health records may need modification to capture the serial number of the endoscope and the reprocessing unit, to facilitate patient-specific notification if an outbreak occurs. **The 2011 and 2016 recommendations do not mention tracking the endoscopist who performs the procedure, however the 2003 recommendations do include endoscopist-level tracking**; cross-documenting the endoscope information in the patient's medical record would facilitate endoscopist-specific tracking.

### Conclusions

- The uncertainty of the source of the most recent outbreaks, the lack of clarity on whether there was any specific adherence issue or other problem with the 2013 guidelines, and the minor changes in the most recent guidelines "suggest that the 2016 guidelines themselves are, thus, **unlikely to guarantee the prevention of future outbreaks**."
- Prevention of future outbreaks is left to the manufacturers to modify their protocols and to the endoscopy units to adopt the protocols rapidly. If followed, the guidelines will make it possible to **better track the source** of future outbreaks if the tracking and monitoring suggested is performed. "As suggested by the FDA panel, the effectiveness of the current cleaning paradigm for duodenoscopes is ineffective and these guidelines reflect changes to **contain, rather than prevent, future outbreaks**."
- **Disposable endoscopes** as an emerging option: in **September 2016 the FDA approved the first disposable colonoscope**, expected to be available in the United States in **early 2017 at a cost of around $250 per colonoscope**. "Although a disposable endoscope with an elevator mechanism is not available at this time, if such a device were available it may be a solution to several of the unresolved issues that were present in the 2003, 2011 and 2016 guidelines. **These unresolved issues include interval of storage after reprocessing, microbiologic surveillance, and endoscope durability and longevity.**" If outbreaks persist after the use of disposable endoscopes, some other product or procedure within the endoscopic procedure is the source of infectious transmission.
- Actively comparing transmission and cost-effectiveness of endoscope reprocessing versus disposable endoscopes will be needed. "It is possible that not all patients will require a disposable endoscope and those patients at **highest risk of transmission and infection can be identified and prioritized** for these devices. Prioritizing patients accurately may have implications for budgeting and reimbursement."
- "We agree that the 2016 guidelines should be followed yet, at the same time, **adherence with them cannot be relied upon as a guarantee to prevent transmission**." The majority of unresolved issues from 2003 are the same unresolved issues in the 2011 and 2016 guidelines; these could be the clue to the source of the recent outbreaks as there was adherence to the guidelines in the units with outbreaks. "Actively addressing the unresolved issues may provide insight into the prevention of outbreaks."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **With [[aga-2016-infection-ercp-endoscopes]] (same society, one year earlier):** no factual contradiction, but a difference in stance. The 2016 commentary presents the FDA supplemental measures as reasonable options to consider (double reprocessing is "perhaps the most simple and readily adopted"); this 2017 commentary emphasizes that none is validated, that ETO failed in a real unit, that culture-and-quarantine is **not cost-effective below a 24% transmission probability**, and that liquid chemical germicide carries a patient harm (colitis) if rinsing is inadequate. **The 2017 document is the newer tier-1 source and governs the page where the two differ in emphasis.**
- **Within the document:** the FDA advisory panel majority wanted duodenoscopes reclassified as *critical* devices requiring sterilization, yet the authors cite CDC data and surgical (laparoscope) experience showing **no difference in infection transmission between sterilization and high level disinfection** — and a unit that sterilized still cultured CRE from an endoscope. Reclassification is therefore unlikely to solve the problem on the evidence presented.
- **Unresolved by the guidelines themselves** (carried unchanged from 2003 through 2016): interval of storage after reprocessing, microbiologic surveillance, endoscope durability and longevity; also, the minimum time allowed between a change in recommendations and updated staff training, and whether endoscopists count as "staff involved in the use."
- **Source not ingested:** the Multi-Society Guideline on Reprocessing Flexible Gastrointestinal Endoscopes — 2016 (Petersen BT, Cohen J, Eisen G, et al.) is quoted here only through this commentary; recommendations referenced by number alone (#2, #3, #6, #8, #12, #16, #28, #32) have no text in the wiki.
