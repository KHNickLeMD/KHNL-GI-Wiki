## Summary

An AGA Expert Review addressing what to do after a first attempt at [[helicobacter-pylori-infection|*H. pylori*]] eradication fails. Its organising claim is that clinicians reflexively reach for a different antibiotic when the actual failure is often something else — nonadherence, inadequate acid suppression, or an unreviewed prior antibiotic exposure — and that these should be identified *before* prescribing an alternative regimen.

The review separates the determinants of failure into **host** (CYP2C19 and other pH-affecting polymorphisms, adherence), **microbial** (antibiotic resistance, strain diversity), and **systems** factors (absent surveillance registries, inaccessible sensitivity testing, practice-pattern variability). It then translates these into 12 Best Practice Advice statements covering regimen selection, dosing, allergy delabelling, acid-suppression optimisation, treatment duration, susceptibility testing, and shared decision making.

A distinctive methodological stance: the authors **deliberately avoid the terms "salvage" and "rescue"**, because the literature uses them without a consistent definition. They also emphasise that selecting therapy on the basis of **prior antibiotic exposure is not inferior** to selecting it on in-vitro susceptibility, and bypasses the logistical barriers to obtaining testing.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definition of refractory infection (this is the operative definition)

- **Refractory *H. pylori* infection** = a **persistently positive non-serologic test** (breath-, stool-, or gastroscopy-based), **at least 4 weeks** after **one or more completed courses** of a current guideline-recommended **first-line** eradication therapy, and **off any medications (e.g. PPIs) that might impact test sensitivity**.
- **Must be distinguished from *recurrent* infection** — a non-serologic test that was **initially negative** after eradication and **subsequently positive** at a later interval. Recurrence may reflect **ongoing intrafamilial exposure**, and is best addressed by **testing household members and treating those who test positive** — a different action entirely.

### Antibiotic resistance — rates and clinical impact

In-vitro resistance and the *fold-increase in treatment failure* it confers when that drug is in the regimen (pooled, treatment-naive + refractory):

| Antibiotic | Fold-higher likelihood of treatment failure if resistant |
|---|---|
| Levofloxacin | **8.2-fold** (95% CI 3.8–17.6) |
| Clarithromycin | **7.0-fold** (95% CI 5.2–9.3) |
| Nitroimidazole (metronidazole) | **2.5-fold** (95% CI 1.8–3.5) — relatively less clinical impact |

Resistance rates, from a systematic review of >50,000 patients across 45 countries:

| Antibiotic | Primary (global range) | Secondary — after failed treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Clarithromycin | 10%–34% | **15%–67%** |
| Levofloxacin | 11%–30% | **19%–30%** |
| Metronidazole | 23%–56% | **30%–65%** |
| Amoxicillin | <5% (usually 1%–2%) | low |
| Tetracycline | <5% (usually 1%–2%) | low |
| Rifabutin | low | low |

- **US-specific data are sparse** and dated: 347 strains 1998–2002 → 13% clarithromycin, 25% metronidazole; 128 Houston VA strains 2009–2013 (110 treatment-naive) → 15% clarithromycin, 17% metronidazole, 29% levofloxacin, **15% resistant to more than one antibiotic**; 345 strains from a recent multicentre trial → 17% clarithromycin, 44% metronidazole.
- **Immigrants from endemic countries may carry the resistance pattern of their country of origin, not the host country** — infection is usually acquired in childhood.
- **Selecting therapy by prior antibiotic exposure is *not inferior* to selecting by in-vitro susceptibility**, and avoids the logistical barriers of testing. A US national survey found **only 38% of providers asked patients about prior antibiotic exposure**.
- Resistance mechanisms: clarithromycin — one of 3 point mutations in the **23S ribosomal subunit**; levofloxacin — **DNA gyrase subunit A**; amoxicillin — **penicillin binding protein 1**; tetracycline — **16S ribosomal binding site** mutations or increased efflux; rifabutin — ***rpoB***. Nitroimidazole resistance is usually **rdxA** but is complex enough that **no single point mutation can be used for molecular resistance profiling**, and phenotypic testing is poorly standardised — which is why in-vitro metronidazole resistance predicts outcome poorly.

### Acid suppression as a cause of failure (mechanism, with numbers)

- *H. pylori* is **most susceptible to antibiotics at intragastric pH 6–8**, the optimal range for its replication.
- Antibiotic half-life is pH-dependent:

| Intragastric pH | Amoxicillin half-life | Clarithromycin half-life |
|---|---|---|
| **pH <2** | ~15.2 ± 0.3 hours | ~1.0 ± 0.04 hours |
| **pH >7** | **>68 hours** | **>68 hours** |

- Consequence: **without adequate, sustained acid suppression, *H. pylori* persists despite exposure to antibiotics it is susceptible to in vitro.**
- **CYP2C19** metabolism-enhancing phenotypes → higher eradication failure when using PPIs heavily metabolised by CYP2C19 (**omeprazole, lansoprazole**). Poor-metaboliser phenotypes give high plasma PPI concentrations. Other pH-affecting polymorphisms implicated: **IL-1B, MDR1**.

### Adherence

- The adherence threshold above which there is negligible incremental benefit is **not known**.
- Studies suggest **>60% to >90% of the prescribed course** may suffice for eradication **in primary infection**; the threshold "might plausibly be higher for refractory *H. pylori*."
- Barriers: regimen complexity, high pill burden, physical intolerance, poor provider communication, lack of understanding of why therapy is indicated.
- Two large Chinese RCTs showed an **interactive smartphone application** and **text-based reminders** improved adherence to primary therapy; not yet studied in the US for refractory infection. Pillboxes, medication calendars, and pharmacist counselling may also help.

### Second-line regimen definitions — doses, frequencies, durations

Reproduced from the source's Table 1 footnote. **"PPI" means standard dose unless "high-dose" is stated; "high-dose" = double the standard dose.**

| Regimen | Composition and dosing | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Bismuth quadruple** | bismuth ~300 mg q.i.d. + metronidazole 500 mg t.i.d. **or** q.i.d. + tetracycline 500 mg q.i.d. + PPI b.i.d. | 14 days |
| **Concomitant** | clarithromycin 500 mg b.i.d. + amoxicillin 1 g b.i.d. + metronidazole **or** tinidazole 500 mg b.i.d. + PPI b.i.d. | 14 days |
| **Clarithromycin triple** | clarithromycin 500 mg b.i.d. + amoxicillin 1 g b.i.d. **or** metronidazole 500 mg b.i.d. + PPI b.i.d. | 14 days |
| **Levofloxacin triple** | levofloxacin 500 mg q.d. + amoxicillin 1 g b.i.d. + PPI b.i.d. | 14 days |
| **Levofloxacin quadruple** | levofloxacin 500 mg q.d. + PPI b.i.d. + 2 antibiotics (multiple variations exist) | 10–14 days |
| **Rifabutin triple** | rifabutin 150 **or** 300 mg daily + amoxicillin 1 g b.i.d. + PPI b.i.d. | 10 days |
| **High-dose dual** | amoxicillin 2–3 g daily in 3–4 split doses + high-dose PPI b.i.d. | 14 days |

**Standard PPI doses as defined by this source:** pantoprazole 40 mg, lansoprazole 30 mg, omeprazole 20 mg, esomeprazole 20 mg, dexlansoprazole 30 mg, rabeprazole 20 mg.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clarithromycin triple failed | Bismuth quad; levofloxacin triple or quad | Bismuth quad; levofloxacin triple | Bismuth quad; levofloxacin triple | Not discussed |
| Bismuth quad failed | Levofloxacin triple or quad; if high levofloxacin resistance — bismuth with other antibiotics, or rifabutin triple | Levofloxacin triple | By antibiotic history: levofloxacin triple; clarithromycin triple | Bismuth + PPI + 2 antibiotics **not used** in the first-line bismuth quad |

## Best Practice Advice Statements (all 12, verbatim; ungraded by design)

1. **Best Practice Advice 1:** The usual cause of refractory *Helicobacter pylori* infection (persistent infection after attempting eradication therapy) is antibiotic resistance. Providers should attempt to identify other contributing etiologies, including inadequate adherence to therapy and insufficient gastric acid suppression.
2. **Best Practice Advice 2:** Providers should conduct a thorough review of prior antibiotic exposures. If there is a history of any treatment with macrolides or fluoroquinolones, then clarithromycin- or levofloxacin-based regimens, respectively, should be avoided given the high likelihood of resistance. By contrast, resistance to amoxicillin, tetracycline, and rifabutin is rare, and these can be considered for subsequent therapies in refractory *H pylori* infection.
3. **Best Practice Advice 3:** Eradication regimens for *H pylori* are complex and might not be fully comprehended by patients. Barriers to adherence should be explored and addressed prior to prescribing therapy. Providers should explain the rationale for therapy, dosing instructions, expected adverse events, and the importance of completing the full therapeutic course.
4. **Best Practice Advice 4:** If bismuth quadruple therapy failed as a first-line treatment, shared decision making between providers and patients should guide selection between (a) levofloxacin- or rifabutin-based triple-therapy regimens with high-dose dual proton pump inhibitor (PPI) and amoxicillin, and (b) an alternative bismuth-containing quadruple therapy, as second-line options.
5. **Best Practice Advice 5:** When using metronidazole-containing regimens, providers should consider adequate dosing of metronidazole (1.5–2 g daily in divided doses) with concomitant bismuth therapy, because this may improve eradication success rates irrespective of observed in vitro metronidazole resistance.
6. **Best Practice Advice 6:** In the absence of a history of anaphylaxis, penicillin allergy testing should be considered in a patient labeled as having this allergy in order to delist penicillin as an allergy and potentially enable its use. Amoxicillin should be used at a daily dose of at least 2 g divided 3 times per day or 4 times per day to avoid low trough levels.
7. **Best Practice Advice 7:** Inadequate acid suppression is associated with *H pylori* eradication failure. The use of high-dose and more potent PPIs, PPIs not metabolized by CYP2C19, or potassium-competitive acid blockers, if available, should be considered in cases of refractory *H pylori* infection.
8. **Best Practice Advice 8:** Longer treatment durations provide higher eradication success rates compared with shorter durations (eg, 14 days vs 7 days). Whenever appropriate, longer treatment durations should be selected for treating refractory *H pylori* infection.
9. **Best Practice Advice 9:** In some cases, there should be shared decision making regarding ongoing attempts to eradicate *H pylori*. The potential benefits of *H pylori* eradication should be weighed carefully against the likelihood of adverse effects and inconvenience of repeated exposure to antibiotics and high-dose acid suppression, particularly in vulnerable populations, such as the elderly.
10. **Best Practice Advice 10:** After 2 failed therapies with confirmed patient adherence, *H pylori* susceptibility testing should be considered to guide the selection of subsequent regimens.
11. **Best Practice Advice 11:** Compiling local data on *H pylori* eradication success rates for each regimen, along with patient demographic and clinical factors (including prior non-*H pylori* antibiotic exposure) is important. Aggregated data should be made publicly available to guide local selection of *H pylori* eradication therapy.
12. **Best Practice Advice 12:** Proposed adjunctive therapies, including probiotics, are of unproven benefit as treatment for refractory *H pylori* infection, and thus, their use should be considered experimental.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Regimen selection is superseded.** Table 1 compiles guidance from Maastricht V (2016), Toronto (2016), ACG (2017) and the Chinese National Consensus (2018) — all predating [[acg-2024-hp-treatment]], which displaced empiric PPI-clarithromycin triple therapy on the strength of US resistance data. Where the two disagree on *which regimen*, the page follows ACG 2024. This CPU's **Table 1 is retained here as a historical record of what those four guidelines said**, not as current wiki guidance.
- **Susceptibility testing — a genuine divergence in threshold.** This CPU says testing "should be considered" **after 2 failed therapies with confirmed adherence** (BPA 10). [[acg-2024-hp-treatment]] Key Concept 6 is framed differently: use testing **whenever the choice of therapy remains unclear** after accounting for prior *H. pylori* treatments, broader antibiotic exposure and penicillin allergy — which can be sooner or later than two failures. Both are on the page; ACG 2024 governs.
- **Probiotics — concordant, differently worded.** This CPU calls adjunctive probiotics **experimental** (BPA 12); ACG 2024 finds **insufficient evidence** that they improve efficacy or tolerability. No conflict.
- **The adherence threshold is unknown.** The source states plainly that the level of adherence above which there is negligible incremental benefit **has not been established** for refractory infection, and that the >60–90% figures come from *primary* infection. Do not present those numbers as a refractory-infection target.
- **No formal evidence grading exists for any of the 12 BPAs** — the source declined to rate them. Any wiki statement sourced here should not be given a strength/quality label.
- **US resistance data are old and thin.** The most recent US figures cited are from a trial cohort and from 2009–2013 VA strains; the CPU's own BPA 11 exists precisely because local eradication-rate registries do not exist.
