## Summary

- Update of the [[acg-2017-ibd-preventive-care|2017 ACG preventive care guideline]] for adults with [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]]; recommendations are for North America.
- Premise: many patients treat their gastroenterologist as their primary provider, and **>70% of patients with IBD will at some time be on immune-modifying therapy**, raising risk of infections — many vaccine-preventable. The gastroenterology team should document and recommend vaccines and coordinate with primary care and other specialists.
- Domains: vaccinations; cervical cancer, melanoma, and non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) screening; osteoporosis; depression and anxiety; smoking cessation. **Colorectal dysplasia surveillance is out of scope.**
- Output: **12 GRADE recommendations** (Table 1) and **11 key concepts** (Table 2). Key concepts are expert-opinion or definitional statements **not** put through GRADE — they carry no grade.
- GRADE: strength **strong** (benefits clearly outweigh harms) or **conditional** (trade-offs uncertain); certainty high / moderate / low / very low. Literature search through 2024.
- Simple office measures (vaccination checklists, stocking influenza and pneumococcal vaccines in the gastroenterology office) significantly raise vaccination rates.

## Key Concepts (Table 2) — ungraded

| # | Key concept |
|---|---|
| 1 | Follow Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) recommendations; **no live vaccine while on immune-modifying therapy**. |
| 2 | Give **age-appropriate vaccines before starting** immune-modifying therapy when possible. |
| 3 | **Annual influenza vaccine** for all adults with IBD. |
| 4 | Adults on immune-modifying therapy **and their household contacts**: nonlive (trivalent inactivated) influenza vaccine — **not** the live inhaled vaccine. |
| 5 | All adults with IBD **≥75 years**: respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccine. |
| 6 | Adults with IBD **aged 50–74** with certain chronic medical conditions or other risk factors for severe RSV disease: RSV vaccine. |
| 7 | Assess **varicella immunity before** immune-modifying therapy — documented chickenpox history **or** completed 2-dose varicella vaccine series. **No serologic testing** in previously vaccinated people (high false-negative rate). If nonimmune, vaccinate before starting therapy when possible. |
| 8 | **Household members** of patients on immune-modifying therapy **can receive live vaccines with certain precautions**. |
| 9 | Tetanus, diphtheria, and acellular pertussis (Tdap), hepatitis A virus (HAV), human papillomavirus (HPV), and meningococcal vaccines per ACIP recommendations. |
| 10 | Vaccinate against **[[chronic-hepatitis-b\|hepatitis B]] if not immune**. |
| 11 | Screen all patients with [[crohns-disease\|CD]] and [[ulcerative-colitis\|UC]] for **depression and anxiety at baseline and annually**; refer positive screens for counseling/therapy. |

## What Changed From 2017

[[acg-2017-ibd-preventive-care|ACG 2017]] had 14 graded statements. Main changes:

| Topic | 2017 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Influenza | Graded (1a/1b; conditional, very low) | Moved to ungraded key concepts 3–4; high-dose vaccine advised on [[anti-tnf-agents\|anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF)]] monotherapy |
| Pneumococcal | Immunosuppressed: both 13-valent conjugate (PCV13) and 23-valent polysaccharide (PPSV23) vaccines (conditional, very low) | Single PCV20 or PCV21: all ≥50 regardless of therapy (strong, low); 19–49 on therapy (conditional, very low); prior-vaccinated → CDC guidance (strong, low) |
| Herpes zoster | >50 "should consider" vaccination, incl. some immunosuppressed (strong, low) — live-vaccine era | 2-dose recombinant vaccine for all ≥50 (conditional, low) and ≥19 on/planning therapy (conditional, very low) |
| Varicella | Graded statement (conditional, very low) | Key concept 7; serology not recommended after prior vaccination |
| Yellow fever | Travel-medicine consult before travel if immunosuppressed (conditional, very low) | No separate statement; yellow fever listed among live vaccines not to give on immune-modifying therapy |
| Meningococcal | Adolescents per routine schedule (conditional, very low) | Key concept 9 (per ACIP) |
| Hepatitis B | Grouped with Tdap/HAV/HPV per ACIP (conditional, very low) | Separate key concept 10 (vaccinate if not immune) |
| Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), RSV, rotavirus | No graded statement or key concept | Rec 6, key concepts 5–6, Rec 7 |
| Cervical screening | Annual on immunosuppressive therapy (conditional, very low) | Adds start (within a year of sexual activity) and, if <30, annual × 3 years then every 3 years |
| Melanoma | Screening independent of biologics (strong, low) | Now **annual**; downgraded to conditional, very low |
| NMSC | Thiopurines only (strong, low) | Adds methotrexate, JAK inhibitors, S1P modulators; annual; conditional, very low |
| Depression/anxiety | Graded (conditional, low) | Key concept 11; baseline and annual, with referral |
| Smoking | **CD** patients who smoke (strong, low) | **All** adults with IBD who smoke (strong, low) |
| Osteoporosis | BMD at diagnosis and periodically (conditional, very low) | Unchanged in substance (Rec 11) |

## Key Findings / Claims

### Vaccination Principles

- **Timing:** review vaccination status at diagnosis, at transfer of care, or during remission. Vaccinate before immune-modifying therapy when possible, but **do not delay needed therapy**. Once on therapy, vaccinate at the **earliest opportunity regardless of the biologic dosing cycle**; small molecules are **not held** for vaccination.
- **Response by drug:**
  - Aminosalicylates or [[thiopurines|thiopurine]] monotherapy: normal, sustained responses.
  - Anti-TNF: blunted responses to inactivated vaccines (influenza, hepatitis B, pneumococcus); lowest with anti-TNF **combined** with thiopurine or methotrexate. Infliximab timing (with infusion vs mid-interval) did not change influenza response.
  - [[vedolizumab|Vedolizumab]] and ustekinumab: small studies show normal influenza and mRNA COVID-19 responses; vedolizumab blunted response to an oral cholera vaccine in healthy controls.
  - Tofacitinib: lower humoral response to pneumococcal (not influenza) vaccine in rheumatoid arthritis. No data for upadacitinib, S1P modulators (ozanimod, etrasimod), or selective interleukin-23 (IL-23) inhibitors (mirikizumab, risankizumab, guselkumab).
  - Temporarily holding methotrexate improved influenza response in rheumatoid arthritis (with flare risk); whether holding an immunomodulator helps in IBD is unknown.
- **Safety:** nonlive vaccines are safe in IBD — adverse events mainly local/mild systemic, no different from patients without IBD (meta-analysis, 2016 patients).

### Live Vaccines (Key Concept 1, Figure 2)

- Contraindicated on immune-modifying therapy: live attenuated influenza, measles-mumps-rubella (MMR), dengue, yellow fever. Small series suggest some live vaccines may be safe in some immunosuppressed patients, but evidence is too limited.
- **Live vaccine decision pathway (Figure 2)** — low- vs higher-level immunosuppression definitions, hold intervals (biologics 3 months; JAK inhibitors and S1P modulators 1 month), and the 4-week wait before starting therapy — recreated on [[ibd-preventive-care#Live Vaccines]].

### Influenza (Key Concepts 3–4)

- IBD carries higher influenza risk and more complications (hospitalization, pneumonia); systemic corticosteroids add risk.
- Anti-TNF (mono or combination) lowers antibody response, but seroprotection/seroconversion rates may be preserved. Booster doses did not help. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT), **high-dose** vaccine gave higher titers than standard dose on anti-TNF monotherapy.
- Practice: annual vaccine for all; **age ≥65 → one of the 3 ACIP-preferred vaccines** (high-dose, recombinant, or adjuvanted); **anti-TNF monotherapy → high-dose vaccine**. Ideally vaccinate September–October, but offer throughout the season. Can be co-administered with pneumococcal, SARS-CoV-2, and RSV vaccines.
- Live intranasal vaccine (approved ages 2–49) not for immunocompromised patients on immune-modifying therapy.

### Pneumococcal (Recs 1–3, Figure 1)

- IBD raises pneumonia risk (adjusted hazard ratio 1.54); higher with opioids, corticosteroids, biologics, thiopurines, proton pump inhibitors. Invasive pneumococcal disease risk elevated before and after diagnosis.
- PPSV23 responses impaired on thiopurine + anti-TNF; IBD data exist mainly for PCV13/PPSV23 — PCV15, PCV20, and PCV21 have not been specifically studied in IBD. PCV21 approved for adults ≥19.
- **Vaccine-naive adults with IBD, regardless of age or immunosuppression: a single dose of PCV20 or PCV21; no further boosters needed.** Prior PCV13/PPSV23 → sequencing per Figure 1 (recreated on [[ibd-preventive-care#Pneumococcal Schedule (ACG 2025 Figure 1)]]).

### RSV (Key Concepts 5–6)

- RSV hospitalization and mortality in older adults can match or exceed influenza; IBD (especially recent systemic corticosteroids) raises serious-infection risk.
- Three vaccines licensed for adults ≥60 (RSVPreF3 OA, RSVpreF, mRNA). ACIP: single dose for all ≥75, and 50–74 at increased risk (chronic cardiovascular, lung, liver, or renal disease; diabetes with end-organ damage; immunocompromise; obesity; neurologic/neuromuscular conditions; frailty; nursing-home residence). Guideline: all eligible patients with IBD should receive it.

### Herpes Zoster (Recs 4–5)

- Zoster risk higher in UC and CD than matched controls; independently linked to anti-TNF, corticosteroids, thiopurines; highest with anti-TNF + thiopurine (odds ratio 3.29); occurs at younger ages.
- Live zoster vaccine (Zostavax) is no longer available in the US.
- **Recombinant zoster vaccine (RZV; Shingrix): 2 doses of 0.5 mL intramuscular at 0 and 2–6 months; in immunocompromised patients may give at 0 and 1–2 months.** 97.2% efficacy in immunocompetent adults ≥50.
- In IBD cohorts, RZV markedly lowered zoster risk (e.g., odds ratio 0.36; adjusted odds ratio 0.44 vs unvaccinated IBD). Nonlive → no disease risk regardless of immune status; safe if chickenpox/varicella vaccine history unknown. Cost-effective for all adults with IBD.

### Varicella (Key Concept 7)

- Commercial assays may give false negatives after vaccination (34% false-negative rate in one study); rely on immunization history rather than antibody testing.
- Give the 2-dose series to nonimmunosuppressed patients without chickenpox history or a completed series; can be given **4 weeks before** starting immunosuppression. If already on immunosuppression, CDC experts recommend waiting **3 months after stopping** therapy. With active disease, treating IBD takes priority over pausing therapy to vaccinate.

### SARS-CoV-2 (Rec 6)

- IBD and its therapies do not appear to raise COVID-19 risk; IBD not linked to worse outcomes absent general risk factors (older age, obesity, chronic corticosteroids).
- Vaccines safe and well tolerated; robust humoral responses, though lower titers possible on anti-TNF or corticosteroids. No appreciable flare risk after mRNA vaccination.

### Household Contacts (Key Concept 8)

- Household contacts should be up to date per pediatric/adult schedules (smallpox vaccine excepted).
- MMR, varicella, and rotavirus vaccines may be given to contacts; precautions:
  - Varicella recipient with a post-vaccine rash → avoid contact with the immunocompromised person until rash resolves.
  - Wash hands after changing diapers of an infant given rotavirus vaccine (shedding up to 1 month after last dose).
- Live attenuated influenza vaccine: contraindicated in immunosuppressed people; may be given to contacts unless the patient is in a protective environment (positive-pressure, high-efficiency particulate air-filtered room).

### Tdap, HAV, HPV, Meningococcal (Key Concept 9)

- **Tdap:** 1 dose of Tdap, then tetanus-diphtheria (Td) or Tdap every 10 years; pregnant women get Tdap at **27–36 weeks** of each pregnancy. Wound management: Tdap if no Td/Tdap in prior 5 years. Anti-TNF may lower pertussis antibody levels; combined anti-TNF + thiopurine lowered response rates.
- **HAV:** adults vaccinated if at increased risk (international travelers, men who have sex with men, illicit drug use, homelessness, occupational exposure) or at risk of severe disease (chronic liver disease, human immunodeficiency virus [HIV]). Seroconversion >97% in one IBD study, slightly lower on anti-TNF.
- **HPV:** 2- or 3-dose series ages 9–14, catch-up through 26; ages 27–45 by shared decision-making (e.g., new sexual partners). Immunogenic and safe in IBD; no increased IBD risk after vaccination.
- **Meningococcal:** IBD has higher meningitis risk vs non-IBD (incidence 27.6/100,000 person-years in CD, 20.7 in UC, vs 12.7 in comparators). Meningococcal conjugate vaccine for serogroups A, C, W, Y (MenACWY) at 11–12 years with repeat at 16; also for at-risk groups. Patients with IBD with risk factors should be vaccinated.

### Hepatitis B (Key Concept 10)

- Test hepatitis B surface antigen, core antibody, and surface antibody (anti-HBs) and vaccinate nonimmune patients **before anti-TNF**.
- Seroprotection threshold debated (anti-HBs >10 vs >100 mIU/mL). Responders whose anti-HBs wanes <10 mIU/mL usually remain protected; ACIP recommends a **challenge dose** to test for an anamnestic response.
- Seroprotection after a primary 3-dose series: >95% in healthy people vs 33%–76% in IBD. Options that improved response: double-dose Engerix-B at 0, 1, 2 months (75% vs 41% standard); 2-dose adjuvanted Heplisav-B (78.3%; a third dose rescued 56.7% of nonresponders); Heplisav-B more cost-effective than Engerix-B on immunosuppression.
- Immunocompromised patients: check anti-HBs **1–3 months after completing** the series.

### Rotavirus (Rec 7)

- Infants with in-utero biologic exposure (anti-TNF, vedolizumab, ustekinumab) given live rotavirus vaccine: no serious adverse events across several series (56, 46, 168, 57 infants); 2024 systematic review of >300 exposed infants found no increased adverse events.

### Cervical Cancer (Rec 8)

- Screening has shifted from cytology (Pap) to HPV testing (2019 American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology guidance).
- On immune-modifying therapy, recommendation mirrors HIV guidance: **baseline cytology; if normal, annual testing × 3 years, then every 3 years**. Abnormal cytology → HPV serotyping; persistent abnormality → colposcopy. Not on immune-modifying therapy → average-risk screening.
- Data on IBD itself are conflicting; consistent trend of higher risk with immunosuppressants (adjusted odds ratio 1.34 for high-grade dysplasia/cancer). Mann et al meta-analysis: no significant increase in cervical cancer (hazard ratio 1.24, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.94–1.63), but more low-grade lesions (hazard ratio 1.15). Women with IBD on immunosuppressants are screened less often.
- No data on JAK inhibitors or S1P modulators; expert opinion applies the same recommendation.

### Skin Cancer (Recs 9–10)

- Everyone starting immunosuppressive therapy: sunscreen protective against ultraviolet A and B, sun-protective clothing.
- All patients on **systemic immunosuppressive therapy: annual total body skin examination** for melanoma and NMSC; encourage regular self-examination. Continue skin surveillance **after stopping thiopurines**.
- Melanoma: IBD associated with 37% higher risk (relative risk [RR] 1.37; CD 1.80, UC 1.23), independent of biologics. Meta-analyses show no significant melanoma increase with biologics or thiopurines.
- NMSC by drug:
  - Thiopurines: RR 1.88 (95% CI 1.48–2.38); incidence rate ratio rises from ~1.6 in year 1 to 3.6 in year 5; persistence after stopping is debated; patients with prior basal cell carcinoma who continued thiopurines had more recurrence.
  - Methotrexate: inadequate IBD data; in inflammatory arthritis 4.6-fold higher basal or squamous cell carcinoma risk, higher with cumulative dose >8,000 mg (standardized incidence ratio 4.81).
  - S1P modulators: reports of basal cell, squamous cell, melanoma (ozanimod); Kaposi sarcoma and Merkel cell carcinoma post-marketing.
  - Ustekinumab: linked mostly in psoriasis (confounded by prior phototherapy).
  - [[jak-inhibitors|JAK inhibitors]]: no escalated malignancy in IBD data; higher NMSC, lung cancer, and lymphoma vs anti-TNF in rheumatoid arthritis patients >50; across indications not higher than placebo or methotrexate but higher than TNF inhibitors.
  - Vedolizumab: not linked to malignancy.

### Depression and Anxiety (Key Concept 11)

- Prevalence (meta-analysis, 77 studies): anxiety symptoms 32.1% in IBD vs 10.4% background; depression 25.2% vs 17.9%. CD vs UC: ~20% more likely to report anxiety (odds ratio 1.2) and depression (odds ratio 1.2). Active vs quiescent IBD: anxiety odds ratio 2.5, depression odds ratio 3.1.
- Effect on course: baseline depression predicted relapses (12–18 months) in one cohort and lower clinical remission after infliximab in another; other studies found no link. Short-term (not long-term) perceived stress predicted UC relapse in one cohort.
- Treatment:
  - Cognitive behavioral therapy: mixed — one RCT of 10 weeks showed no significant effect; an updated meta-analysis of psychological therapies showed significant, persistent decreases in depression and anxiety scores.
  - Acceptance and commitment therapy: single small study (anxiety, not depression, improved).
  - Antidepressants: effective for psychological and somatic symptoms in a review of 12 studies; in a meta-analysis restricted to good-quality RCTs, significant effect on depressive symptoms; serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors improved depression and anxiety scores.
  - IBD treatment alone lowered depression scores in a meta-analysis, but heterogeneity made results inconclusive.

### Bone Health (Rec 11)

- Osteoporosis = BMD ≥2.5 standard deviations below mean young-adult peak bone mass on dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DEXA); "established osteoporosis" adds a fragility fracture.
- IBD fracture risk: overall RR 1.38 (95% CI 1.11–1.73); vertebral RR 2.26 (95% CI 1.04–4.90).
- **[[corticosteroids-ibd|Corticosteroids]]:**
  - 30%–50% of patients on long-term corticosteroids develop fractures; risk is dose-dependent and best predicted by **daily** (not cumulative) dose.
  - Prednisone 3–10 mg/day already raises fracture risk; **as low as 2.5 mg/day → 1.55-fold spinal fracture risk**; **>7.5 mg/day → fivefold spine and hip fracture risk**.
  - Fractures appear **3–6 months after starting**; risk falls as early as **3 months after stopping** and returns to baseline within **1–2 years**.
  - Steroid use in the past 12 months for <90 days still lowered femoral neck BMD and raised major (5.4% vs 7.7%) and hip (1.1% vs 1.8%) fracture rates.
  - Added glucocorticoid-fracture risk factors: postmenopausal women and older men on ≥20 mg/day prednisone, low body mass index (BMI), smoking, parental hip fracture, >3 alcoholic drinks/day, intravenous pulse steroids.
- Conventional low-BMD risk factors listed: hormone excess (parathyroid, thyroxine, cortisol) or deficiency (estrogen, testosterone, vitamin D); medications (heparin, warfarin, cyclosporine A, methotrexate, tacrolimus); modifiable factors (smoking, weight <129 lb, estrogen deficiency, menopause <45, low calcium, excess alcohol, falls, inactivity, frailty); nonmodifiable (prior adult fracture, family fracture history, White race, female sex, dementia, older age).
- **Fracture Risk Assessment Tool (FRAX):** 10-year hip and major osteoporotic fracture probability from femoral neck BMD + clinical factors (incl. oral glucocorticoids ≥5 mg/day prednisone for ≥3 months). Limits: built for postmenopausal women and men ≥50; not validated in those treated for osteoporosis; may overestimate risk in IBD; FRAX alone should not replace DEXA (small retrospective data only).
- **Spinal x-rays** only if height loss **≥4 cm** vs reported height at age 20, **≥2 cm** during follow-up, or symptoms such as back pain.
- **Repeat DEXA:** BMD changes ~1%/year, below scanner error (~3%) — so 1–2-year repeat scans for monitoring are contested. ~10% of patients on oral bisphosphonates lose significant BMD; a repeat DEXA **at a minimum of 1 year after starting therapy** is appropriate.

### Smoking (Rec 12)

- CD development: smokers have ~2- to >3-fold higher CD risk; meta-analysis odds ratio 1.76.
- CD course: more stricturing (hazard ratio 1.5) and perianal complications (1.50), more thiopurine need, 56% higher flare risk, twofold flare risk after surgery, and more need for first surgery (odds ratio 1.68) and second surgery (2.17); more endoscopic recurrence after ileocolonic resection.
- Anti-TNF response: in studies with a clear smoking definition, smokers were less likely to respond (RR 0.63).
- Quitting: quitters >1 year had flare risk similar to never-smokers and lower than continuing smokers.
- Colorectal neoplasia in IBD: former smoking raised risk in UC (hazard ratio 1.73); active (2.20) and passive smoking (1.87) raised risk in CD.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **RSV age:** ACG 2025 (following ACIP) — all ≥75, and 50–74 with risk factors; [[aga-2025-noncolorectal-cancer-ibd|AGA 2025]] — all adults with IBD ≥60.
- **Cervical screening:** ACG 2025 — annual on immune-modifying therapy (× 3 years if <30, then every 3 years); AGA 2025 — age-appropriate standard screening, data insufficient to require more frequent screening on combined immunosuppression or thiopurines; shared decision-making.
- **Skin screening population:** ACG 2025 — annual melanoma screening for **all** IBD, NMSC for thiopurines/methotrexate/JAK/S1P; AGA 2025 — yearly total body skin exam for patients on immunomodulators, anti-TNF, or small molecules, continued after thiopurine cessation.
- **Pneumococcal:** ACG 2025 — a single PCV20 or PCV21 for vaccine-naive adults, no further doses needed (Figure 1 adds one more dose 5 years later for ≥65 who completed an adult series); AGA 2025 — initial vaccine at 19–64, second dose at ≥65.
- **HPV ages:** ACG 2025 — routine 9–26, shared decision 27–45; AGA 2025 — all adults 18–26, and 27–45 if likely to have a new sexual partner. Compatible in substance.
- **Melanoma downgrade:** 2017 graded melanoma screening strong; 2025 conditional, very low, despite meta-analyses showing no significant biologic- or thiopurine-associated melanoma increase.
- Newer conjugate pneumococcal vaccines (PCV15/20/21) and newer IBD agents (selective IL-23 inhibitors, S1P modulators, upadacitinib) lack vaccine-response data in IBD.
