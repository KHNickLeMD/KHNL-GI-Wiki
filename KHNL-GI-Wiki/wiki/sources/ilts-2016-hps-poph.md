## Summary

Official International Liver Transplant Society (ILTS) practice guidelines on the two pulmonary vascular disorders of liver disease: hepatopulmonary syndrome (HPS) and portopulmonary hypertension (POPH). They build on the 2002 European Respiratory Society (ERS) Task Force consensus on pulmonary-hepatic vascular disorders and are presented in five parts — I. Definitions/diagnostic criteria; II. HPS; III. POPH; IV. Implications for liver transplantation (LT); V. Suggestions for future clinical research.

The document is the wiki's source for the **numeric diagnostic criteria and severity bands** of both disorders, which the newer [[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-candidate-evaluation|AASLD/AST 2025]] candidate-evaluation guideline invokes but does not print. HPS requires liver disease, a positive contrast-enhanced transthoracic echocardiogram (CE-TTE) and an abnormal alveolar–arterial oxygen gradient; POPH requires portal hypertension plus a right heart catheterization (RHC) triad of mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP), pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) and pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP). Severity is graded by arterial oxygen tension in HPS and by mPAP in POPH.

The authors are explicit that the evidence base is thin — a MEDLINE review of over 1000 articles (1985–2015) yielded almost no randomized controlled trials in either disorder, so most statements rest on single-centre reports, small series, registries and expert opinion. Accordingly the great majority of the 63 recommendations are graded at the lowest quality level.

Clinically, the two disorders pull in opposite directions at transplant: severe hypoxemia from HPS is an *indication* for LT with Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) exception points, whereas POPH above the severity ceiling is an *absolute contraindication*, and POPH — treated or untreated — is not itself an indication.

## Recommendations

*All 63 numbered recommendations, with the document's own numbers and grades. Wording is condensed where the published statement is long; direction, force, population and every numeric qualifier are unchanged.*

### Part I — Definitions / diagnostic criteria

**Hepatopulmonary syndrome**

1. Follow the ERS Task Force diagnostic criteria for HPS in research and clinical settings. **(1C)**
2. Determine abnormal arterial oxygenation due to HPS by arterial blood gas (ABG). **(1C)**
3. When conducting and comparing HPS clinical studies, note that abnormal PaO₂ cutoffs for HPS may vary. **(2C)**

**Portopulmonary hypertension**

4. Follow the ERS Task Force diagnostic criteria for POPH in research and clinical settings. **(1C)**
5. Perform right heart catheterization to confirm hemodynamics consistent with POPH: **mPAP >25 mm Hg, PVR >3 Wood units (240 dynes/s per cm⁻⁵), and PAWP <15 mm Hg**. **(1B)**
6. Exclude other causes of pulmonary hypertension in the setting of liver disease. **(2B)**

### Part II — Hepatopulmonary syndrome

7. The ERS severity classification for HPS is appropriate for both clinical and clinical investigative studies, to allow uniform assessment. **(2C)**
8. Translational studies are needed to define whether mechanisms in animal models are relevant to treatment of human disease. **(2C)**
9. The clinical presentation of HPS may be variable and adversely affects quality of life and survival, so screening is appropriate — especially in liver transplant candidates. **(2B)**
10. Pulse oximetry (O₂ saturation **<96%**) is a reasonable screening test to detect hypoxemia in adults who are otherwise suitable LT candidates; **ABG determination of oxygenation is still necessary to diagnose HPS**. **(1C)**
11. CE-TTE is the optimal screening test and criterion in adults for detecting intrapulmonary vascular dilatations (IPVD). **(1B)**
12. Macroaggregated albumin (MAA) lung perfusion scans clarify the contribution of HPS-related hypoxemia when intrinsic cardiopulmonary disease coexists. **(1C)**
13. Aside from supplemental oxygen (rest, exercise and sleep), **no medical therapy is definitively established or FDA approved for HPS**. **(2B)**
14. Portal decompression with transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) is of **uncertain** benefit in HPS in adults. **(2C)**
15. Rarely, coil embolization may improve oxygenation in selected HPS patients. **(2C)**
16. Correction of congenital portosystemic shunts in children appears beneficial. **(2C)**
17. Sequential pulse oximetry is advised for oxygenation monitoring. **(1C)**

### Part III — Portopulmonary hypertension

18. POPH severity can be described by mPAP (assuming increased PVR): **mild 25 ≤ mPAP < 35 mm Hg; moderate 35 ≤ mPAP < 45; severe 45 ≤ mPAP**. **(1B)**
19. Risk factors for POPH exist — **female sex and autoimmune liver disease** — but they **should not be used for screening**. **(2B)**
20. The natural history of POPH may be affected by cardiac limitation or by direct complications of liver disease; monitor both clinically and simultaneously. **(1C)**
21. Screen for POPH with transthoracic Doppler echocardiography (TDE) in patients with portal hypertension who are **candidates for TIPS or LT**. **(1B)**
22. In LT candidates with portal hypertension, **repeat** TDE screening while waiting — **the optimal interval is unclear**. **(2C)**
23. The safety of anticoagulation in POPH has not been studied; **such therapy is not advised**. **(2C)**
24. **Do not initiate calcium channel blockers** to treat POPH. **(2C)**
25. Acute vasoreactivity testing during RHC to guide treatment in POPH is **not required**. **(2C)**
26. **Minimize beta-blockers** in POPH and treat varices by other means. **(2B)**
27. Agents approved for pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) may be useful in improving hemodynamics and exercise capacity in POPH. **(1C)**
28. Severe POPH (**mPAP ≥45–50 mm Hg**) is an **absolute contraindication to elective TIPS**. **(1C)**

### Part IV — Implications for liver transplantation: HPS

**Indication and risk**

29. Severe hypoxemia due to HPS (**PaO₂ <60 mm Hg**) should be considered an **indication for LT**, and such individuals should have **expedited** LT consideration. **(1B)**
30. Multicenter and recent United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) data suggest **pre-LT PaO₂ <45–50 mm Hg** is associated with increased transplant hospital mortality, morbidity, and severe hypoxemia post-LT. **(1B)**
31. Prospective center-specific data show selected HPS patients with **pre-LT PaO₂ <50 mm Hg** may have good outcomes, suggesting center-specific excellence. **(1C)**

**MELD / PELD exception**

32. Give **standard MELD exception** scores if PaO₂ is **<60 mm Hg** due to HPS. **(1B)**
33. Consider an **increased MELD exception score** (higher MELD points if **PaO₂ <50 mm Hg**) in view of recent UNOS post-LT data analysis. **(1B)**

**Intraoperative issues** *(the document numbers these 34 and 37)*

34. Continuously monitor mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO₂). If SvO₂ **falls below 65%** on vascular exclusion of the liver, venovenous bypass may be beneficial. **(2C)**
37. Intraoperative oxygenation is **not** adversely affected by different anesthetic deliveries. **(1B)**

**Posttransplant / intensive care**

36. Accomplish **early extubation** to prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia. **(1B)**
37. Use 100% inspired oxygen via face mask / noninvasive ventilation / nasal oxygen to maintain **O₂ saturation ≥85%**. **(1C)**
38. Conduct **goal-directed fluid therapy** to avoid fluid overload and pulmonary congestion. **(2C)**
39. Inhaled pulmonary vasodilators (nitric oxide) can be used to improve post-LT oxygenation. **(2B)**
40. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) can be used as a **bridge to LT** and to reduce the need for mechanical ventilation in severe HPS. **(2B)**

**Expected outcomes**

41. Resolution of HPS after LT (deceased or living donor) is **expected**; it is temporally related to pre-LT severity, may take several months, and may require continued supplemental oxygen. **(1B)**
42. Perform periodic oxygenation assessment by pulse oximetry to decide the need for supplemental oxygen. **(2C)**
43. **Discontinue supplemental oxygen when O₂ saturation remains >88%** at rest, on exercise and during sleep. **(2C)**
44. Post-LT CE-TTE is **not routinely advised** unless clinically indicated. **(2C)**

### Part IV — Implications for liver transplantation: POPH

**Indication and contraindication**

45. Unlike HPS, there are **no data to support POPH — treated or untreated — as an indication for LT**. **(2C)**
46. Initiate pulmonary-artery (PA)-targeted therapy in POPH if **mPAP ≥35 mm Hg**. **(2B)**
47. Inform patients that **pre-LT mPAP ≥35 mm Hg with increased PVR** is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. **(1B)**
48. POPH with **mPAP of 45 to 50 mm Hg or greater** should be considered an **absolute contraindication to LT**. **(1C)**

**MELD / PELD exception**

49. Consider MELD exception in correctly diagnosed POPH patients of at least moderate hemodynamic severity (**baseline mPAP >35 mm Hg**) **if** PA-targeted therapy brings **mPAP <35 mm Hg and PVR <400 dynes/s per cm⁻⁵**. **(1C)**
50. MELD exception **can** be considered if treated POPH does **not** reduce mPAP below 35 mm Hg **but** there is normalization of **PVR (<240 dynes/s per cm⁻⁵)** and of right ventricular (RV) function. **(2C)**

**Intraoperative issues**

51. Conduct PA catheter monitoring **prior to and during LT in all adult cases**. **(1C)**
52. Intraoperative pre-abdominal-incision **mPAP ≥35 mm Hg** should be assessed with cardiac output (CO), PAWP and PVR measurements. **(2C)**
53. If **mPAP exceeds 45 to 50 mm Hg before abdominal incision**, **defer LT** while RV function and reversibility of the POPH are assessed. **(1C)**
54. Administer PA-targeted therapy **continuously during LT**. **(1C)**
55. Have transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) readily available for RV monitoring in POPH. **(1C)**
56. Especially **during reperfusion**, inhaled nitric oxide or intravenous milrinone can be used to enhance pulmonary vasodilation. **(2C)**
57. ECMO can be used if an acute rise in mPAP occurs at reperfusion with RV dysfunction. **(2C)**
58. **Do not use right ventricular assist devices** in POPH — potential for PA rupture. **(2C)**

**Posttransplant / ICU / outpatient follow-up**

59. Continue PA-targeted therapy in the immediate post-LT period unless it is contributing to hemodynamic instability. **(1C)**
60. **Wean** POPH PA-targeted therapies rather than stopping them abruptly. **(1C)**
61. Conduct TDE **every 3 months** to guide weaning decisions. **(2C)**
62. Post-LT RHC is **not routinely advised** unless clinically indicated. **(2C)**
63. POPH counseling should emphasize **higher LT risk regardless of hemodynamics**. **(1B)**

### Part V — Suggestions for future clinical research (not graded)

Three ungraded research priorities: update the MELD exception criteria for both disorders (HPS indexed by duration and degree of hypoxemia; POPH by calculated MELD, PVR and RV function); formalize HPS/POPH phenotyping and multicentre data collection, including a registry, biologic samples, and study of a lower PVR cutoff for the POPH definition; and initiate prospective multicentre drug studies (angiogenesis inhibitors in HPS; endothelin receptor antagonists, growth factor inhibitors, estrogen blockers and prostacyclin receptor agents in POPH).

## Supporting commentary (condensed)

- **HPS mechanism** — dilation of capillary and precapillary vessels up to **100 µm** in diameter plus increased numbers of dilated vessels, producing ventilation–perfusion mismatch, diffusion limitation and anatomic shunting. Normal lung vascular capillary diameter is **8–15 µm**; agitated saline creates microbubbles **>10 µm** that normally do not pass the pulmonary capillary bed.
- **Timing distinguishes intrapulmonary from intracardiac shunt on CE-TTE** — left-heart microbubbles at **≥3 cardiac cycles** indicate intrapulmonary shunting; **1 to 2 cycles** indicate intracardiac shunting (patent foramen ovale, atrial septal defect). TEE can separate the two by imaging the source of the bubbles.
- **MAA lung perfusion scan** — peripheral-vein injection of 20 µm ⁹⁹ᵐTc-labelled macroaggregated albumin; brain **uptake >6%** after lung perfusion helps attribute hypoxemia to IPVD rather than to nonvascular lung parenchymal abnormality. Normal in non-HPS causes of hypoxemia, and cannot distinguish intracardiac from intrapulmonary shunting.
- **HPS clinical features** — platypnea (dyspnea worsening supine to upright) and orthodeoxia (**PaO₂ fall >5% or >4 mm Hg** moving supine to upright) occur in up to **25%**. Digital clubbing, cyanosis and diffuse telangiectasias are typical in advanced disease; diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide is often reduced.
- **HPS prevalence** — **5% to 30%** of adults with cirrhosis evaluated for LT; **3% to 20%** in children with cirrhosis. HPS doubles the risk of death versus cirrhosis of similar severity without it.
- **HPS drug therapy** — somatostatin, almitrine, indomethacin, norfloxacin, inhaled (nebulized) L-NAME, aspirin and plasma exchange have been tried in small studies without clear benefit; the norfloxacin randomized crossover pilot (9 patients) was negative; pentoxifylline results in humans are conflicting.
- **POPH epidemiology** — incidence at least **1 case per 3 million inhabitants per year**; **0.13%** of over 17 000 autopsied decedents and **0.73%** in cirrhosis; **5%** of 1235 prospectively evaluated LT candidates met hemodynamic criteria. POPH accounts for **5% to 10%** of the overall PAH population. Untreated 1-year survival **35% to 46%**.
- **Neither liver disease severity nor degree of portal hypertension (hepatic venous pressure gradient) predicts the presence or severity of POPH.**
- **TDE screening performance** — a pulmonary artery systolic pressure cutoff **>30 mm Hg** had a negative predictive value of **100%** but positive predictive value only **59%**; RHC is required for diagnosis. LT and TIPS candidates with **right ventricular systolic pressure >50 mm Hg** and/or significant RV hypertrophy or dysfunction likely require RHC.
- **POPH drug classes** named by the document: **prostacyclin analogues** (intravenous epoprostenol, intravenous/subcutaneous treprostinil, inhaled iloprost), **phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors** (sildenafil — esophageal variceal hemorrhage has been reported with its use), and **endothelin receptor antagonists** (bosentan; the selective agent ambrisentan; macitentan under trial). The FDA label recommends **avoiding any endothelin receptor antagonist in moderate to severe liver dysfunction or elevated transaminases**.
- **Why calcium channel blockers are avoided** — potential to increase fluid retention, reduce RV function, and increase portal pressures.
- **Why beta-blockers are minimized** — in patients with more advanced POPH (mean PAP ≥35–40 mm Hg), withdrawal of beta-blocker therapy may increase CO (via release of chronotropic response) and exercise capacity; the risk–benefit must be weighed against variceal bleeding risk, with band ligation as the alternative.
- **TIPS in POPH** — typical postprocedure changes are increased RV filling pressures and increased CO, usually well tolerated at significant elevations in PVR and RV volume or pressure overload. Congestive heart failure and severe pulmonary hypertension (mPAP ≥45 mm Hg) are absolute contraindications to TIPS; moderate POPH (35 ≤ mPAP < 45 mm Hg) is a relative contraindication.
- **UNOS HPS exception policy** — since December 2010, standard MELD (22 points) / PELD (28 points) exception for HPS if PaO₂ is **<60 mm Hg** and no other clinically significant pulmonary condition exists, increased every 3 months if the repeat PaO₂ remains below 60 mm Hg breathing room air in the sitting position. UNOS analysis of HPS exception patients documented a waitlist mortality of **8%**, significantly worse if pre-LT PaO₂ was **<44 mm Hg**.
- **UNOS POPH exception policy** — since December 2010, standard MELD (22) / PELD (28) exception for POPH patients with **mPAP >25 mm Hg and PVR >240 dynes/s per cm⁻⁵** who attain, with PA-targeted therapy, **improved mPAP <35 mm Hg and PVR <400 dynes/s per cm⁻⁵** from at least moderate baseline severity (mPAP >35 mm Hg). Waitlist mortality in POPH exceptions granted 2006–2014 (n = 190) was **7.0%**.
- **Transpulmonary gradient** — baseline (pretreatment) mPAP − PAWP should be determined to classify the effect of an increased PAWP from volume overload; **transpulmonary gradient <12 mm Hg** suggests pulmonary venous hypertension rather than true POPH and does not warrant MELD/PELD exception.
- **Post-LT POPH outcomes** — single-institution experience reports **29% to 64%** of moderate-to-severe POPH patients dismissed post-LT were able to discontinue PA-targeted therapy. Weaning is monitored by serial TDE, usually every 4 to 6 months.
- **HPS post-LT** — severe hypoxemia requiring 100% FiO₂ to maintain hemoglobin saturation ≥85% was associated with **45% mortality** in those with pretransplant PaO₂ <70 mm Hg. Recurrence of HPS post-LT is rare and related to recurrence of liver disease.
- **Figures/tables:** the document contains no clinical algorithm or decision figure; its three tables are Table 1 (diagnostic criteria — recreated above), Table 2 (references supporting LT outcomes in moderate-to-severe POPH, a bibliography map with no clinical content), and Table 3 (HPS vs POPH distinctions — recreated above).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **POPH candidacy ceiling — ILTS 2016 vs [[aasld-ast-2025-liver-transplant-candidate-evaluation|AASLD/AST 2025]].** ILTS Rec 48 makes **mPAP of 45 to 50 mm Hg or greater** with no stated PVR condition an absolute contraindication to LT. AASLD/AST 2025 instead pairs **45 mm Hg** with **PVR >3 Wood units**. AASLD/AST 2025 is the newer guideline, so **its combined mPAP + PVR rule is what the entity page asserts**; the ILTS range is recorded here and noted on the page as the older formulation. The two are compatible in direction and differ mainly in whether PVR is part of the contraindication.
- **The missing operator in AASLD/AST 2025 is resolved by neither document.** The entity page notes that AASLD/AST 2025 prints "a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 45 mm Hg" with no `≥` or `>`. ILTS 2016's own wording is a range ("45 to 50 mm Hg or greater"), so it cannot supply the missing operator either.
- **PVR units differ between recommendations within this document.** Rec 5 and the diagnostic table use **Wood units** (>3 WU = 240 dynes/s per cm⁻⁵); Recs 49 and 50 use **dynes/s per cm⁻⁵** (<400 and <240 respectively). 400 dynes/s per cm⁻⁵ is 5 Wood units. The page converts where it quotes them together.
- **Evidence quality is low throughout** — the document states outright that randomized controlled trials are lacking in both disorders and that consensus and expert opinion are often invoked for quality of evidence. Only a minority of the 63 recommendations reach Level B; **none reaches Level A**.
- **Unanswered by the document:** the optimal interval for repeat TDE screening in waitlisted candidates (Rec 22 says so explicitly); whether modifying the HPS MELD exception to grant higher points below PaO₂ 50 mm Hg would improve outcomes; and the process of weaning PA-targeted therapy post-LT, which no controlled study has addressed.
