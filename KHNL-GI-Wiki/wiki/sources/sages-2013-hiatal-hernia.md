## Summary

The **broad SAGES hiatal hernia document** — 28 graded statements covering definitions, diagnosis, indications for surgery, operative approach, sac excision, mesh, fundoplication, short esophagus, gastropexy, postoperative care, revisional surgery and pediatrics. [[sages-2024-hiatal-hernia-surgery|SAGES 2024]] answers only four focused questions and explicitly **complements rather than replaces** this document, so the 2013 guideline remains the source for everything the 2024 one does not address: the type I–IV anatomy, the natural history numbers behind watchful waiting, the operative steps, and the whole pediatric section.

Its central decisions are the two that separate a sliding hernia from a paraesophageal one. **A type I hernia is not itself an indication for repair** — [[gerd|gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)]] is, and the hernia is simply repaired as part of that operation. **A symptomatic paraesophageal hernia (PEH) is repaired**, with the strongest evidence rating in the document when obstructive symptoms or volvulus are present. Between those sits the asymptomatic PEH, where this guideline had already stepped away from the old "repair all PEH electively" dictum: the progression rate from asymptomatic to symptomatic is about **14% per year**, the annual risk of needing emergency surgery is probably **under 2%**, and decision modelling suggests elective repair in a completely asymptomatic patient **aged 65 or older may actually reduce quality-adjusted life expectancy**.

On mesh the document is openly split between two of its own statements, and the split is the point. **Guideline 11 says mesh reduces *short-term* recurrence (moderate quality, strong).** **Guideline 12 says the long-term data are inadequate to recommend either for or against mesh at the hiatus — and carries no grade at all.** The three randomized trials in its Table 1 are the reason: two showed large early reductions in radiographic recurrence (22% → 0%, 26% → 8%), and the third showed **59% vs 54% at a mean of 4.8 years (p=0.7)**, with both arms exceeding 50% recurrence at 4-year follow-up. The 2024 guideline later declined to make any mesh recommendation at all.

The operative content a non-surgeon still needs is the set of steps that define an adequate repair: **laparoscopy is preferred for most hiatal hernias**, the **sac is dissected off mediastinal structures and preferably excised**, the **gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) must end up below the diaphragm** with **at least 2–3 cm of intra-abdominal esophagus**, achieved by mediastinal dissection and/or gastroplasty, and a **fundoplication is mandatory in a type I repair** (and important in PEH repair). Gastropexy is safe as an addition; **gastropexy *alone* without hiatal repair is a fallback for the high-risk patient only** — one series reported **22% radiologic recurrence at 3 months**.

### Supporting data worth carrying

**Anatomy and epidemiology**

- **More than 95% of hiatal hernias are type I.** Among the paraesophageal hernias, **over 90% are type III**; **type II is the least common.**
- "**Giant** PEH" has no consistent definition — some use *all* type III and IV, but most restrict it to a PEH with **more than a third to a half of the stomach in the chest**.
- Mechanism: **depletion of elastin fibers** stretches the phrenoesophageal ligament and displaces the GEJ proximally. Most hernias are acquired; familial clustering is reported.
- **Recurrence is defined inconsistently** — some count any hernia seen on postoperative imaging or endoscopy, others only those **>2 cm**. Most small recurrences are **not clinically significant**.

**Natural history — the numbers behind watchful waiting**

- Progression from **asymptomatic to symptomatic PEH ≈ 14% per year**.
- Risk of developing acute symptoms **requiring emergency surgery < 2% per year**.
- **Emergency repair mortality 0–5.4%** in recent reports, though average mortality for emergency hiatal hernia surgery is around **17%**.
- **Decision modelling: elective repair of completely asymptomatic PEH may *decrease* quality-adjusted life expectancy in patients aged 65 and older.** Reserve repair for symptoms of **gastric outlet obstruction**, **severe reflux or anemia**, or **possible gastric strangulation**.
- **Only hernias in which the gastric fundus has migrated above the diaphragm — that is, PEH — are at risk of obstruction.**

**Symptoms that are easy to miss**

- With PEH, **heartburn and reflux symptoms are uncommon**; the patient more often describes **postprandial chest fullness or shortness of breath**, with respiratory symptoms from pulmonary compression and reduced forced vital capacity.
- **[[iron-deficiency-anemia|Iron deficiency anemia]] is seen in up to 50%** of patients with PEH, from gastric mucosal ischemia and ulceration.
- Pain — "a full or heavy feeling in the upper abdomen or a severe postprandial pain" — **is often relieved by vomiting**.

**Gastric volvulus**

- **Organoaxial** rotation (about the long axis, GEJ to pylorus) is the commonest type; **mesenteroaxial** (about the short axis) is less common.
- **Borchardt triad = severe epigastric pain + unproductive retching + inability to pass a nasogastric tube**, representing total gastric obstruction.
- Presentation of acute intrathoracic gastric volvulus: **progressive chest pain, severe vomiting, epigastric distention**.
- Emergency presentations may first be **decompressed with a nasogastric tube**, then plain chest radiograph and endoscopy; excessive investigation delays treatment.

**Diagnostic modalities**

- **A retrocardiac air–fluid level on chest X-ray is pathognomonic** for a paraesophageal hernia.
- **Contrast study** gauges size and reducibility, localizes the GEJ relative to the hiatus, and may raise suspicion of a **short esophagus** before the operation. **Avoid ionic water-soluble contrast** in PEH with acute gastric outlet obstruction — aspiration pneumonitis risk.
- **Computed tomography** is most useful urgently, for suspected complications of a volvulized PEH and for assessing herniated organs.
- **Manometry:** catheter placement across the lower esophageal sphincter and below the diaphragm **can be difficult in PEH**; a contrast swallow showing normal motility may substitute. A motility study **is** critical before antireflux surgery for a *sliding* hernia, to position the pH probe correctly.
- **pH testing** has limited role in diagnosing the hernia but is needed to confirm pathologic acid exposure before operating on a type I hernia — unless erosive esophagitis or [[barretts-esophagus|Barrett's esophagus]] is already seen endoscopically.

**Mesh — the three randomized trials (Table 1)**

| Trial | Entry | Mesh | Follow-up | Recurrence control → mesh | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frantzides (n=72) | hiatal defect **>8 cm** | keyhole polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) | 3.3 y | **22% → 0%** | <0.006 |
| Granderath (n=100) | symptomatic reflux | rectangular polypropylene onlay | 1 y | **26% → 8%** | <0.001 |
| Oelschlager (n=60) | hiatal defect **>5 cm** | U-shaped porcine small-intestinal submucosa | 4.8 y | **59% → 54%** | **0.7** |

- **No mesh-related complications in any of the three trials.** At 4-year follow-up of the third trial, **both arms exceeded 50% recurrence**.
- Primary sutured crural repair alone has been reported with recurrence **42% and higher** after laparoscopic PEH repair — the reason mesh was proposed.
- **Bridging synthetic mesh should be avoided**: mesh placed as a bridge is more likely to contact the esophagus directly and is probably associated with **erosion**. Other reported complications: esophageal stenosis, pericardial tamponade, effusion. Most series have median follow-up under 3 years.
- Fixation: inadequate evidence for an optimal technique, but **tacks must not breach the aorta or pericardium** when applied low on the left crus or near the apex of the crura anteriorly.

**Recurrence predictors**

- **Obesity** is an independent risk factor both for developing a hiatal hernia and for recurrence after repair.
- **Hiatal surface area >5.6 cm²** predicts recurrence, **independent of patient height, weight and body mass index**.
- Early postoperative **gagging, belching and vomiting** predispose to anatomical failure and the need for revision.
- Morbidity is higher in elderly and comorbid patients **but recurrence is not**; elderly mortality remains high after **emergency** PEH surgery (pulmonary complications, thromboembolism, hemorrhage).

**Short esophagus**

- Extensive mediastinal mobilization alone usually achieves 2–3 cm of intra-abdominal esophagus without tension and **may remove the need for a lengthening procedure**.
- If it does not, perform a **Collis gastroplasty**. The gastric neoesophagus **does not peristalse**, so postoperative dysphagia is a real risk, and gastroplasty increased postoperative leak rates in some series.
- **Vagotomy as an alternative lengthening maneuver cannot be recommended** on the single available study.

**Postoperative course**

- **Early dysphagia occurs in up to 50%** — advance the diet slowly from liquids to solids.
- Expect **10–15 lb (4.5–7 kg)** of weight loss after laparoscopic fundoplication and hernia repair. **Persistent dysphagia, or loss of more than 20 lb (>9 kg), should be evaluated.**
- Gastric distention in the immediate postoperative phase is dangerous — decompress with a nasogastric tube, or vent through an intraoperatively placed gastrostomy.
- **Gastropexy alone without crural repair or sac excision: 22% radiologic recurrence at 3 months.** Low mortality and morbidity, but inferior to formal repair — a fallback, not the aim.

**Revisional surgery**

- Indicated when **symptoms match the anatomical findings**. Take down any previous fundoplication **in its entirety**, expose both crura, excise the sac, and ensure adequate intra-abdominal esophageal length. **Recurrence rates remain higher than after primary repair.** Mesh can be used safely in revisional surgery, on inadequate and underpowered data.

**Pediatric**

- Incidence is low and high-quality data are lacking. Associations: familial inheritance, right isomerism, Marfan syndrome, collagen type III alpha 1 — but most cases are sporadic.
- **Children with a hiatal hernia and symptomatic reflux fail conservative management at high rates** (prospective trial, 718 patients) → repair with concomitant fundoplication.
- **Upper gastrointestinal contrast study is the most efficient and reliable test** to delineate the anatomy and exclude malrotation.
- Reflux is present in **over half** of children with hiatal hernia.
- Hernias diagnosed in infancy may mature and resolve; in a 20–40-year follow-up of 118 infants, the hernia persisted into adulthood in **53%** of those managed nonsurgically.
- **Minimal rather than extensive hiatal dissection during primary antireflux surgery cut postoperative PEH from 30% to 7.8%** in a randomized trial of 177 children.
- **Transabdominal repair is preferred by most pediatric surgeons.**

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- ⚠ **Internal tension on mesh, carried as published.** Guideline 11 recommends mesh for reduced **short-term** recurrence (⊕⊕⊕, strong); Guideline 12 states the long-term data cannot support a recommendation **either way** and is the **only statement in the document with no grade at all**. Both are reproduced; neither is reconciled.
- ⚠ **Superseded on the four questions [[sages-2024-hiatal-hernia-surgery|SAGES 2024]] re-asked.** Within the same society and tier, the newer document governs where they overlap: fundoplication during type II–IV repair, mesh, repair of the asymptomatic hernia, and conversion to Roux-en-Y gastric bypass for recurrence. 2024 is a formal GRADE guideline with evidence profiles; this one is a narrative review with graded statements. **2013 remains the only source for everything 2024 does not ask**, which is most of this document.
- ⚠ **The 2024 guideline makes no mesh recommendation at all**, having considered and rejected a conditional recommendation against routine mesh. The 2013 statement 11 (*in favour*, short-term) therefore does **not** stand as current society guidance and should not be read as one.
- **Guideline 8 and Guideline 9 together are a preference, not a prohibition** — transthoracic repair remains "effective" at the document's highest evidence rating while laparoscopy is preferred for the majority. The document calls the transthoracic approach obsolete except in rare circumstances, in expert opinion, not in a graded statement.
- **No hernia-size threshold for repair, and no body mass index threshold**, in this document either — the same gaps [[sages-2024-hiatal-hernia-surgery|SAGES 2024]] declines to fill. The only size figure given is a **recurrence** predictor (hiatal surface area >5.6 cm²), not an indication.
- **Pediatric hiatal hernia is out of scope for every other source in the wiki**, so statements 23–28 have no counterpart to be checked against.
