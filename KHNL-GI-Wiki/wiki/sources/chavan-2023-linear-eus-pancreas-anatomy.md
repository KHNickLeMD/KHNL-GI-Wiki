## Summary
- Teaching essay on how to find and trace the whole pancreas at endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) with a **curved linear array echoendoscope**, organized as a **station-wise approach**: stomach (gastroesophageal (GE) junction, push method — Station 1A via the celiac artery, Station 1B via the liver hilum; or distal stomach body, pull method), duodenal bulb (Station 2), and descending duodenum (Station 3) (Fig. 4; Figs. 6–13).
- Each station has a **homebase** structure to find first, then clockwise / anticlockwise torque with small push/pull movements traces the gland (homebase: Station-Wise Approach, p. 91 [4]; torque with minimal push/pull: Introduction, p. 88 [1]).
- Opens with general principles: orientation from the image, the "rule of 360 degrees," tracing a structure from screen centre, vessel identification, air-artifact reduction, and station-specific tips for stomach, bulb and second part of the duodenum (D2) (pp. 88–91 [1–4]).
- Also gives normal pancreatic duct calibre and endosonographic signs of pancreas divisum, cited from other papers (p. 94 [7], p. 95 [8], p. 97 [10]).

## Key Findings / Claims

### General principles (Basic Principles of EUS Examination, pp. 88–91 [1–4])
- **Scanning range:** curved linear array 120 to 180 degrees (radial = 360-degree view) → the scope must be rotated clockwise or anticlockwise to see a structure completely (Introduction, p. 88 [1]; "Rule of 360 degrees," p. 90 [3]).
- **Orientation from the image:** descending aorta or pancreas on screen = transducer facing posteriorly; left lobe of liver or heart = facing anteriorly (Introduction, p. 88 [1]).
- **Terminology** (Fig. 1, p. 89 [2]): big knob down = toward you; big knob up = away from you; clockwise / anticlockwise rotation.
- **Rule of 360 degrees** (p. 90 [3], Fig. 2): an extra 180-degree rotation is needed for the complete image; a point can be reached by a **270-degree clockwise** or a **90-degree anticlockwise** rotation — e.g., from the GE junction the spleen is reached by clockwise rotation while tracing the pancreas (270 degrees) or from the left liver lobe by anticlockwise rotation (90 degrees).
- **Tracing a structure** (p. 90 [3], Fig. 3): bring it to screen centre (around 6 o'clock), then rotate; if at the extreme left/right of the screen, add minimal push/pull to centre it first.
- **Vessels** (p. 90 [3]): trace from origin to end or branching; veins (oval) change shape along their course and on compression, arteries (round/tubular) keep their shape.
- **Endoscopic vs EUS view** (p. 89 [2]): transducer sits 1–1.5 cm distal to the camera — pyloric ring seen endoscopically means the transducer is already in the duodenal bulb. Navigate between stations under endoscopic vision to avoid complications.
- **Don't trust the scope's insertion-depth markings** to locate the scope; read position from the EUS image (e.g., left atrium + right pulmonary artery = mid-esophagus) (p. 89 [2]).
- **Air artifacts** (pp. 89–90 [2–3]): suction air, keep mucosa–transducer contact with the big knob, water immersion; avoid needless insufflation.
- **Image optimization** (p. 91 [4]): adjust gain, contrast, depth, frequency and focus.
- **Normal echotexture** (p. 91 [4], Fig. 5): "salt and pepper." Ventral pancreas (posterior head + uncinate process) hypoechoic; dorsal pancreas (anterior head, genu, body, tail) hyperechoic.
- **All stations needed:** whole pancreas can be seen from the stomach, but scan from all stations (p. 91 [4]).

### Station-specific tips and pitfalls
- **GE junction resistance on clockwise rotation** = scope hitting the lesser curvature → switch to endoscopic view, torque anticlockwise, push into the proximal body (Imaging from stomach, p. 91 [4]).
- **Stomach:** suction all air until the wall collapses onto the transducer; clockwise shows left-sided structures, anticlockwise right-sided (pp. 90–91 [3–4]).
- **Difficult pylorus:** pull/push the scope two to three times from the body-antrum so the stomach remodels (Imaging from the duodenal bulb, p. 91 [4]).
- **Bulb compresses structures:** instil water, release big-knob pressure, use fine clockwise/anticlockwise movements (p. 91 [4]).
- **Entering D2:** unlike a duodenoscope in endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), start shortening the echoendoscope **from the bulb** under endoscopic vision — small wheel to the right + clockwise shaft torque; **never push against resistance at D1–D2**; in D2 replace air with water for acoustic coupling and stability (Duodenal part 2, p. 91 [4]).
- **Obese patients:** trace the splenic vessels, which run parallel to the pancreas; identify the adjacent left renal vessels separately (p. 94 [7]; Fig. 8B–D).
- **Pancreatic tail:** always trace to the splenic hilum so tail pathology is not missed (p. 92 [5]; Fig. 8F).
- **Ampulla:** release the big knob and instil water to push the ampulla away so small stones or lesions are not missed (p. 97 [10]).
- **Pull method trade-off:** no resistance as in the push method, but the pancreas is harder to find without a homebase; not commonly used (p. 95 [8]).
- **Learning order:** orientation and scope stability in the descending duodenum can be difficult at first — learn this station once confident at the other stations (p. 97 [10]).

### Pancreatic duct and pancreas divisum (p. 94 [7], p. 95 [8], p. 97 [10])
- PD = small, tubular, anechoic, central; no special manoeuvre to trace it. Assess dilatation, wall echogenicity, intraductal filling defect (e.g., calculi), anatomical variation (p. 94 [7]).
- **Normal PD diameter (cited from Sharma 2015):** 4 mm head, 3 mm body, 2 mm tail; physiological dilatation may be seen in elderly people (p. 94 [7]).
- **Pancreas divisum (cited):** most common congenital PD anomaly, found in 10% of the general population; diagnosis matters especially in idiopathic recurrent acute pancreatitis; EUS sensitivity 51–95% ("moderate"), diagnostic accuracy 97% (p. 94 [7]).
- **Divisum signs:** normally the PD is stacked behind the CBD in the bulb (**stack sign**). Absence of the stack sign + presence of the **crossed duct sign** (PD crossing the CBD) indicates divisum (p. 95 [8]). Inability to trace the PD from the major papilla to the body from D2 also suggests divisum (p. 97 [10]). Divisum can be precisely diagnosed from the duodenal bulb (p. 94 [7]).

### Training advice (Table 1, p. 89 [2]; Conclusion, pp. 97–98 [10–11])
- Table 1, beginner's guide (14 items, condensed): know cross-sectional imaging; study EUS materials; hands-on animal/simulator training; learn scope handling and knob terminology; learn orientation; identify structures first, starting with easy ones (aorta, left liver lobe); find a homebase at each station; one structure and one station at a time; record and label your own videos and confirm with mentors; normal before abnormal; discuss difficulties with peers/mentors; practise daily.
- Examine the pancreas during every EUS examination (Conclusion, p. 97 [10]).

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Normal PD calibre vs CP criterion on [[endoscopic-ultrasound]]:** this essay calls 4 mm (head) / 3 mm (body) / 2 mm (tail) normal (p. 94 [7]), whereas the page's conventional chronic pancreatitis (CP) criterion from [[apa-2014-chronic-pancreatitis|American Pancreatic Association (APA) 2014]] (Table 4, PDF p. 64) counts main duct dilatation at >3 mm head, >2 mm body, >1 mm tail. The guideline threshold takes priority; a 4 mm head duct called "normal" here would meet the APA dilatation criterion. APA 2014's own text (PDF p. 16) gives average normal diameters of 3.6, 2.7 and 1.6 mm (head, body, tail) and an upper limit of normal as high as 6.5, 5 and 3 mm, so "normal" calibre is not settled in either source.
- **Homebase wording in the bulb:** text says portal vein (PV) (p. 95 [8]); Fig. 12A caption says main portal vein (MPV) — same structure, different label.
- **"SMV" used two ways:** superior mesenteric vein in the text, but "superior mesenteric vessels (SMVs)" in the Fig. 10 and Fig. 13 captions.
- **Fig. 11B caption** calls the umbilical portion the "left portal vein (LPV)," while its own abbreviation list separates UPV (umbilical portion) from LPV.
- Pull-method rationale is internally mixed: it begins in the distal stomach body/duodenal bulb and is "not commonly used," yet the essay also says starting from the bulb "may be a good practice" because most pathology is in the head (p. 95 [8]).
- Diagnostic-performance figures for divisum (51–95% sensitivity, 97% accuracy) and the 10% prevalence are cited from other papers (refs 14–16), not generated by this essay.
