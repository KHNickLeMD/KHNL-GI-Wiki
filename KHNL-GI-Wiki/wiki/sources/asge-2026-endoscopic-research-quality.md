## Summary
- **Purpose:** ASGE quality measures to guide the **design, conduct, and reporting** of observational studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in endoscopy (p. 521).
- **Out of scope:** nonclinical studies, artificial intelligence–related work, and meta-analysis are not directly addressed (p. 521).
- **Not minimum standards:** the measures are "not comprehensive" and not intended as minimum standards for funding, regulatory approval, publication, or acceptance of findings — a framework for improving research quality, "akin to the role adenoma detection rate plays in improving colonoscopy quality" (p. 521; Discussion, p. 528).
- **Methods (p. 521):**
  - Task force of members of the ASGE Quality Assurance in Endoscopy and Research Committees plus research-methodology thought leaders; proposed measures drafted by task-force consensus through meetings and electronic communications.
  - Literature review per measure: systematic PubMed search from inception **through June 2025** (MeSH subject headings + keywords), PubMed "similar articles", and manual reference-list review.
  - Evidence drawn from other areas of medicine and surgery, because the measures are not unique to endoscopy; **expert opinion** used for important measures with limited evidence (unstructured, agreed by all authors).
  - Final list refined by group consensus; **unanimous consensus regarding all measures** (not required for inclusion).
- **Output:** **9 quality measures** (Table 1, p. 522; "we propose 9 measures", Discussion, p. 528). The measures are **numbered 1–9** in the document; **no grade, strength, evidence level, performance target, or agreement percentage** is attached to any measure.
- **Discussion (pp. 528–529):** measures judged practical and achievable were included in this **first version**; some need no extra resources, others need statistics or health-equity partners; high-fidelity data verification, blinding and trial oversight cost more but were judged worthwhile. Ideals will not be fully met in every project — even partial application is likely of value. Not all concepts are, or could be, evidence-based (expert opinion, experience, logic). Content expected to evolve; training programs should consider formal education around the document.

## Key Findings / Claims
Each measure's commentary, condensed (all ungraded discussion text).

### 1. Reporting guidelines (p. 522)
- Reporting checklists double as a **blueprint for study structure** → consult them **early in the design phase**, not only as a post hoc submission checklist.
- **Study registration** — standard practice for clinical trials, included in most reporting guidelines — should occur **a priori**.
- Reporting guidelines optimize reporting; **not to be used in isolation as study-design tools**, but a helpful adjunct.
- Enhancing the Quality and Transparency of Health Research (EQUATOR) Network = umbrella resource (equator-network.org).
- Suboptimal reporting has been commonplace and remains a problem in biomedical and endoscopy research.

**Table 2 — Reporting guidelines by study type** (p. 523):

| Study type | Reporting guideline |
|---|---|
| Randomized trial | Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT)* |
| Observational study | Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE)* |
| Systematic review or meta-analysis | Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)* |
| Diagnostic or prognostic study | Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) |
| Quality improvement study | Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) |
| Economic study | Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) |
| Animal preclinical study | Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) |
| Case report | CAse REport (CARE) |
| Clinical practice guideline | Appraisal of Guidelines for REsearch & Evaluation (AGREE) |

### 2. Sample size (pp. 522–524)
- Prospective **and retrospective** studies: consider sample size and statistical power early in design, using available evidence (principle of beneficence — volunteers' altruism not to be spent on a study unlikely to give valid, meaningful results).
- Partner with statisticians (formulas out of scope); define the assumptions and practicalities (e.g., anticipated loss to follow-up).
- **Core assumptions:** anticipated event rate (categorical outcomes) or response variable/control rate (continuous outcomes), and anticipated effect size — from published evidence and/or unbiased preliminary data.
- **Generally accepted error margins:** **5%** probability of a false-positive result (type I error) and **20%** probability of a false-negative result (type II error) (p. 523).
- Small (underpowered) studies risk **type II and also type I** error (imprecision from few outcome events) → positive results of small studies need replication in larger samples.
  - Example: small pilot trial of aggressive Lactated Ringer's solution for post–endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) pancreatitis — **0% vs 17%; P = .016**; larger trials showed a more modest risk reduction (p. 523).
- **Do not overestimate the effect size to shrink the sample** — a common practice in endoscopic research; a positive study of that kind is less believable. Design to detect a smaller but still clinically relevant effect (example given: even if a hemostatic agent is expected to cut postresection bleeding by 50%) (p. 523).
- Check feasibility of enrolling the needed sample within budget and on time **before study initiation**.
- **Raising event rates in categorical-outcome trials:**
  - Enrich for **high-risk** patients — overly restrictive criteria may limit generalizability, but this is necessary for most hypotheses, especially early in the research continuum (example: the initial rectal indomethacin post-ERCP pancreatitis trial was restricted to high-risk patients; later studies extended it to broader populations).
  - **Extend follow-up** where appropriate — balanced against cost and attrition.
- Case-control and cohort studies: measure statistical power **a priori**; increase it by prolonging follow-up or adding sites/broader populations.
- **Multicenter studies (p. 524):** select sites on realistic enrollment expectations vs the predefined sample size (e.g., a pre-launch data query log of potentially eligible patients); site selection should also consider generalizability (geography; community/academic/private; rural/urban).

### 3. Representative study sample (p. 524)
- Eligibility criteria should enroll patients as similar as possible to the population the findings will be applied to; some extrapolation is common and necessary.
- Examples of non-representative samples given:
  - Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) RCTs criticized as too restrictive (healthier, less complicated patients than real-world practice), though still the cornerstone of IBD management.
  - Pancreatic cyst risk once inferred from surgically resected, higher-risk series → **overestimated cancer risk and overuse of surgery**; endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and cyst fluid studies restricted to patients who went to surgery share this limitation. Likened to early Barrett's esophagus high-grade dysplasia literature.
  - Lifetime gastric cancer risk with "hereditary diffuse gastric syndrome (*CDH1* mutations)": early estimates **40% to 60%**; later studies with more representative samples of varying familial risk found **7% to 10%**.
- Weigh each inclusion/exclusion criterion against applicability to the broadest appropriate population — including women, minorities and traditionally overlooked groups; comorbidity exclusions may reduce confounding and protect safety but limit generalizability. Justify each criterion.
- **Include all patients in whom the intervention was attempted**, not only technical successes — define eligibility by disease, not by completed intervention (example: all chronic pancreatitis patients, not only those with complete duct clearance by intraductal lithotripsy).
  - Example: an over-the-scope clip (OTSC) RCT for nonvariceal upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding excluded lesions judged unfavorable for OTSC → results apply only to OTSC candidates, not all bleeding patients.
- Observational research: include all patients who might have received the intervention but did not (**intention-to-treat** principle); per-protocol (technically successful only) analysis as a secondary sensitivity analysis.

### 4. Outcomes and exposures defined a priori, as objective as possible (pp. 524–525)
- Define exposures and outcomes **a priori**, objective and reproducible by other investigators.
- Cohort and case-control studies are prone to recall and observer bias → standardized measurement methods with clear instructions and definitions.
- Observational studies: **duplicate and independent data abstraction**; blinding the abstractor to the study hypothesis may further reduce bias.
- RCTs: standardized approach (e.g., manual of operations), clear definitions, and **blinded outcome assessment** (the text cross-refers to "measure 7"; blinding is measure 6).
- Adverse events are rare → most comparisons of two techniques are underpowered and draw inappropriate safety conclusions. Options:
  - **Composite outcome** — raises event rate and power, but choose carefully (results can be hard to interpret and translate).
  - **Large databases/registries** (e.g., sedation-related adverse events) — mind incomplete variable documentation and incomplete capture of eligible patients.
- Attributing adverse events to the intervention is ambiguous → use tools such as the consensus **causal attribution instrument for post-ERCP adverse events**, especially with unblinded investigators.

### 5. Data integrity (pp. 525–526)
- Data should be **attributable, accurate, complete, and consistent**, minimizing error and missingness.
- **Three steps** against random and systematic error:
  1. A credible, reliable data source, with a systematic approach to capturing **all potentially eligible patients** in it.
  2. A standardized approach to data measurement and extraction — training of all research personnel, clear definitions per data item, carefully designed case report forms (CRFs); **validated instruments "strongly recommended"** whenever applicable.
  3. Data validation and verification, cross-checking, routine data monitoring (electronic data capture platforms help).
- Administrative-data studies: reporting guidelines exist to assure data quality.
- **Duplicate and independent data extraction** — limits random error, likely mitigates bias, and gives trainees meaningful participation; a dedicated data manager can help if feasible.
- Data safety: secure storage and transmission, encryption, adherence to data-protection regulations.
- **A priori data monitoring plan** — critical, especially in multicenter research (one site's misapplication has multiplicative effects); central monitoring spots trends early.
  - Example: in the rectal indomethacin trial, no monitoring apparatus → missed enrollment enriching for sphincter of Oddi dysfunction (SOD), the trial's most important limitation.
  - Low-cost options are still an improvement: central monitoring of aggregate eligibility trends; an unfunded multicenter ERCP vs percutaneous drainage trial (suspected malignant hilar obstruction) aimed to centrally review **approximately 20%** of randomized patients' records; a large Canadian upper GI bleeding registry mandated duplicate data entry in a fraction of patients (p. 526).

### 6. Blinding in RCTs (pp. 526–527)
- Underused in endoscopic research because masking invasive procedures is hard, but achievable at least partly, often simply.
- **Four levels of blinding** (p. 526):

| Level | Bias if unblinded |
|---|---|
| (1) Patient | Symptom interpretation, threshold to seek care, questionnaire responses |
| (2) Treating clinician(s) | Differential care / unequal co-interventions (e.g., closer monitoring when prophylaxis was withheld after high-bleeding-risk endoscopy) |
| (3) Outcome assessor(s) | Unconscious interpretation in favor of the intervention ("positive" results are incentivized) |
| (4) Statistician(s) | Partial blinding: treatment indicator (A or B) known, but not which treatment it is |

- **Concealed allocation** should be standard practice in clinical trials.
- Few end points are truly absolute — even death needs attribution; Revised Atlanta acute pancreatitis criteria vary with pain expression and imaging interpretation; the consensus post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP) definition depends on the nonobjective decision to hospitalize (high-propensity admitters may record more PEP); resource utilization can also be biased.
- **Even when patients and clinicians cannot be blinded, always consider masking the assessors.**
- Endoscopic interventions may need a separate masking step per level. Example (pancreas divisum, minor papillotomy RCT): sham arm had EUS, prophylactic pancreatic stent dropped in the duodenum (no unmasking by stent passage), blinded medical record for the endoscopy report, and a non-ERCP blinded provider for follow-up and outcome adjudication.
- Blinding at one level may be enough: a standard vs disposable-cap duodenoscope RCT did not blind patients or clinicians, but technical success was adjudicated by a blinded panel from procedure reports redacted of endoscope type, not by the endoscopists.
- Extent of blinding depends on study phase (phase 3 vs exploratory), resources (funded vs pilot), and end points (more subjective → more masking effort).
- **Bottom line (p. 527):** actively consider blinding — **at least at the level of the outcome adjudicator — in all RCTs**; open-label trials should discuss the potential impact of group awareness in the manuscript.

### 7. Independent data and safety oversight of RCTs (p. 527)
- Data and safety monitoring board (DSMB): protects participant safety and well-being, and with it study validity; provides methodologic guidance and accountability.
- Ideally all RCTs under a DSMB (as mandated by the National Institutes of Health for federally funded trials); meets **before study launch and on a schedule** throughout.
- **DSMB responsibilities:**
  1. Threats to trial validity — accrual/retention rates, protocol adherence, data quality.
  2. Safety concerns — adverse event data.
  3. Planned interim analyses — advise and review, including futility guidance.
  - Plus oversight of protocol changes during the trial, so they add no risk of bias.
- Operates under a **DSMB charter approved before trial initiation** that ensures independent decision-making; typically subject-matter experts and methodologists.
- **Single-center or pilot studies:** a **2-member or 3-member** DSMB may suffice, with less extensive duties, provided independence is maintained.

### 8. Inclusive and equitable research (pp. 527–528)
- Equity should be woven into all research, not a niche — study choice, patient selection, data collection, interpretation and reporting.
- Examples of research inequity cited: race-based performance of glomerular filtration rate, pulmonary function testing and pulse oximetry; under-representation of Black and Hispanic patients in oncology trials; esophageal cancer prevention research focused on Barrett's esophagus/adenocarcinoma although squamous cell carcinoma (predominantly affecting African Americans) is more common and costly in the United States; the research gap between IBD and chronic pancreatitis.
- Diagnostic/predictive work (conventional or artificial intelligence): unbiased data collection, training-set variable selection, data classifiers and model development so the deliverable works equally for all patients.
- Partner with health-equity experts early; in large trials, engage community representatives from diverse populations.
- Aim of the measure: active consideration of equity at the individual-study level.

### 9. Conflicts of interest (p. 528)
- Definition quoted: "a set of circumstances that creates a risk that professional judgment or actions regarding a primary interest will be unduly influenced by a secondary interest."
- Secondary interests: financial, but also professional advancement, recognition and fame, and favors to one's social network.
- Industry-sponsored trials report more positive findings and fewer adverse events; guideline authors often had substantial industry support, **approximately 25%** of which was not disclosed.
- **Nonfinancial (intellectual) conflicts** — preconceived bias, worldview — may be equally influential; management approaches are undeveloped.
- Mitigation: education on conscious and unconscious bias, public registration of all protocols; **disclosure is the cornerstone** — conflicts are underreported in some **40% to 60%** of cases, so complete disclosure is a fundamental first step.
- Encourage **contextual disclosure forms** (commercial relations relative to the topic); investigators with commercial conflicts should state in the manuscript how the conflict may have biased the study and what mitigated it.
