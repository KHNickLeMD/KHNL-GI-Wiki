## Summary

The ASCRS update of its 2011 practice parameters, and the **only operative guideline on rectal prolapse in the corpus**. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]] gives [[rectal-prolapse]] one conditional sentence — full-thickness prolapse often requires abdominal rectopexy, or a perineal procedure in selected cases — and the page carried a standing note that no ingested source graded prolapse severity or covered non-operative management. This document supplies both, plus the pre-referral workup and the two patient-selection rules that actually change which operation gets done.

**The nonoperative answer is blunt: rectal prolapse cannot be corrected without surgery** (weak, 2C). What can be treated medically are the symptoms — fiber and stool softeners for constipation, **topical table sugar to reduce edema and assist reduction of an incarcerated prolapse**, and skin care. ⚠ And the guideline argues against waiting: patients managed medically **develop irreversible fecal incontinence (FI)**, and **leaving prolapse untreated beyond 4 years is associated with higher recurrence after later repair**, presumably through a secondarily weakened pelvic floor. Elderly patients are told to act quickly too.

Two selection rules drive the operation. **First, when prolapse and FI coexist, fix the prolapse first** — many patients' continence improves once the prolapse is gone — and the constipation-inducing manoeuvres (lateral-ligament division) are actually *desirable* in that patient. **Second, coexisting constipation or dyssynergia disqualifies the manoeuvres that worsen it** — posterior rectal mobilization, lateral-ligament transection during suture rectopexy, and levatorplasty during perineal proctectomy. The same logic sets the one hard technical rule: **posterior mobilization without a rectopexy is not recommended** (strong, 1B) — a randomized trial found an **8-fold increase in recurrence** with mobilization alone, and **rectopexy is a key component of the abdominal approach** (strong, 1A).

The one length-based threshold in the document is for the **Delorme procedure — a short (<5 cm) full-thickness prolapse**. Otherwise the choice between abdominal and perineal approaches is by comorbidity, surgeon experience, age and bowel function, and the document explicitly declines to rank **posterior (suture/resection rectopexy) against anterior (ventral mesh) repair** — insufficient evidence either way (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence, 1C).

## Key Findings / Claims

### Grading and numbering

GRADE per Guyatt et al.: **1A/1B/1C** strong on high / moderate / low-quality evidence; **2A/2B/2C** weak on the same. **The document numbers its recommendations within each section, restarting at 1 each time** — the section headings below are the document's, and the numbers are its own. **16 recommendations in total.**

### Associated findings and epidemiology

- **FI in 50%–75%**; **constipation in 25%–50%** of patients with rectal prolapse.
- **Up to half demonstrate pudendal neuropathy**, which may cause denervation atrophy of the external sphincter.
- **Urinary incontinence in 20%–35%**; **significant vaginal vault prolapse in ≈15%–30%** — multivisceral prolapse needs a multidisciplinary approach.
- **Proctoscopy shows an anterior solitary rectal ulcer in 10%–15%.**
- **Goals of surgery are threefold:** eliminate the prolapse · correct associated constipation or incontinence · **avoid creating de novo bowel dysfunction**.

### Evaluation of Rectal Prolapse (3 recommendations)

1. **Initial evaluation = complete history and physical, focused on the prolapse, anal sphincter structure and function, and concomitant symptoms and underlying conditions.** — Strong, low-quality, **1C**
   - If history suggests it but examination does not show it: have the patient **strain on a toilet, with or without an enema or a rectal balloon**, and inspect the perineum **sitting or squatting**. If still elusive, the patient can **photograph it at home**, or undergo echography.
   - ⚠ **Do not confuse it with prolapsing internal hemorrhoids or rectal mucosal prolapse:** full-thickness prolapse always has **concentric folds**; hemorrhoids or mucosa give **radial invaginations**.
   - Expect a **patulous anus with diminished sphincter tone**.
2. **Additional testing — fluoroscopic or MRI defecography, colonoscopy, barium enema, urodynamics — may be used selectively** to refine the diagnosis and find coexisting pathology. — Strong, moderate-quality, **1B**
   - ⚠ **Colonoscopy should typically be performed before surgery**: rarely a **neoplasm forms the lead point**, the population is older, and a finding **may change the operative plan**.
   - Defecography also reveals anterior support defects (cystocele, vaginal vault prolapse, enterocele). Anterior-compartment or urinary-incontinence symptoms → urodynamics and urogynecologic examination, to allow concomitant repair of both compartments.
3. **Anal physiologic testing may be considered** to assess and treat coexisting constipation or FI. — Weak, low-quality, **2C**
   - **Decreased pudendal nerve terminal motor latency may predict more incontinence after prolapse repair** (needs confirmation).

### Nonoperative Management (1 recommendation)

1. **Rectal prolapse cannot be corrected nonoperatively**, although the associated FI, pain and constipation **can be palliated medically**. — Weak, low-quality, **2C**
   - **No reports of prolapse resolving with medical therapy alone.**
   - Palliation: **fiber and stool softeners** for constipation · **table sugar applied topically** to reduce edema and assist reduction of an **incarcerated** prolapse · **skin care** to avoid maceration.
   - **Pre-treating incontinence symptoms before surgery produced better postoperative continence** (retrospective, 139 women).
   - ⚠ **Against delay, including in the elderly:** patients managed medically only **develop irreversible FI**, and **prolapse left untreated beyond 4 years** is associated with **higher recurrence** after eventual repair.

### Abdominal Procedures (2 recommendations)

1. **In patients with acceptable risk, the procedure of choice should typically incorporate transabdominal rectal fixation.** — Weak, moderate-quality, **2B**
   - Recurrence after abdominal surgery is **approximately one fourth** that after perineal surgery in retrospective series.
   - ⚠ The abdominal-vs-perineal choice "continues to be debated"; the document asks for consideration of comorbidities and bowel dysfunction rather than a rule.
2. **Insufficient evidence that posterior repairs (suture or resection rectopexy) are better or worse than anterior repairs (ventral mesh rectopexy).** — Strong, low-quality, **1C**
   - Both **<10% recurrence at 10-year follow-up** retrospectively, with less long-term data for ventral rectopexy. **Choice driven by surgical expertise, surgical history, comorbidities and patient preference.**

### Posterior Rectal Dissection (1 recommendation)

1. **Posterior rectal mobilization without a rectopexy (±anterior resection) is associated with higher recurrence and complications and is typically not recommended.** — Strong, moderate-quality, **1B**
   - Mobilization + low anterior resection, 113 patients: recurrence climbing **3% / 6% / 12% at 2 / 5 / 10 years**, operative morbidity **29%** including 3 anastomotic leaks. Another review: **7%** recurrence at 6 years mean.
   - Low pelvic anastomoses in borderline continence may cost further function.

### Posterior Suture Rectopexy ± Sigmoid Resection (3 recommendations)

1. **Rectopexy is a key component of the abdominal approach.** — Strong, high-quality, **1A**
   - Suture rectopexy recurrence **3%–9% at 2 years**; ⚠ **the Rectal Prolapse Recurrence Study Group suggests 29% at 10 years**.
   - RCT, 116 mobilization-only vs 136 rectopexy patients: **8-fold increase in recurrence with mobilization alone**.
   - ⚠ **It can create or worsen constipation:** **≥50%** of patients with preoperative constipation report worsening, and **≈15%** with none beforehand become constipated. **Severe preoperative constipation → do more than a suture rectopexy** (resection rectopexy, ventral rectopexy, or a perineal repair).
2. **Sigmoid resection may be added to posterior suture rectopexy in patients with prolapse and preoperative constipation.** — Strong, moderate-quality, **1B**
   - Recurrence **2%–5%**, major complications **0%–20%**; two RCTs found less postoperative constipation than rectopexy alone.
   - ⚠ **Avoid after a failed previous perineal rectosigmoidectomy** — transabdominal resection then risks **ischemic bowel**.
   - ⚠ **Resolution of FI is lower when sigmoid resection is performed**; some experts would not offer it to patients with **markedly reduced anal pressures on manometry or severe baseline incontinence**. **Not usually combined with mesh repairs.**
3. **Division of the lateral stalks may worsen postoperative constipation but is associated with decreased recurrence.** — Weak, moderate-quality, **2B**
   - Cochrane review, 15 RCTs, 1,007 patients: **less recurrence, more postoperative constipation** — "especially attractive in the patients presenting with fecal incontinence without constipation."

### Posterior Mesh Rectopexy (2 recommendations)

1. **Posterior mobilization with mesh fixation of the anterior rectal wall to the sacral promontory may be used, but is associated with higher morbidity.** — Strong, low-quality, **1C**
   - Original **Ripstein** repair: recurrence **4%–10%** but excessive complications, primarily from foreign material on the anterior rectal wall (large-bowel obstruction, mesh erosion, ureteral injury or fibrosis, small-bowel obstruction, rectovaginal fistula, fecal impaction).
   - **McMahan–Ripstein modification** (posterior sacral fixation, ends attached laterally): recurrence **2%–5%**, morbidity **20%**, mostly minor.
   - **Mesh rectopexy significantly improves FI in 20%–60%.**
2. **A modified Wells procedure using a variety of foreign materials for posterior fixation may be used.** — Weak, moderate-quality, **2B**
   - The original **Ivalon (polyvinyl alcohol) sponge** was abandoned after a randomized trial showed more complications and more postoperative constipation with no recurrence benefit; it is no longer commercially available. Polyester, polypropylene and biologic mesh remain popular, **with no data showing superiority over sutures alone**.

### Ventral Rectopexy (1 recommendation)

1. **Ventral mesh rectopexy offers an alternative approach with acceptable short- and long-term complication rates.** — Strong, low-quality, **1C**
   - **The only technique using limited anterior mobilization only** — the rectum is separated from vagina (or prostate) to the perineal body with **no posterior dissection**, then mesh buttresses the anterior rectal wall and resuspends it to the sacral promontory. Contrast with **Orr-Loygue**, which mobilizes both anteriorly and posteriorly.
   - D'Hoore: **84% improvement in postoperative constipation and no de novo constipation**, attributed to sparing the rectal parasympathetic and sympathetic innervation.
   - Systematic review, 728 non-randomized patients: recurrence **3.4%**, weighted decrease in postoperative constipation **23.0%**. Series of 919 consecutive patients: **10-year recurrence 8.2%, mesh-related complications 4.6%**.
   - ⚠ **Preferred by many in Europe, but "has yet to gain full acceptance in the United States"** given limited long-term data and mesh-complication concerns. An international consensus panel suggested it for **pre-existing constipation and anterior-compartment abnormalities such as enterocele**.

### Additional Abdominal Considerations (1 recommendation)

1. **A minimally invasive approach by experienced surgeons gives improved morbidity and comparable recurrence versus open surgery, and should be considered when technically feasible.** — Strong, moderate-quality, **1B**
   - Laparoscopic vs open: equivalent recurrence **4%–8%** and morbidity **10%–33%**, with clear benefits in **pain control, length of stay and return of bowel function**.
   - **Robotic appears comparable to laparoscopic**; no well-powered RCT on long-term recurrence. Trade-offs: easier suturing and better visualization of the deep pouch of Douglas versus longer operating time and cost (possibly a learning-curve effect). One RCT and a meta-analysis of ventral rectopexy found no difference.

### Perineal Operations (2 recommendations)

1. **Patients with a short segment of full-thickness rectal prolapse can be treated with mucosal sleeve resection.** — Strong, low-quality, **1C**
   - **Delorme procedure — for a short (<5 cm) full-thickness prolapse**: circumferential mucosal sleeve resection with imbrication of the muscularis.
   - Recurrence **10%–15%** retrospectively (higher than abdominal), though one RCT found it comparable to perineal rectosigmoidectomy or abdominal repair. Early complications **4%–12%** — infection, urinary retention, bleeding, fecal impaction. Urgency and tenesmus occur. One manometry study found **resting and squeeze pressures rose significantly with improved continence**.
2. **Rectal prolapse may be treated with a perineal rectosigmoidectomy.** — Strong, low-quality, **1C**
   - **Altemeier procedure**: transanal full-thickness resection of the prolapsed rectum with coloanal anastomosis. **Can be performed without general anesthesia**, with shorter stay and lower complication rates than transabdominal surgery.
   - ⚠ **Recurrence 16%–30% at 2 years**, possibly affected by the length of intestine resected.
   - **Adding a levatorplasty reduced recurrence from 21% to 7%** in one study, presumably by treating concomitant levator diastasis.
   - Patients selected for it are older with significantly more comorbidity. The two RCTs comparing perineal with abdominal approaches found no significant recurrence difference, **but both were underpowered**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No contradiction with [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]], which is newer and governs where they overlap.** ACG 2021's conditional statement (full-thickness prolapse often requires abdominal rectopexy, or a perineal procedure in selected cases) and its preference for laparoscopic rectopexy are **compatible** with ASCRS 2017's transabdominal-fixation recommendation (2B) and its minimally-invasive recommendation (1B). ASCRS adds detail below the level ACG addresses; it does not overwrite anything.
- **Altemeier recurrence figures differ in precision, not direction.** ACG 2021 prints **27%**; ASCRS 2017 gives **16%–30% at 2 years**. ACG's single figure stays as the page's headline; the range is recorded for context.
- ⚠ **A geographic disagreement the document names itself:** ventral mesh rectopexy is considered by many in Europe to be the preferred method, but **"has yet to gain full acceptance in the United States."** Recorded as the document states it, since it affects what a US patient is likely to be offered.
- **Open — the document declines to rank posterior versus anterior repair** (1C, insufficient evidence). The wiki should not assert a winner either.
- **Open — no severity grading scale.** The page's standing note asked for one; ASCRS 2017 supplies **no staging or grading system**, only the **<5 cm** threshold that selects the Delorme procedure. The narrowed gap stays flagged.
