## Summary

The Rome V chapter that **prints the esophageal disorders of gut–brain interaction (E-DGBI) criteria**, in five categories: **A1 functional chest pain**, **A2 functional heartburn**, **A3 reflux hypersensitivity**, **A4 globus**, and **A5 functional dysphagia**. All five are defined by esophageal symptoms in the absence of structural, inflammatory, or major motor abnormality, with **visceral hypersensitivity and hypervigilance** as the shared mechanism.

**The headline change in Rome V is that the exclusion thresholds are no longer Rome's own** — they are imported wholesale from two external frameworks. Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is now ruled out by **Lyon Consensus 2.0** (Rome IV used Lyon 1.0), and esophageal motility disorders by **Chicago Classification version 4.0** (Rome IV used version 3.0). This makes the criteria boxes unusable without those two documents' numbers, which the chapter reproduces where they matter: acid exposure time (AET) <4% rules out GERD, >6% supports it, 4%–6% is inconclusive; mean nocturnal baseline impedance (MNBI) <1500 Ω supports GERD while >2500 Ω reasonably excludes it; **Los Angeles (LA) grade B is now accepted as conclusive GERD** alongside grades C and D.

Two further definitional shifts: **esophageal biopsies for functional chest pain are now required only if the chest pain is meal-related** (Rome IV required them in all chest-pain patients), reflecting eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE) as a diagnostic confounder rather than a universal one; and the **clinical impact of a gastric inlet patch in globus is downgraded** — Rome IV was more supportive of ablation therapy.

The chapter also sharpens the **functional heartburn vs reflux hypersensitivity** boundary, which is the chapter's main clinical decision: both have physiologic acid exposure and both fail proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy, and they are separated **only by reflux-symptom association** (symptom index ≥50% and/or symptom association probability ≥95%). Rome V additionally **excludes regurgitation and belching as qualifying symptoms for reflux hypersensitivity** — if those are the only symptoms associated with reflux events, the answer is rumination or supragastric belching instead.

### Shared framework

- **All five disorders:** criteria fulfilled for the **past 3 months**, symptom onset **≥6 months** before diagnosis, frequency **at least once a week**. For A1–A4 the symptoms must persist **despite optimized antisecretory therapy for at least 8 weeks**; **A5 functional dysphagia carries no antisecretory-therapy requirement.**
- **Ineffective esophageal motility (IEM) is not exclusionary for any of the five.** The exclusionary motor disorders are achalasia, conclusive esophagogastric junction (EGJ) outflow obstruction (EGJOO), absent contractility, hypercontractile esophagus, and distal esophageal spasm.
- **Inconclusive EGJOO may coexist with functional chest pain; conclusive EGJOO remains exclusionary.**
- **EoE must be excluded by histology (<15 eosinophils/high power field)** in the appropriate clinical context before any E-DGBI diagnosis is made.
- Mechanism: esophageal hypersensitivity from peripheral and/or central sensitization, amplified by **hypervigilance** (excessive attention to bodily sensations) and **symptom-specific anxiety**, both of which are themselves patient-reported outcomes and moderators of symptom severity (Figure 2 concept model).

### Figure 1 — global algorithm for evaluation and classification of E-DGBI

- **Dysphagia is the warning sign.** A patient with dysphagia goes straight to endoscopic visualization; chest pain, heartburn and globus may be given a **PPI trial first if there are no warning signs** on history and physical to suggest a cardiac issue or GI malignancy.
- **All patients with chest pain should have cardiac issues ruled out** before esophageal workup.
- **8-week PPI trial** → responder: evaluate and treat accordingly. Nonresponder → endoscopic visualization.
- **What to biopsy at endoscopy:** isolated chest pain — **add biopsy only if it occurs with eating**; dysphagia — esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) **with** biopsy; globus — laryngoscopy ± EGD; heartburn — EGD **without** biopsy unless Barrett's esophagus is suspected.
- Abnormality identified → evaluate and treat accordingly. No abnormality → **esophageal function testing**: reflux testing per Lyon 2.0 (wireless pH / 24-hour pH-impedance) and motility testing per Chicago Classification version 4.0 (high-resolution manometry [HRM] ± functional lumen imaging probe [FLIP] / timed barium esophagram).
- Still no abnormality → **esophageal disorder of gut–brain interaction**.

### Lyon 2.0 and Chicago v4.0 numbers the chapter reproduces

| Metric | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| AET | **<4%** on all days | Rules out GERD |
| AET | **4%–6%** | **Inconclusive** |
| AET | **>6%** | Supports / conclusive GERD |
| Reflux episodes | **<40** | Supports non-GERD |
| Reflux episodes | **>80** | Suggests GERD |
| MNBI | **<1500 Ω** | Supports GERD |
| MNBI | **>2500 Ω** | Reasonably excludes GERD; supports functional heartburn |
| Endoscopy | **LA grade B, C or D**, biopsy-proven Barrett's, peptic stricture | Conclusive GERD (LA grade B newly conclusive in Lyon 2.0) |
| Symptom index | **≥50%** | Positive reflux-symptom association |
| Symptom association probability | **≥95%** | Positive reflux-symptom association |

- **Off vs on therapy:** wireless pH monitoring off PPI is preferred for assessing day-to-day AET variability and for ruling out nonerosive reflux disease (NERD). Testing **off acid suppression** is advised when clinical suspicion for GERD remains high after PPI nonresponse. Stop PPIs **2 to 4 weeks** before testing to detect baseline GERD.
- **Refractory heartburn on double-dose PPI** is defined by **AET >4% and >80 reflux episodes**; **functional heartburn on double-dose PPI** by **AET <1%, <40 reflux episodes and MNBI >2500 Ω**.
- **A poor PPI response carries high negative predictive value for GERD** and supports an E-DGBI diagnosis.

### A1. Diagnostic criteria for functional chest pain

> Must include **all** of the following:
> 1. **Retrosternal chest pain or discomfort** (cardiac causes should be ruled out)
> 2. **Absence of associated esophageal symptoms** such as heartburn or dysphagia
> 3. Absence of evidence that **GERD** (abnormal endoscopy ≥LA grade B esophagitis, or reflux burden by Lyon 2.0) **or eosinophilic esophagitis** is the cause of the symptoms
> 4. **Absence of disorders of EGJ outflow and/or disorders of esophageal peristalsis** (achalasia, conclusive EGJOO, absent contractility, hypercontractile esophagus, distal esophageal spasm) — **except IEM**, per Chicago Classification version 4.0
>
> Criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months, onset ≥6 months before diagnosis, frequency at least once weekly, despite optimized antisecretory therapy for at least 8 weeks.
>
> *Consider mucosal biopsies if chest pain occurs with meals, as this could be a surrogate for dysphagia.*

- **Prevalence 1.4%** (95% confidence interval 1.3%–1.5%) in a global survey of 54,127 individuals; slightly more common in women (1.5% vs 1.3%) and in those **younger than 65** (1.4%–1.5% vs 1.0%).
- Among patients with noncardiac chest pain (NCCP): **~50%–60% have GERD, 15%–18% have an esophageal motility disorder, and 32%–35% meet criteria for functional chest pain.**
- **Cardiac exclusion comes first** — esophageal workup should only proceed after a cardiologist or primary care physician confirms the symptoms are unrelated to concurrent or new coronary artery disease.
- **8-week course of optimized PPI or potassium-competitive acid blocker (PCAB)** is simple and cost-effective given the high GERD prevalence in NCCP. Nonresponders with persisting GERD suspicion → ambulatory reflux testing **off acid suppression**.
- **No single reflux-monitoring method is superior** — pH monitoring, combined pH-impedance, or prolonged wireless pH beyond 24 hours can all be used, depending on availability and interpreting expertise.
- Most NCCP patients have **normal motor function**; HRM should be considered **once GERD has been comfortably ruled out**.
- **Functional chest pain is a diagnosis of exclusion** — there is no definitive clinical test to confirm esophageal origin.
- **Psychiatric comorbidity in up to 75%** of NCCP patients (anxiety, depression, somatization); chest pain is a common feature of panic attacks.
- **Abelchia** (retrograde cricopharyngeal dysfunction / inability to belch) is an under-recognized motility disorder that can cause chest pain and audible gurgling; HRM may show absent upper esophageal sphincter (UES) relaxation during reflux events, and pH-impedance shows continuously high impedance from esophageal air entrapment.
- **Treatment:** neuromodulation of pain — TCAs, serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), trazodone — with randomized trials showing **up to 50% effectiveness above placebo**, whereas **selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) show inconsistent results**. Side effects or stigma may limit use. Alternatives explored: gabapentin, pregabalin, dronabinol, theophylline (cautious use). Behavioral: **GI-directed cognitive behavioral therapy (GI-CBT)** — a Cochrane review found reduced symptom frequency (relative risk 0.70; 95% confidence interval 0.53–0.92) — plus gut-directed hypnotherapy and biofeedback.

### A2. Diagnostic criteria for functional heartburn

> Must include **all** of the following:
> 1. **Burning retrosternal discomfort**
> 2. **No symptom relief despite optimized antisecretory therapy for at least 8 weeks**
> 3. Absence of evidence that **GERD** (abnormal endoscopy ≥LA grade B esophagitis, or reflux burden by Lyon 2.0; **normal MNBI is supportive of functional heartburn**) **or nonreflux esophagitis** is the cause of symptoms
> 4. **Absence of disorders of EGJ obstruction and/or disorders of esophageal peristalsis** — except IEM, per Chicago Classification version 4.0
>
> Criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months, onset ≥6 months before diagnosis, frequency at least once a week, despite ≥8 weeks of optimized antisecretory therapy.

- **Rome III placed functional heartburn under the NERD spectrum; Rome IV excluded patients with abnormal acid exposure or positive reflux-symptom association. Rome V maintains that approach** but aligns the thresholds to Lyon 2.0 and Chicago v4.0, **clearly distinguishing functional heartburn from reflux hypersensitivity**.
- **Only 20%–30% of patients achieve relief with high-dose PPIs**; PCABs may offer better outcomes. **PPI nonresponse is central to the diagnosis.**
- Global prevalence **1.1%** (internet-based) and **0.4%** (household survey). Among patients referred to GI visits, **functional heartburn affects 20%–30% of those with heartburn**; up to **70% of heartburn patients have normal endoscopy** and need reflux monitoring to be phenotyped.
- Supporting data: of 366 patients with refractory reflux symptoms, **99 (27%) had normal acid exposure and no symptom correlation**, meeting functional heartburn criteria; in a 96-hour wireless pH study, **18% (24 of 132) had normal acid exposure across all days**. So **18%–30% of PPI-refractory reflux-like symptoms fall within the E-DGBI spectrum**.
- **Overlap with GERD is real:** in patients with proven GERD, only **28%–40% had proven ongoing GERD** and the remainder had GERD/E-DGBI overlap. Overlap patients resemble conventional E-DGBI clinically, psychologically and functionally.
- Patients often **mislabel throat symptoms or epigastric burning as heartburn** — careful history improves accuracy. Functional heartburn and GERD patients are otherwise clinically similar.
- **Routine esophageal biopsies for EoE are not recommended** in the absence of dysphagia, chest pain with eating, or suggestive endoscopic features.
- **Long-term outcome:** at 2 years, **two-thirds remained symptomatic** while about **20% had reduced symptom intensity and frequency** — generally persistent, with modest improvement in a subset.
- **Treatment:** empirical and tailored. Provide reassurance, **discourage repeated invasive testing**, watch for excessive or disproportionate food avoidance. **Antireflux surgery should be avoided.** Neuromodulators (low-dose TCAs, SSRIs) are appropriate. **A sham-controlled trial of esophageal endoscopic radiofrequency energy delivery showed no benefit** in refractory heartburn including functional heartburn (small, 24 weeks). Psychogastroenterological care — GI-CBT, hypnotherapy, diaphragmatic breathing, relaxation therapy — is low-risk and aids **PPI withdrawal**.

### A3. Diagnostic criteria for reflux hypersensitivity

> Must include **all** of the following:
> 1. **Retrosternal burning and/or chest pain**
> 2. **Regurgitation and belching are not considered in this definition** if they occur by themselves or together
> 3. **Evidence of triggering of symptoms by reflux events**
> 4. Absence of evidence that **GERD** (abnormal acid exposure by technology and approach, and/or LA grade B or higher esophagitis) **or nonreflux esophagitis** is the cause of the symptoms
> 5. **Absence of disorders of EGJ obstruction and/or disorders of esophageal peristalsis** — except IEM, per Chicago Classification version 4.0
>
> Criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months, onset ≥6 months before diagnosis, frequency at least once a week, despite ≥8 weeks of optimized antisecretory therapy.

- **The key distinction from functional heartburn is a positive symptom association** with reflux events: **symptom index ≥50% and/or symptom association probability ≥95%**.
- Physiologic reflux is confirmed when **AET <4% on all days of a prolonged wireless pH study, or AET <4% with <40 reflux episodes and MNBI >2500 Ω** on pH-impedance monitoring. Testing may be on or off PPI therapy.
- **If belching and/or regurgitation are the only symptoms associated with reflux events, consider rumination or supragastric belching instead.**
- Rome V **clearly excludes regurgitation, belching, rumination and abelchia** as qualifying symptoms or diagnoses.
- Prevalence **0.8%** (internet-based) and **0.6%** (household). Among patients without esophagitis, **37%–60% have normal pH monitoring off therapy**; reported prevalence in refractory populations ranges **10%–42%** (one study: 36% reflux hypersensitivity, 40% NERD, 24% functional heartburn; a larger 573-patient study: 42%; the Spechler trial: only 10%).
- In GERD/E-DGBI overlap, reflux hypersensitivity is **less common than functional heartburn**, affecting **25%–37%** of patients.
- Impaired mucosal integrity may contribute — **MNBI is lower in reflux hypersensitivity than in functional heartburn**, though MNBI thresholds are not yet diagnostic. Upregulation of acid-sensitive receptors (transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 1) and neurogenic inflammation markers (substance P, neurokinin-1 receptor) support a sensitization model.
- **Treatment** parallels functional heartburn — reassurance and education that no serious disease is present. **Response to antisecretory therapy may be better than in the other E-DGBIs**; patients with an acid-sensitive esophagus may improve on standard or high-dose PPIs or PCABs, but those with weakly acid or nonacid reflux triggers often remain refractory.
  - **Limited data suggest antireflux surgery may help select patients, particularly with concurrent hiatal hernia.** A sham-controlled trial of endoscopic radiofrequency showed no benefit; the Spechler randomized trial found antireflux surgery superior to medical therapy (PPI, baclofen, desipramine) in refractory heartburn including reflux hypersensitivity, although groups did not differ by acid burden.
  - **Pain modulators — TCAs, SSRIs, SNRIs, gabapentinoids — remain the cornerstone.** A placebo-controlled trial of **citalopram** reduced symptoms (38.5% symptomatic vs 66.7% on placebo; *P* = .021).
  - Psychological therapies (gut-directed CBT, hypnosis, biofeedback) should be integrated when appropriate.

### A4. Diagnostic criteria for globus

> Must include **all** of the following:
> 1. **Persistent or intermittent, nonpainful, sensation of a lump or foreign body in the throat** with no structural lesion identified on physical examination and laryngoscopy
>    - a. **Occurrence of the sensation between meals**
>    - b. **Absence of dysphagia or odynophagia**
>    - c. **Absence of a gastric inlet patch in the proximal esophagus** (DGBI requires lack of a mucosal abnormality; a gastric inlet patch precludes a DGBI diagnosis)
> 2. Absence of evidence that **GERD** (abnormal endoscopy ≥LA grade B esophagitis, or reflux burden by Lyon 2.0) **or nonreflux esophagitis** is the cause of the symptom
> 3. **Absence of disorders of EGJ obstruction and/or disorders of esophageal peristalsis** — except IEM, per Chicago Classification version 4.0
>
> Criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months, onset ≥6 months before diagnosis, frequency at least once a week following optimized antisecretory therapy.

- Globus is **common and benign but chronic**: up to **46%** of otherwise healthy individuals report the sensation at some point, typically peaking in middle age, equally in both sexes, though **women seek care more frequently**. Lifetime prevalence **21.5%**, peak onset ages **35–54**. Rome IV population study across the US, UK and Canada: **8.1%**. Rome Foundation global data: **7.5%** more than twice weekly.
- **Persists in 75% of patients at 3 years and 50% at 7 years.** Accounts for about **4% of new ear, nose and throat referrals**.
- Prevalence of feeling a lump between meals **7.0%**; among those, **9.9% also reported dysphagia**. Overall globus prevalence when strictly defined: **0.8%**.
- **Workup:** eliciting a compatible history and ruling out structural lesion, GERD, or a major motor disorder. **There must be no dysphagia and no alarm features (odynophagia, weight loss).** Physical examination of the neck followed by **laryngoscopy** is the advised initial evaluation.
- Once localized structural or inflammatory causes are excluded → **empiric trial of antisecretory therapy (PPI or PCAB) for 4 to 8 weeks**. Responder → manage as GERD. Nonresponder → **endoscopy to assess for a gastric inlet patch** or other mucosal process. **HRM may help rule out a major motor disorder**, but there is limited data supporting a distinct motor pattern in globus.
- **Treatment:** rule out structural lesions, assess for GERD (**discontinue PPI if ineffective** — response rates are modest and PCABs have not been studied here). **Data do not support antireflux surgery for globus alone.** Gastric inlet patch ablation (argon plasma coagulation or radiofrequency) has shown symptomatic benefit in small, low-quality studies — **not definitive**.
  - Once GERD and structural/motor abnormalities are excluded, the primary treatment centers on **explanation, reassurance and psychobehavioral approaches** — CBT-based psychoeducation, **speech therapy with pharyngolaryngeal relaxation exercises**, and hypnosis have shown symptom improvement in small studies.
  - Neuromodulators (**amitriptyline, paroxetine, flupentixol/melitracen combinations**) demonstrated **greater efficacy than PPIs** in randomized trials. Given the benign course, **behavioral interventions should be prioritized over medications or invasive procedures**.

### A5. Diagnostic criteria for functional dysphagia

> Must include **all** of the following:
> 1. **Sense of solid and/or liquid foods sticking to, lodging in, or passing abnormally through the esophagus**
> 2. **Absence of evidence that esophageal mucosal or structural abnormality is the cause of the symptom** (a normal esophagram with a tablet/marshmallow, or a normal FLIP study, may be supportive)
> 3. Absence of evidence that **GERD** (abnormal endoscopy ≥LA grade B esophagitis, or reflux burden by Lyon 2.0) **or nonreflux esophagitis** is the cause of the symptoms
> 4. **Absence of disorders of EGJ obstruction and/or disorders of esophageal peristalsis** — except IEM, per Chicago Classification version 4.0 (a **normal solid test meal during HRM may be supportive** of functional dysphagia)
>
> Criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months, onset ≥6 months before diagnosis, frequency at least once a week. **No antisecretory-therapy requirement.**

- **The least common E-DGBI.** Early surveys estimated 7%–8% of dysphagia was unexplained; a global Rome IV survey of ~55,000 individuals reported **3.2% prevalence** (3.5% female, 2.9% male), but many of those cases likely included oropharyngeal or structural causes. In a large tertiary referral series, **only 2.4% of 694 patients had persistent dysphagia despite negative testing** (FLIP was not used in that cohort), so the true prevalence is likely even lower.
- **Opioid use has emerged as a significant factor** contributing to esophageal dysmotility, outflow obstruction and heightened symptom reporting — **routinely query opioid use.**
- **GERD and EoE are key exclusions**, ruled out by antisecretory therapy plus **upper endoscopy with biopsy**. Biopsies may miss EoE if taken on PPI therapy or in fibro-stenotic phenotypes. Other mucosal diseases (lichen planus, pemphigus, pemphigoid, lymphocytic esophagitis) can occur without visible endoscopic findings — **mucosal biopsies are essential**.
- **Barium studies with solid boluses (tablet, cookie, marshmallow)** detect subtle strictures, obstructive lesions and paraesophageal hernias missed on endoscopy. **Videofluoroscopy is the test of choice when oropharyngeal dysphagia is suspected.**
- **HRM with provocative maneuvers** — multiple rapid swallows, test meals, rapid drink challenge — enhances detection of subtle motor dysfunction.
- **FLIP panometry now plays a key complementary role.** FLIP detects abnormal EGJ opening in patients with otherwise normal testing: in one study **17 of 34 symptomatic patients with normal HRM had abnormal FLIP responses**, and another found **up to 32% of 164 patients with normal HRM had abnormal FLIP patterns**. In 164 symptomatic patients with normal HRM, FLIP identified previously undetected EGJ obstruction or abnormal contractility in **nearly one-third**. **FLIP is not mandatory** for diagnosis, as it is not universally available. The **Dallas Classification** recommends that with negative endoscopy and normal FLIP metrics, functional dysphagia is likely and can be diagnosed earlier in the workup.
- As FLIP is integrated earlier, **the prevalence of truly idiopathic functional dysphagia is anticipated to continue to decline**.
- **Treatment:** often a **benign, self-limited course**. Reassurance and simple strategies — **upright eating, thorough chewing, liquid chasers** — suffice in mild cases. A short course of acid suppression may be helpful, particularly if reflux is suspected. TCAs and other neuromodulators may relieve symptoms in selected patients, though not definitively proven.
  - **Empiric bougie dilation to 50 to 54 Fr has shown benefit in 68% to 85% of patients with unexplained food dysphagia**, likely addressing subtle proximal esophageal abnormalities missed on routine testing — **unlike balloon dilation at the EGJ, which has shown limited utility**. With FLIP panometry, dilation can now be targeted more precisely to areas of subtle obstruction or wall stiffness.
  - In patients with maladaptive beliefs, symptom-specific anxiety or other psychological comorbidity, **GI-CBT techniques (psychoeducation, diaphragmatic breathing, fear-exposure) may be effective**, and gut-directed hypnotherapy may reduce visceral hypersensitivity and hypervigilance.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **LA grade B is now conclusive for GERD.** Lyon 1.0 and older guidance treated grade B as inconclusive requiring reflux monitoring. Pages that still describe grade B as equivocal should be read against this.
- **Esophageal biopsies in chest pain:** Rome IV required them for all chest-pain patients; **Rome V requires them only when chest pain is meal related.** Readers carrying the Rome IV rule will over-biopsy.
- **Gastric inlet patch in globus:** Rome IV was more supportive of ablation; **Rome V downgrades it** and makes the presence of an inlet patch *exclusionary* for the globus DGBI diagnosis — the recommendation to ablate rests on low-quality evidence.
- **Antireflux surgery splits by diagnosis:** the chapter says to **avoid** it in functional heartburn, says there is **no support** for it in globus alone, but allows that it **may help select reflux-hypersensitivity patients, particularly with concurrent hiatal hernia**. The same Spechler trial is cited for the positive signal, and its groups did not differ by acid burden — so the distinction rests on weak evidence.
- **IEM is not exclusionary in any of the five disorders**, which differs from readings that treat any manometric abnormality as disqualifying for a functional diagnosis.
