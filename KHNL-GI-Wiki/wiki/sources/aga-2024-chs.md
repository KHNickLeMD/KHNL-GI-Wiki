## Summary

Expert commentary on cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS), a chronic disorder of gut–brain interaction included in the Rome IV classification since 2016 and framed here as a **subtype of cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS)**. CHS is associated with chronic (typically years) and heavy (typically daily or near-daily) cannabis use and is seen predominantly in males. Prevalence is rising in the United States while awareness among clinicians and the public stays low; early recognition may reduce complications.

Diagnosis is clinical — there is no confirmatory test. It rests on three proposed elements: stereotyped episodic vomiting resembling CVS, a defined cannabis-use pattern, and resolution after a defined period of abstinence. Compulsive hot-water bathing supports the diagnosis but is not pathognomonic. Testing is individualized and aimed only at excluding mimics; the emergency department (ED) approach differs entirely, since life-threatening causes must be excluded immediately.

Management pairs acute antiemetic control — topical capsaicin, benzodiazepines, haloperidol, promethazine, olanzapine, ondansetron, with opioids avoided — against long-term cannabis cessation plus a tricyclic antidepressant. Evidence throughout is limited to case series and small clinical trials; recidivism is high and several widely suggested harm-reduction strategies are explicitly unvalidated.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Document format

- **Commentary-format CPU — narrative expert advice.** The document contains **no Best Practice Advice statements, no numbered recommendations, and no evidence grades or strength-of-recommendation ratings.** Headings and bullet grouping below are the wiki's; the only numbering the document itself gives is the 1–3 of the proposed CHS diagnostic criteria.
- Commissioned and approved by the AGA Institute Clinical Practice Updates Committee (CPUC) and the AGA Governing Board; internal CPUC peer review plus external peer review.

### Epidemiology and use pattern

- Prevalence rising; CHS is an increasingly frequent reason for ED and gastroenterology clinic visits, yet remains an unfamiliar entity among physicians worldwide.
- ED treatment prevalence for CHS **doubled between 2017 and 2021** in North America; highest in the **16–34 year** age group.
- Prospective ED cohort (39 patients): cannabis use **daily or multiple times per day** (median 3×/day), **72% began using before age 16**, nearly all had high cannabis withdrawal scores.
- Systematic review of **271 CHS cases:** mean age **30 years**, **69% male**, mean **6.6 years** of cannabis use before symptom onset, **daily use in 68%**, hot-water bathing in **71%**.
- CVS accounts for **10.8%** of patients presenting to outpatient gastroenterology clinics with intermittent nausea and vomiting; CVS is associated with younger age, tobacco use, psychiatric comorbidity, and symptoms of other disorders of gut–brain interaction.
- Hot-water bathing to relieve emesis is reported by **44% of subjects with CVS vs 19% with chronic nausea and vomiting syndrome** (*P* = .03) — the behaviour is **independent of cannabis but augmented by it**, so it is **not pathognomonic** of CHS.

### Pathophysiology — the endocannabinoid system

- Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) is the psychoactive constituent; cannabidiol (CBD) is not psychoactive.
- THC activates **CB1** (brain; gut myenteric/submucosal neurons and epithelium) and **CB2** (mainly immunocytes/macrophages and epithelium) receptors, and also binds **transient receptor potential vanilloid type 1 (TRPV1)**.
- The emetic effects are mediated predominantly by **central CB1 receptors**, densely distributed in the **dorsal vagal complex**. Loss of endocannabinoid negative feedback on the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis increases vagal discharge.
- Peripheral CB1 activation also inhibits gastric acid secretion, gastric motility/emptying, and more distal gut motility.

### CVS — Rome IV criteria in adults (Table 1)

- Stereotypical episodes of vomiting regarding **onset (acute)** and **duration (<1 week)**.
- **≥3 discrete episodes in the previous year AND 2 episodes in the past 6 months**, occurring **≥1 week apart**.
- **Absence of nausea and vomiting between episodes** — other, milder symptoms can be present between episodes.
- *Supportive remark:* personal or family history of **migraine headaches**.
- Footnote: criteria fulfilled for the **last 3 months**, with symptom onset **≥6 months before diagnosis**.

### CHS — proposed diagnostic criteria (Venkatesan et al.)

1. **Clinical features:** stereotypical episodic vomiting resembling CVS in terms of onset, with frequency **≥3 times annually**.
2. **Cannabis use pattern:** duration of use **>1 year before symptom onset**; frequency **>4 times per week on average**.
3. **Cannabis cessation:** resolution of symptoms after abstinence for **≥6 months**, or **≥ the total duration of 3 typical vomiting cycles in that patient**.

### Cannabis and the vomiting syndromes

- Cannabis is associated with **CVS, CHS, cannabinoid withdrawal syndrome (CWS), and nausea and vomiting in pregnancy**.
- **CWS:** common on cessation of heavy, prolonged use. Systematic review/meta-analysis of 47 articles (23,518 cannabis users; 69% men, median age 29 y) — **pooled prevalence 47%**. Higher with **daily cannabis use** and with concurrent cannabis, tobacco, or other substance use disorders.
- **Pregnancy:** cannabis is used to relieve nausea and vomiting of pregnancy, especially in more severe cases, but there is also evidence it **aggravates** it. Given adverse perinatal outcomes — especially with heavy use — **women are advised to refrain from cannabis during pregnancy and lactation**.

### Evaluation

- **No confirmatory test.** Limited, individualized outpatient evaluation to exclude mimics.
- **No consensus exists on minimum testing.** A good clinical history narrows the possibilities; suspicion-driven testing may include:
  - Anatomic evaluation — **upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy, or computed tomography (CT) of abdomen or pelvis** to exclude obstruction.
  - **Brain imaging** if focal neurologic symptoms and signs.
- **ED approach is entirely different** — immediate evaluation for life-threatening disorders: acute abdomen, bowel obstruction, mesenteric ischemia, pancreatitis, myocardial infarction, among others.

### Differential diagnosis (outpatient, after structural disease excluded)

Rumination syndrome · gastroparesis · CVS · pregnancy · migraine · functional chronic nausea and vomiting syndrome.

### Phases of CVS (Figure 1, adapted from Frazier et al.)

| Phase | Manifestations | Management options |
|---|---|---|
| **Inter-episodic** | Absent or minimal symptoms; **30%** may have dyspepsia or nausea | Prophylactic medications in **moderate to severe** CVS: tricyclics, anti-convulsants, mitochondrial supplements (coenzyme Q10, vitamin B2), neurokinin-1 (NK1) antagonist |
| **Prodromal** | Nausea and abdominal pain; **median 30–90 minute** duration; autonomic symptoms (sweating, feeling hot or cold); panic and "out of control" | Take abortive medications — triptans; antiemetics: NK1 or histamine H1-antagonists, benzodiazepines |
| **Emetic** | Relentless vomiting, retching, abdominal pain, neurological symptoms; extreme thirst with large water intake; relief with empty stomach, induce emesis | Rest in a quiet dark room; supportive treatment |
| **Recovery** | Vomiting subsides; triggers noted — stressful events (positive or negative), sleep deprivation, infections | Start oral intake and advance as tolerated; resume regular activities |

### Management

**Acute / short-term** — evidence limited to case series and small clinical trials:
- **Topical capsaicin 0.1% cream** applied to the **upper abdomen**; may reduce the need for antiemetics. Proposed mechanism: TRPV1 activation.
- Benzodiazepines, **haloperidol**, promethazine, olanzapine, ondansetron.
- **Avoid opioids** — worsen nausea, high risk of addiction.

**Long-term** — cannabis cessation plus a tricyclic antidepressant are the mainstay:
- **Counseling to achieve cannabis cessation**, considered necessary for successful treatment.
- Advising immediate ("cold turkey") cessation may cause **significant withdrawal symptoms and a high frequency of recidivism**; cannabis-specific withdrawal resources are limited.
- **Amitriptyline (tricyclic antidepressant):** start **25 mg**, titrate with increments **each week** to the **minimal effective dose of 75–100 mg at bedtime**, with close monitoring of efficacy and adverse effects. Efficacious in **~70%** of patients with minor adverse events.
- Combining evidence-based psychosocial interventions with pharmacology may be necessary. **Co-management with a psychologist or psychiatrist** for patients lacking response to standard therapy or with extensive psychiatric comorbidity; **anxiety and depression are very common** associated conditions.

**Explicitly lacking scientific validation** (frequently suggested anyway):
- Switching to a **lower Δ⁹-THC / higher CBD** formulation.
- Use of **edible** forms.
- Avoiding **Δ⁹-THC concentrates**.

**Investigational:** fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) inhibitors and CBD each reduced cannabis use in 2 small placebo-controlled randomized studies in adults with cannabinoid use disorder — larger studies needed.

**Course:** a single-center experience reported **>40% of patients could stop all CHS treatment over time**, but recidivism was high. In a survey, many patients with recurrent vomiting and a CHS diagnosis **remained uncertain about the role of cannabis**, attributing symptoms to food and alcohol patterns, stress, and existing GI disorders.

**Complications of CHS:** dehydration, acute kidney injury, electrolyte and metabolic imbalance; much rarer pneumothorax or pneumomediastinum; death.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Commentary format — no evidence ratings of any kind.** Treatment evidence is case series and small clinical trials; no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) support the long-term regimen.
- A **critical appraisal of the literature has questioned the etiologic role of cannabis** in CHS, even though cessation is considered necessary for treatment.
- Overall CHS prevalence remains unknown and underdiagnosis is a challenge; recidivism is high.
- **Superseded in part by [[rome-v-2026-dgbi]] (2026):** this CPU treats CHS as a subtype of CVS, whereas Rome V makes CHS a separate adult entity (B2c). The newer consensus governs placement; see [[cannabinoid-hyperemesis-syndrome]].
- Where this CPU and [[aga-2024-cvs]] (same year, CVS-specific) overlap, the CVS-specific CPU carries the more detailed dosing and severity framework.
