## Summary

An AGA expert review commissioned and approved by the AGA Institute Clinical Practice Updates Committee and the AGA Governing Board, presenting **ten Best Practice Advice (BPA) statements** on palliative care (PC) in cirrhosis. The review's central argument is that PC is **not** synonymous with hospice care, **not** restricted to decompensated disease, and **not** mutually exclusive with transplant listing — it is needs-based care delivered **concurrently** with curative and life-prolonging therapy across the whole spectrum of cirrhosis.

The update frames PC around a **primary palliative care toolkit** that any non-PC specialist can deliver: routine symptom and distress screening, functional/prognostic assessment, advance care planning (ACP) and goals-of-care discussions (GCD) at defined sentinel events, caregiver-burden screening, and pre-agreed local triggers and pathways for referral to specialty PC and hospice.

Three misconceptions are named and rebutted: (1) PC equals hospice/end-of-life care; (2) PC cannot be delivered alongside curative treatment such as liver transplantation; (3) PC applies only to decompensated patients — distress and depressive symptoms are identifiable in **Child-Pugh class A** patients, many of whom have quality-of-life and symptom scores well below population norms.

The review also addresses the delivery problem directly: specialty PC is a limited workforce resource, lack of clinician time is a major barrier, and the practical answer is generalist ("primary") PC by hepatology/gastroenterology and primary care, supported by ancillary-staff prescreening, billing codes for chronic care management/ACP/transitional care management, and multidisciplinary teams.

## Best Practice Advice Statements (verbatim)

Reproduced exactly as printed in Table 1 and in the body of the article. The article numbers them **BPA 1–10**.

**BPA 1.** Care with palliative care principles should be provided to any patient with advanced serious chronic illness or life-limiting illness such as cirrhosis, irrespective of transplant candidacy; this care should be based on needs assessment instead of prognosis alone, delivered concurrently with curative or life-prolonging treatments, and tailored to stage of disease.

**BPA 2.** Care inclusive of palliative care principles may be delivered by healthcare providers from any specialty within any healthcare setting.

**BPA 3.** Providers caring for persons with cirrhosis should assess for the presence and severity of symptoms within physical, psychological, social, and spiritual domains related to their liver disease, its treatment, and prognosis.

**BPA 4.** Across the spectrum of cirrhosis, excellence in communication is integral to high-quality advance care planning, goals of care conversations, and the cultivation of prognostic awareness with patients and caregivers.

**BPA 5.** Routine care for patients with cirrhosis, and particularly those with decompensated disease, should include assessment of caregiver support and screening for caregiver needs.

**BPA 6.** Prognosis should be evaluated by gastroenterology/hepatology providers during routine care visits and at sentinel events.

**BPA 7.** Goals of care discussions in patients with cirrhosis should be repeated at sentinel events including hospital or intensive care admission, before initiation of life-supporting therapies, before surgery, on new onset of cirrhosis-related complications, and after determination of transplant eligibility.

**BPA 8.** Because lack of time is one of the major barriers to administering palliative care, healthcare providers should consider how they can optimize efficiencies in palliative care delivery (identifying local billing codes, prescreening surveys carried out by ancillary staff, development of multidisciplinary teams).

**BPA 9.** Dedicated specialist palliative care services are often a limited resource. As such, healthcare providers should work together with local specialist palliative care teams to establish clear triggers and pathways for referral.

**BPA 10.** Healthcare providers caring for patients with cirrhosis should provide timely referral to hospice for patients who have comfort-oriented goals and prognosis of 6 months or less.

### Evidence grading

**This document attaches no evidence grades to its Best Practice Advice statements.** There is no GRADE rating, no strength-of-recommendation label, and no quality-of-evidence label on any of the ten statements — the only footnote to Table 1 is the abbreviation expansion "BPA, best practice advice." The statements are numbered 1–10 exactly as shown above and carry no other label.

## Terminology (Table 1)

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| **Palliative care principles** | The early identification and management of physical, psychological, social, and spiritual suffering; excellence in communication for advance care planning, goals of care discussions, and prognostication; screening for caregiver fatigue; and providing needed caregiver support. The incorporation of these principles is meant to optimize quality of life for patients and their families and caregivers. |
| **Palliative care** | The provision of care inclusive of palliative care principles that may be delivered by healthcare providers from any specialty and within any healthcare setting. |
| **Specialty palliative care** | Delivery of care by a palliative care specialist with dedicated training and/or board certification in hospice and palliative medicine. |
| **Primary palliative care** | Delivery of care by a clinician who is not a palliative care specialist. |
| **Hospice care** (from the body text) | Falls within the larger umbrella term of PC. Hospice care focuses on end-of-life care for patients who have an estimated survival of **<6 months**. |

## The Comprehensive Primary Palliative Care Toolkit (Figure 1)

Figure 1 places the patient and caregivers at the centre of a circle of care — hepatology/gastroenterology, specialty palliative care, primary care practitioner, allied health providers, advance practice providers, and ICU/emergency room providers — bounded by four requirements: **communication, documentation, collaboration, and role clarity** (who does what).

The toolkit itself, as printed, with the BPA each element serves:

| Toolkit element | BPA | Examples given in the figure |
|---|---|---|
| Enhance ACP and communication skills | 4, 7 | Online provider- and patient-facing tools (Supplementary Table 1) |
| Establish care pathways with specialty palliative care | 9 | — |
| **Symptoms** | 3 | ESAS (including pruritus), PHQ-2, Distress Thermometer, a quality-of-life scale (Table 3) |
| **Prognosis, functional status** | 6 | Ask: *"Compared to last year, have you noticed any changes in your ability to carry out your day-to-day tasks? Is this change happening over years? months? days?"* Measure: MELD-Na, Karnofsky Index, Liver Frailty Index |
| **ACP readiness → ACP/GCD** | 6, 7 | Ask: *"I want to share with you my understanding of where things are at with your illness… Is this okay with you?"* |
| **Caregiver needs** | 5 | Ask: *"I know this must be hard on you. How are you doing?"* Measure: Caregiver Strain Index, Zarit Burden Interview |
| Access specialty services when needed | 9, 10 | Refer to specialty palliative care; refer to hospice |

Additional conversation openers given in the body text (Serious Illness Conversation Guide and a review on ACP in cirrhosis):

- *"To make sure that both you and your family are prepared, I like to address both the best- and worst-case scenarios regarding how your cirrhosis may progress. Can we talk about these now?"*
- Where prognostic ambivalence and worsening disease make the conversation time-sensitive, **"what if" scenarios** can be useful to elicit preferred healthcare decisions.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Symptom burden

- Median survival in cirrhosis ranges from **2 years in decompensated disease to 12 years in compensated disease**.
- Quality of life in patients with worsening disease severity **parallels that of patients with advanced cancer**.
- Symptom prevalence in adult end-stage liver disease, from a systematic review (ranges across studies):

| Symptom | Prevalence |
|---|---|
| Sexual dysfunction | 53%–93% |
| Breathlessness | 20%–88% |
| Insomnia | 26%–77% |
| Pain | 30%–77% |
| Daytime sleepiness | 30%–71% |
| Muscle cramps | 56%–68% |
| Pruritus | 47%–64% |
| Depression | 10%–64% |
| Fatigue | 52%–86% |
| Anxiety | 14%–45% |

- A separate integrative review of 26 quantitative studies found the most commonly reported symptoms across chronic liver disease populations to be **fatigue, depression, sleep disturbance, pain, cognitive impairment, and dizziness**.
- **Most physical symptoms in cirrhosis are best addressed via optimal management of cirrhosis-related complications** (e.g. treatment of fluid overload), although some (e.g. pruritus) may require additional specific therapies. *This update gives no drug names, doses, or dosing intervals for symptom management.*
- In the SUPPORT study, patients with at least 2 features of advanced disease (serum albumin ≤3.0 mg/dL **as printed**, uncontrolled ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, cachexia, upper gastrointestinal bleed) were surveyed: **43% would rather die than receive care in a nursing home**, and most reported wanting to die rather than live in a coma or with a ventilator or feeding tube.

### Prognosis and prognostication

- **Sentinel events at which prognosis should be re-evaluated (BPA 6)**: hospitalization, intensive care unit admission, development of liver-related complications including hepatocellular carcinoma, after determination of transplant eligibility, and before initiation of life-supporting therapies or surgery.
- Population-based prognostic scores — **MELD, MELD sodium, the Child-Pugh classification**, and acute-on-chronic liver failure scores such as **CLIF-C ACLF** — have recognized limitations when applied to individual patients. A more complete understanding of prognosis is gained by adding **comorbidities, functional status/frailty, the rapidity of functional decline, and the potential for improvement with etiological therapy**.
- Clinicians remain **overly optimistic** about individual patients' prognoses; in a large multicenter end-of-life study both physician-estimated and modeled prognoses **failed to predict mortality in cirrhosis**.
- Quality of life independently predicts outcome (see Supplementary Table 2 below).
- A 9-member multidisciplinary expert panel using the RAND/UCLA modified Delphi method identified **13 quality indicators** for information and care planning in "end stage liver disease." **Sentinel events for initiating goals of care discussions** per those indicators: diagnosis of decompensated cirrhosis, consideration of liver transplant, admission to an intensive care unit, admission to a hospital, use of a mechanical ventilator, and initiation of hemodialysis.

### Caregiver burden

- **Caregiver burden** is defined by: perception of physical symptoms, psychological distress, impaired social relationships, spiritual distress, financial crisis, role strain, disruption of daily life, and uncertainty. Downstream consequences include impaired physical health status, psychiatric illness, and poor quality of life.
- Instruments named: **Zarit Burden Interview, Caregiver Reaction Assessment, Family Strain Questionnaire-Short Form (FSQ-SF), Caregiver Strain Index (CSI)**, and PROMIS measures.
- In a cross-sectional study of 100 patients with cirrhosis and their caregivers, caregiver burden scores were significantly increased among patients with either **previous overt hepatic encephalopathy or minimal hepatic encephalopathy**, and were inversely correlated with liver disease severity scores. **Independent predictors of caregiver burden: repeated hospital admissions, alcohol as etiology, and lower socioeconomic status.**
- Among patients with cirrhosis who have previously experienced hepatic encephalopathy, **cognitive performance and MELD score are correlated with caregiver burden**.
- Caregiver strain before and after liver transplant appears to **predominantly impair role, rather than physical, quality of life**.
- Functional decline: in a prospective Health and Retirement Study cohort of older adults with cirrhosis, **nearly 30% demonstrated functional decline over a median of 2 years**, and **18% had severe functional decline**.
- Annual costs of home health aide provision to older adults with cirrhosis were estimated at **more than double** those of an age-matched comparison group — **for 2009, $4700 per person with cirrhosis vs $2100 without cirrhosis**.
- A small prospective trial of **20 patient/caregiver dyads** showed that a short (**4-week**) intervention of mindfulness and supportive group therapy improved caregiver burden, mental health, and sleep.

### Barriers to delivery

- In a survey study of cirrhosis providers, **91%** noted competing demands for time as a significant barrier to engaging in PC, and **76%** cited insufficient reimbursement.
- In the same survey, **81%** of respondents thought that ACP discussions occurred **too late** in the illness trajectory.
- In an Alberta public-awareness campaign cohort, **one third or less** had documented directives or goals of care.
- Reimbursement trends are beginning to reflect PC's value, with codes for **chronic care management, ACP, and transitional care management**.
- Practical efficiency levers (BPA 8): validated screening surveys administered by **ancillary clinic staff**; embedding **advance practice providers with PC training**; multidisciplinary delivery "shouldered by a group rather than an individual."
- **Referral triggers (BPA 9):** clear, locally agreed triggers and pathways should be established with the local specialist PC team. Needs appropriate for **specialty** PC referral include difficult ACP and goals-of-care discussions, management of refractory symptoms, complex care coordination, challenging family and treatment team dynamics, and referrals to hospice that involve conflict. Tools including the **Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool (SPICT)** can be used to guide referral, though evidence for this in cirrhosis is limited.
- **Hospice referral (BPA 10):** appropriate when prognosis is estimated to be **≤6 months** and goals are comfort-oriented. Insurance coverage of hospice benefits **usually requires comfort-focused care without pursuit of further curative treatments**. Referral should not come within days of death or as a surprise; it should follow preceding ACP and goals-of-care discussions.
- Barriers to receiving PC/hospice in cirrhosis relate to **ethnicity, geography, and comorbid medical and psychiatric conditions**.
- In non-cirrhosis populations, PC is associated with better quality of life, less aggressive treatment at the end of life, lower costs of care, improved communication between healthcare professionals, and better caregiver outcomes; in advanced cancer, ACP and goals-of-care conversations were associated with improved quality of life, less intensive end-of-life treatment, earlier hospice enrollment, and better bereavement adjustment in family members.

## Instruments for Symptom Assessment (Table 3)

*Table 3 note, as printed: the table includes selected liver-specific tools as well as instruments applicable to other patient populations. There are few data to support which tools are best to incorporate into research or clinical practice; the instruments vary in domains assessed, length, scoring, and availability in languages other than English, and choice will vary with clinical need and expediency.*

| Instrument | Domains | Items, scale |
|---|---|---|
| Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ) | Abdominal symptoms, fatigue, systemic symptoms, activity, emotional function, worry | 29 questions, scale 1–7 |
| Liver Disease Quality of Life (LDQOL) | Symptoms, effects on activities of daily living, concentration, memory, sexual function, sexual problems, sleep, loneliness, hopelessness, quality of social interaction, health distress, self-perceived stigma of liver disease | 75 questions; 0–100 scale |
| Short Form Liver Disease Quality of Life (SF-LDQOL) | Symptoms, effects of liver disease, memory/concentration, sleep, hopelessness, distress, loneliness, stigma of liver | 36 questions; 0–100 scale |
| Liver Disease Symptom Index (LDSI) | Itch, joint pain, abdominal pain, daytime sleepiness, worry about family situation, decreased appetite, depression, fear of complications, jaundice | 18 questions; symptoms 1–5 scale; symptom hindrance 0–10 scale |
| Liver Cirrhosis Patient-reported Outcome Measure (LC-PROM) | Physical, psychological, social, therapeutic | 55 items (0–4 scale) within 13 dimensions belonging to 4 domains |
| PROMIS (PROMIS-29, PROMIS-CAT) | Anxiety/fear, cognitive function, depression/sadness, fatigue, instrumental support, pain interference, physical function, sleep disturbance, social roles | 9 health domains; access to gender- and age-matched normative data |
| SF-36 | Vitality, physical role functioning, bodily pain, general health perception, physical function, social role functioning, emotional role functioning, mental health | 36 questions, split into 8 domains with 2 summary scores: physical component summary (PCS) and mental component summary (MCS) |
| Sickness Impact Profile (SIP) | Sleep and rest, eating, work, home management, recreation and pastimes, ambulation, mobility, body care and movement, social interaction, alertness behavior, emotional behavior, communication | 136 items in 12 categories |
| Nottingham Health Profile (NHP) | I: energy, sleep, emotions, pain, mobility, social isolation; II: paid employment, housework, hobbies, family life, social life, sex life, holidays | 2 parts; Part I: 38 questions in 6 subareas; Part II: 7 life areas |
| Distress Thermometer (DT) | Overall assessment of distress plus practical problems, family problems, emotional problems, spiritual/religious concerns, physical problems | Distress: **0 (no distress) to 10 (extreme distress)** visual analogue scale resembling a thermometer; problem list: 39 (Yes/No) and 1 open-ended (other problems) |
| Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) | Pain, fatigue, myalgia, sexual dysfunction, anxiety, sleep disturbance, appetite, well-being, dyspnea, pruritus | **10 items, 0–10 visual analogue scale**; scores: Physical, Emotional, and Total |
| Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) | Anhedonia, feeling down, sleep, feeling tired, appetite, feeling bad about self, concentration, activity, suicidality | **9 items scored 0–3; total score 0–27**, used to categorize depression severity as minimal/none, mild, moderate, moderately severe, or severe |
| Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) | Anhedonia, feeling down (first two items of the PHQ-9) | **2 items.** If yes is selected for one or both questions, go on to perform the PHQ-9. If no to both questions, the screen is negative for depression. |

*The update does not state which PHQ-9 total-score ranges correspond to each severity band; it names the bands only.*

## Quality of Life as a Prognostic Marker (Supplementary Table 2)

| Study population | n | Instrument | Key findings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retrospective analysis of patients with cirrhosis and severe ascites in a satavaptan clinical trial | 405 | SF-36 | After confounder adjustment, hazard ratio for a **10-point increase in the physical component score** was **0.83 (95% CI 0.72–0.97)** for all-cause mortality and **0.84 (95% CI 0.71–0.99)** for cirrhosis-related deaths |
| Multicenter prospective study of patients with cirrhosis, mean MELD 12 | 402 | CLDQ and EuroQoL Group visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS) | Health-related quality of life was independently associated with the primary outcome of short-term unplanned hospitalization/mortality. Every **1-point increase in the CLDQ** and every **10-point increase in the EQ-VAS** reduced the risk of reaching this outcome by **30%** and **13%**, respectively |
| Single-center prospective study of patients awaiting liver transplant | 156 | Short Form Liver Disease Quality of Life | Higher baseline HRQOL predicted lower mortality (**HR 0.96; 95% CI 0.94–0.99**), independent of MELD |
| Multicenter prospective study, Pulmonary Vascular Complications of Liver Disease study | 252 | SF-36 | Lower baseline Physical Component Summary scores were associated with increased mortality rate despite adjustment for age, gender, MELD score, and liver transplantation (**P for trend = .0001**) |

## Moving From Awareness to Execution (Table 2)

*Table 2 note, as printed: integration of universal palliative care for patients with cirrhosis is a process; the table presents both current target expectations and targets to aspire to.*

| Expected (awareness) | Aspirational (execution) |
|---|---|
| Cirrhosis is life-limiting | Early integration of care with palliative principles |
| Goals of care discussion | Advanced directive, relevant location-specific goals of care designation on every patient, reviewed at every event |
| Routine assessment of symptoms | Routine administration of symptom surveys by clinic staff; care coordination that allows continuous management of symptoms |
| Referral to specialist palliative care when symptoms are beyond scope | Co-management of patients with a multidisciplinary team |
| Ask about caregiver burden | Systematic assessment of caregiver burden; routine management of caregiver burden |
| As-needed adjustment of clinical schedules to allow time for discussion with use of time-based billing codes | Preemptive scheduling with use of advance care planning codes |
| Patients have opportunity to receive hospice services at the end of life | Patients understand overall trajectory of illness and are informed with prognosis in line with prognostic awareness and readiness. Timely referral to hospice for patients with a prognosis of 6 months or less to ensure a "comprehensive, socially supportive, pain-reducing, and comforting alternative to technologically elaborate, medically centered interventions." |

## Implementation Roadmap (Supplementary Table 1)

*Intended for delivery of PC by clinicians who are not PC specialists and who do not have routine integration of specialist PC within their outpatient or inpatient system.*

| BPA | Awareness | Suggested first step | Expand implementation | Mature practice target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | PC ≠ hospice; PC can and should be offered alongside "curative" therapies; PC can also be relevant for compensated patients | Teach learners about PC principles and their broad applicability across the range of patients with cirrhosis | Pick a week to actively identify opportunities to initiate PC in outpatients and inpatients with cirrhosis | Early integration of care with palliative principles in all patients with cirrhosis |
| 2 | Providers involved in the care of patients with cirrhosis should strive to contribute to care with palliative principles | Identify gaps in comfort/knowledge: symptom screen and basic management; prognostication and prognostic-readiness assessment; ACP and GCD; function screen; caregiver needs screen and management | From BPAs 3–10 choose an area to begin integrating into practice | A systematic approach to early integration of palliative principles in all patients with cirrhosis by all providers involved in their care |
| 3 | The symptom burden of patients with cirrhosis is tremendous | Set up a collaborative brainstorming session between local primary care physicians and specialty PC colleagues to determine who manages symptoms flagged on routine screening | Initiate routine screening of symptoms and distress in the liver clinic using the **ESAS and Distress Thermometer** (or similar); ancillary staff can initiate these | Evidence-based symptom scales and guidelines readily available to all providers; role-clarity discussions between GI/hepatology and primary care about who screens and who acts |
| 4 | Early ACP and goals-of-care designations are infrequent but important to ensure value-aligned care; requires practice with high-quality communication | Explore ACP tools and communication courses (provider-facing: Vital Talk, Respecting Choices/Center to Advance Palliative Care courses, Serious Illness Conversation Guide; patient-facing: American Society on Aging website) | Block off separate clinic visits to initiate ACP and GCD conversations, initially in decompensated patients, then tailored conversations in compensated patients | Advanced directive and relevant locale-specific goals-of-care designation on every patient |
| 5 | Caregiver burden is common, especially in decompensated disease; it has physical, mental, and financial consequences | Become familiar with one of the caregiver burden indices (e.g. **Caregiver Strain Index**) | Talk to local primary care and specialty PC colleagues about resources available once caregiver burden is identified | Routine screening of caregiver burden, carried out by ancillary staff; locale-specific resources provided to patient/caregiver |
| 6 | Prognostication is challenging in cirrhosis; providers may avoid these discussions because of prognostic uncertainty | Become familiar with prognostication tools **in addition to MELD and Child-Pugh** (e.g. functional assessment such as frailty, changes in function over time) | Sign up for a short course on high-quality ACP and GCD conversations (e.g. Serious Illness Conversation Guide) | Prognostic readiness assessed on every patient, at routine care visits and sentinel events |
| 7 | Goals of care discussions should be repeated at sentinel events to ensure value-aligned care | Become familiar with locally accepted GCD documentation forms and associated resources | Use online resources supporting high-quality ACP and GCD conversations | Advance directive and relevant locale-specific goals-of-care designation on every patient, reviewed at every event |
| 8 | Strategies exist to optimize efficiency of PC delivery by all members of the healthcare team | Find out local billing numbers for chronic care management, ACP, and transitional care management | Adjust clinic schedules to allow time for discussion with time-based billing codes | Preemptive scheduling of patients for ACP discussions with use of ACP codes |
| 9 | It is useful for liver specialists and primary care physicians to work with local specialist PC teams to establish clear triggers and pathways for referral | Make contact with a member of the local specialist PC team to determine the process for accepting cirrhosis referrals | Referral to specialist PC when symptoms are beyond scope | Referral to specialist PC when symptoms are beyond scope; eventually integrated PC management with a multidisciplinary team |
| 10 | Existing hospice referrals are often made late; they should ideally be made in a timely fashion | Find out local referral criteria for hospice and what would be required to refer a patient with cirrhosis | If consistent with the patient's values, patients have the opportunity to receive hospice services at end of life | Patients understand the overall trajectory of illness and are informed with prognosis in line with prognostic awareness and readiness; timely referral to hospice for patients with a prognosis of 6 months or less |

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Versus [[aasld-2022-palliative-cirrhosis]] (newer, same tier — AASLD wins where they differ):** no direct clinical contradiction was found. The two are complementary — AASLD 2022 carries the **symptom-by-symptom pharmacologic management** (pain and the agents to avoid, pruritus, cramps, dyspnea, sleep, mood), which AGA 2020 deliberately does not supply; AGA 2020 carries the **delivery framework** (BPA statements, primary vs specialty PC, referral triggers, sentinel events, caregiver screening, billing/efficiency levers), which AASLD does not enumerate. Scope differs: AGA 2020 explicitly covers **compensated cirrhosis and transplant candidates**, whereas the AASLD guidance is titled for **decompensated** cirrhosis — where a claim is specific to decompensated disease, the AASLD text governs.
- **This update contains no symptom-management drug names, doses, or intervals.** Its position is that most physical symptoms are best addressed by optimal management of cirrhosis-related complications, with pruritus named as an example needing specific therapy; it does not say which therapy. Dosing for symptom control comes from AASLD 2022, not from here.
- **No evidence grades.** The ten statements are unlabelled best practice advice; no GRADE or strength/quality rating exists in the document (see [[#Evidence grading]]).
- **Prognostication is acknowledged as unreliable at the individual-patient level.** MELD, MELD-Na, Child-Pugh, and CLIF-C ACLF all have recognized limitations, and both clinician estimates and models failed to predict mortality in a large end-of-life cohort — yet BPA 10 conditions hospice referral on a ≤6-month prognosis. The update does not resolve this tension or give a score-based threshold that operationalizes "6 months or less."
- **No threshold is given for any screening instrument.** The update names ESAS, PHQ-2/PHQ-9, the Distress Thermometer, the Caregiver Strain Index, and the Zarit Burden Interview and gives their scales, but states no cut-point that should trigger action or referral; it says only that "there are few data to support which tools are best."
- **SUPPORT albumin criterion printed as "serum albumin ≤3.0 mg/dL"** — reproduced as printed; the unit is as it appears in the source.
