## Summary

Chronic digestive diseases — irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) among them — cannot be disentangled from their psychological context. The burden of these diseases is **co-determined** by symptom/disease severity *and* by the patient's ability to cope without significant interruption to daily life. Burdens are amplified when symptoms are severe or refractory, when psychiatric comorbidity is present, and when coping skills are impaired. The update frames psychogastroenterology as the field applying scientifically based psychological principles and techniques to digestive symptoms, and argues that the gastroenterologist — not the mental health provider — is the rate-limiting step: without a strong, well-informed recommendation from the gastroenterologist, many patients never receive these treatments at all, or receive them far too late, after self-management has failed and refractory psychopathology and/or inflammatory disease have developed.

The document describes the structure and efficacy of **2 major classes of brain–gut psychotherapy — cognitive-behavior therapy (CBT) and gut-directed hypnotherapy** — and their impact on gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms and on coping, resilience and self-regulation. Brain–gut psychotherapies work on **2 pathways**: they target abdominal pain, visceral hypersensitivity and GI motility; and/or they facilitate improved coping, resilience and self-regulation skills. They are optimally delivered by mental health professionals specializing in psychogastroenterology, and have the capacity to reduce health care utilization and symptom burden **especially when integrated directly into GI practice settings**.

The five Best Practice Advice statements move from assessment (health-related quality of life [QOL], symptom-specific anxiety, early life adversity, functional impairment), through patient-facing explanation (the bi-directional brain–gut pathway; behavioral/psychosocial perpetuating factors; why a referral is being made), to the gastroenterologist's own working knowledge of the therapies, the construction of a **direct referral and ongoing communication pathway with 1–2 qualified mental health providers**, and familiarity with **1 or 2 neuromodulators** to complement behavioral therapy.

Three tables carry the operative content: **Table 1** (the five Best Practice Advice statements), **Table 2** (key points for explaining the brain–gut axis to a patient, in lay language) and **Table 3** (key points for explaining a mental-health referral to a patient, in lay language). **Table 4** is a reading list mapping recommended articles to the Best Practice Advice they support. There are **no figures and no diagnostic or treatment algorithm** in this document.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Burden and rationale

- Chronic digestive disorders cost the health care system **billions of dollars**.
- The most chronic digestive disorders are the functional GI and motility disorders; burdens are amplified by severe/refractory symptoms, psychiatric comorbidity, impaired coping.
- **Reflux hypersensitivity and functional heartburn:** patients acquire risk from **unnecessary long-term proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use** when esophageal hypervigilance overshadows symptom–reflux correlation.
- **IBS can progress into severely disabling centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome**; psychosocial factors **coupled with opiate use increase the risk of narcotic bowel syndrome**.
- **IBD: 15% of patients account for approximately 50% of health care expenditures** — apparently driven by concomitant chronic pain, depression and poor social support.
- **Depression in IBD** increases risk for surgery, hospitalizations and disability, and may contribute to disease flare.
- Stress has important and commonly deleterious effects on gut function through **neural, immune and microbiome-related interactions**. Chronic stress and emotional distress impair the body's control of inflammation, increasing risk of peripheral and central pain sensitization and affecting symptom status and clinical outcomes in IBD, GERD and peptic ulcer disease.

### Best Practice Advice 1 — what to assess, and with what

- **QOL impact varies greatly among patients with the same diagnosis**, even when disease history, location, severity and medical therapy are similar — it depends on individual coping and resilience, GI-specific anxiety, need for lifestyle adaptation and stress. Patients with substantial GI-related life impact may need more, or differently targeted, medical interventions than patients with the same diagnosis.
- Most pronounced impacts of GI symptoms on QOL: **fatigue, limitation of life activities, and pain**.
- **IBS patients demonstrate lower QOL than patients with end-stage renal disease and diabetes.**
- **Assessment can be informal and brief — 1–2 open-ended questions**, e.g. *"How do your bowel symptoms interfere with your ability to do what you want to do in your daily life?"* and *"What areas of your life are affected most?"* Four stated benefits:
  1. Without an open-ended option the physician may remain unaware of key areas where targeted medical intervention would most restore life functioning.
  2. Life-impact questions **build rapport** — the doctor is aware of, and interested in, the burden of the illness.
  3. Responses give **comparison data points** for rechecking later, a more complete picture of true progress than change in digestive symptoms alone.
  4. Impact questions identify patients who clearly need a behavioral health professional — and a referral **proposed specifically to improve QOL is more likely to be well received**.

| Instrument | Use as described |
|---|---|
| Irritable Bowel Syndrome Quality of Life (IBS-QOL) | Disease-specific QOL measure; clinical aid for quantifying current disease impact if feasible to incorporate into routine visits |
| Inflammatory Bowel Disease Questionnaire (IBDQ) | Disease-specific QOL measure, same use |
| 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36) | **General** QOL measure — *not* usually clinically useful in GI care; unclear whether poor scores are attributable to the GI disorder; less likely to reflect treatment response |
| Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) | Free-to-use brief questionnaire for depression; useful to have on hand for a quick measurement when symptoms are exhibited |
| Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS), National Institutes of Health–supported | Free screening tools; useful where patients are being screened in comparison to the general population (e.g. primary care) |
| Adverse Childhood Experience (ACE) Questionnaire | Commonly used screen for **early life adversity** |

- **Routine screening for depression and anxiety is not required for the general GI population**, but may be helpful in special, high-risk populations **including IBD**.
- **Early life adversity — what to ask about:** personal history of physical, emotional or sexual abuse; witnessed domestic violence in the household growing up; raised by a caregiver with a substance abuse problem or mental illness; a family member who was incarcerated. These identify critical factors affecting the onset and expression of functional GI disorder symptoms and **which patients would benefit from psychological intervention earlier in care**.
- **Symptom-specific anxiety** amplifies disease burden and leads to higher health care utilization. In a survey of **1242 IBS patients**, **43%** thought their disorder could develop into colitis and **21%** into cancer. In IBD, worries concern the effect of biologic therapy or surgery, whether pain indicates flare/obstruction/perforation, and infertility risk.
- Single screening question: ***"What worries or concerns do you have about your symptoms?"*** — yields an opportunity for education and reassurance, and can identify excessive anxiety, catastrophizing or depressive symptoms weighing heavily enough to warrant behavioral referral.

### Best Practice Advice 2 — explaining the brain–gut axis (Table 2, lay language)

Framing sentence: in recent years we have learned that the brain and the intestines are much more closely connected than previously thought, and the brain actually plays a big role in keeping the functioning of the intestines normal and healthy.

1. The brain and the intestines **communicate with each other continually** through nerves and chemical signals.
2. The intestines send frequent messages to the brain to let the brain know about their condition — such as fullness from a meal or the need to have a bowel movement.
3. The brain usually **dampens these nerve signals** so they are not uncomfortable, and keeps them outside conscious awareness most of the time.
4. The brain sends frequent messages to the intestines, both in response to these internal messages and to **fine-tune the activity of gut muscles, secretion of acid and fluids, and immune activity** — coordinating gut function with what is going on inside and around us (e.g. telling the gut to increase stomach acid secretion when food is expected; instructing the intestines to inhibit digestive activity during physical exercise).
5. This normal brain–gut communication **can sometimes go wrong** when something disturbs the brain or the nervous system for an extended time — very substantial life stress, strong negative emotions or inadequate sleep. Inflammation or infection in the body can also make the normal brain–gut communication go awry. The brain then perceives sensations from the gut more strongly than usual, and may start sending inappropriate signals down to the gut that disturb intestinal functioning.

Closing point: because the brain is part of the control system of the gut, it is often possible to get it to help reduce the intestinal disturbance and symptoms **regardless of whether it is actively contributing to making the symptoms happen** — by specialized psychological treatment (CBT, gut-directed hypnosis) and by medications that make the brain less sensitive to input from the intestines.

- **Educate early in the relationship.** Raised only after everything else has failed, the explanation implies a solely psychological cause of symptoms. Presented early, when conventional medication or treatment has produced unsatisfactory relief, it gives patients a clear rationale for alternative approaches.
- Self-regulatory and self-care methods — **meditation, relaxation, yoga, physical exercise** — can also be an integral part of GI health.

### Best Practice Advice 2 — behavioral perpetuating factors

- **Stress, poor sleep quality and quantity, maladaptive eating patterns** (large meals, eating late at night) and **lack of physical activity** are potent symptom contributors — and are **within the patient's control and can be adjusted**.
- Emphasize that behavioral/psychosocial contributors, though changeable and able to attenuate disease severity, are **generally not the causes of the GI disease**.
- Most health problems are **multifactorial**, with unlikely to be a single solution, at least long-term → introduce the concept of routine mental health referral **early in the evaluation**, where it will be better received.
- **Factors that initiate a disease process** (inflammation or immune dysregulation, surgery, stress) **do not always remain active** despite ongoing symptoms. Models illustrating this well: **post-infectious IBS** and **IBD with functional overlap** — both common conditions whose etiologies differ from their maintaining factors. Highlighting these models moves patients past looking for a cause or explanation for why they are symptomatic (e.g. the infection they got, the surgery they had) toward **solutions they can act upon** (exercise, diet, lifestyle, stress, adherence).

### Best Practice Advice 2 — explaining the referral (Table 3, lay language)

Once the patient understands the brain–gut connection, shift attention to the therapies that work on this pathway:

1. **Introduce neuromodulation** — some medications, such as low-dose antidepressants and anti-seizure medications, can alter neurologic transmission of signals to the brain and dampen some of the discomfort.
   - Cons: side effects; limited efficacy, especially when compared to behavioral therapies; most patients will need to commit to these medications long-term.
2. **When introducing hypnosis or cognitive therapy, give the biologic reasoning:**
   - The GI tract is always moving through peristalsis and accommodation, but the brain's normal job is to prevent you from feeling this movement — feeling your GI tract all day would be too distracting. Compare to putting on a watch or glasses: you feel it when you put them on to make sure they are correctly placed, but after a minute your brain stops paying attention to that signal — a healthy mechanism so you can focus your attention on important tasks at hand.
   - This **filtering out of non-essential sensations is extremely important but very complex and poorly understood**. Psychological therapies can help you divert your brain's attention away from the symptoms, can help you to be less bothered by your symptoms, and allow you to focus on more important things.
3. **Stay factual about effects and potential mechanisms** of brain–gut psychotherapies — this sets the stage for a much more informed visit with the psychologist and **dispels the pretense that the GI doctor thinks the patient has nothing wrong**.
   - CBT provides a skill set that helps change the way you view your symptoms, or makes your symptoms feel less threatening.
   - **Hypnosis is not a stage entertainment show and is nothing magical** — it is a real medical therapy that has been studied with great outcomes.
   - **There are no side effects to these therapies**, and the positive effects on symptoms often last for years.

- Failure to properly explain the reason for referral leads to poor follow-through and will likely lead the patient to seek care with another provider; it can also damage an otherwise good physician–patient relationship and decrease the likelihood of successful symptom control.
- **The physician does not need to decide on the specific type of behavior therapy** the patient will benefit from — this is usually best decided by the behavioral health care provider.

### Best Practice Advice 3 — the therapies themselves

**Shared features of brain–gut psychotherapies**, distinguishing them from traditional psychotherapy:

- They leverage the brain's ability to bring under voluntary control those symptom processes that seem, initially, to be driven completely by the gut.
- **Typically short-term and GI symptom–focused.**
- **Skills-based**, focused specifically on down-regulation of unpleasant GI sensations, decreasing avoidance behaviors associated with fear of having symptoms, and building coping and resilience to stress or lifestyle changes imposed by a chronic condition. They differ from traditional psychotherapy, which focuses on providing support, normalizing psychopathology and/or facilitating insight into the reason the patient is experiencing symptoms.
- **Impact can be durable long after therapy is discontinued**, making them cost-effective long-term compared with psychotropics.
- Best results when delivered by a health psychologist or other medically trained mental health provider familiar with the physiological basis of symptoms and the goals of the medical team.
- **Patients with comorbid chronic depression or anxiety are typically not good candidates.**

| | Cognitive-behavior therapy (CBT) | Gut-directed hypnotherapy |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence base cited | **>30 randomized controlled trials**, number needed to treat **3**; in IBS specifically, >30 trials for CBT and 11 for hypnosis, numbers needed to treat **between 2 and 4** | **>11 randomized controlled trials** across pediatric and adult GI disorders |
| Conditions | Depressed teenagers with IBD; augmenting self-management behavior in Crohn's disease; functional GI disorders including those in the context of IBD | IBS, heartburn, IBD; also used across non-cardiac chest pain, functional dyspepsia, GERD and IBD (whole class) |
| Targets | Maladaptive or "unhelpful" thoughts, feelings and behaviors **as they relate to symptoms**, rather than identifying a root cause or initiating event. Specific maladaptive cognitive-affective processes: **catastrophizing, cognitive inflexibility, fear of symptoms, hypervigilance/attentional bias to benign gut sensations** | **Visceral hypersensitivity, motility disturbance, hypervigilance to benign sensations, somatization** |
| Techniques | **Diaphragmatic breathing, cognitive restructuring, learning new problem-solving skills** — varying forms of skills remediation depending on the patient's GI symptom profile | Scripted protocols; **interoceptive exposure** (building tolerance to unpleasant GI symptoms), with and without mindfulness, is another CBT technique used successfully in patients with symptom-specific anxiety |
| Dose / delivery | **As few as 4 therapy sessions** with efficacy equivalent to longer treatment courses in IBS; effectively delivered **by nurses, over the telephone, and in groups**; promise when delivered online for both IBS and IBD. **Diaphragmatic breathing elements used for esophageal conditions including rumination disorder and supragastric belching** | **7-session North Carolina Protocol** and **12-session Manchester protocol** — similar benefits in refractory IBS, with **up to 80% of patients acquiring at least a 50% reduction in IBS Severity Scoring**. The **fully scripted North Carolina Protocol** has been delivered as a **home-based therapy**, validated for use with **gastro-esophageal reflux** and **ulcerative colitis**, and is particularly useful for enabling **less-experienced providers** to deliver gut-directed hypnotherapy |

### Best Practice Advice 3 — who the providers are

- **GI psychologists** — licensed providers with a **doctoral degree**, falling under the American Psychological Association's recognized subspecialty of **clinical health psychology** (application of psychological principles and conditions to medical settings and multidisciplinary teams). Health psychologists may develop further expertise within a specific medical subspecialty (GI, obstetrics/gynecology, oncology, pain) or practice as generalists. Generalists are often pleased to see patients with GI conditions and will usually seek out either specialized training in GI or read the literature on the disease they are treating and its effective treatment approaches.
- **Psychiatrists** — medical doctors focused on determining a patient's specific psychiatric diagnosis and remediating symptoms with psychotropic medication. A smaller subset specialize in **psychosomatic medicine** and are a welcome addition to a multidisciplinary GI team.
- The **Rome Foundation** has recently created an internationally based **psychogastroenterology section** bringing together psychologists, gastroenterologists, psychiatrists, internists/pediatricians with psychosomatic interests, and non-doctorally trained providers such as nurses, nurse practitioners, physician assistants and social workers. **Future directions may include establishing core competencies or necessary training credentials** for the practice of psychogastroenterology.

### Best Practice Advice 4 — building the referral pathway

- Access to evidence-based brain–gut psychotherapies is limited, and not every gastroenterologist will know of a GI health psychologist. Viable alternatives: **reach out to the state psychological society** (e.g. [state name] Psychological Association) or to a **national registry** for therapists interested in seeing GI patients and familiar with at least one of the evidence-based brain–gut psychotherapies.
- **The typical toolbox of someone equipped to see GI patients:** experience with medical populations or chronic illness; collaborates with physicians; trained under a cognitive-behavioral theoretical orientation; collaborative, active patient interaction style; practices 1 or more of the evidence-based brain–gut psychotherapies; and if practising hypnotherapy, training provided through a reputable organization focused on training health professionals.
- A clear referral and communication pathway improves outcomes by **establishing buy-in on the part of the patient** (they are not just left to find a mental health provider on their own) and ensures the focus of treatment remains the GI condition.
- **The mental health provider should feel free to call and ask the GI doctor questions** about the patient's symptoms, workup and medical recommendations. GI doctors should **expect to receive a copy of the patient's psychological intake report and possibly a final treatment summary**, and should **plan to see the patient themselves sometime during the course of active psychotherapy** — to maintain continuity of care, reinforce therapy gains, change or reduce medication use, and troubleshoot any challenges.
- **Contrary to common assumption, the presence of significant psychological distress does not suggest suitability for referral to a GI psychologist** — brain–gut psychotherapies are **less effective when a patient has comorbid psychopathology**.

| Patient's psychological comorbidity | Sequencing advised |
|---|---|
| Comorbid Axis I disorder with **very pronounced emotional symptoms** | **Address those problems first** — either a more general psychologist in the community focusing on depression and anxiety disorders, or psychotropic medications. This often improves the odds of the patient subsequently benefitting from GI-focused psychological treatment |
| **More moderate** emotional symptoms | Affective disorders can be addressed **in parallel** with the work of the GI psychologist |

- Gastroenterologists should have information on a **wide range of mental health resources, including free or sliding-scale programs**, in their communities to ensure appropriate mental health system access.

### Best Practice Advice 5 — neuromodulators

- Neuromodulation/psychotropic use is **sometimes necessary and often effective** in chronic digestive diseases and should be on the radar of practising gastroenterologists, **especially given a shortage of qualified psychiatrists**.
- In keeping with the patient-friendly, de-stigmatizing rationale for brain–gut psychotherapies, there may be scenarios in which a health psychologist and gastroenterologist are comfortable that the introduction of a neuromodulator — **particularly a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI); serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI); or tricyclic antidepressant (TCA)** — could **augment ongoing behavior therapy**.
- **Common reasons to add one:** a **diagnosis of a minor comorbid depression or anxiety disorder**; **lack of insight or motivation** that impedes participation in psychological treatment; or **chronic pain that is not responding fully to behavioral intervention**.
- **No doses, titration schedules or durations are given in this document** — it refers the reader to Sobin et al (2017) for a primer on central neuromodulation in functional GI disorders and to Thorkelson et al (2016) for the same in IBD.

### Cost, coverage and access

- These highly effective therapies are **short term (usually less than 3 months)** and have a **one-time total cost between $1000 and $2000 out of pocket**.
- **Effects are commonly durable for several years, and therapy usually does not need to be re-administered.** Framed as monthly out-of-pocket cost against other uncovered interventions (probiotics, herbs, special diets, colonic cleanses), the expense often becomes of less concern.
- **Health psychologists can be reimbursed for health and behavior codes** for treating these conditions — **Current Procedural Terminology codes 96150/96152** — but there are **restrictions on which other types of professionals can use them**. Medicare and commercial insurance plans often cover the cost of services; many providers are out of network or do not accept insurance.

### Research and clinical gaps named by the authors

- Lack of **comparative effectiveness trials** comparing brain–gut psychotherapies against each other and/or against psychotropic medications.
- Insufficient research to date on **other promising brain–gut therapies — mindfulness meditation and acceptance-based approaches**.
- **Only a few studies** support the impact of brain–gut psychotherapies on **actual disease course or inflammation** in Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis; **limited studies** demonstrate utility in accelerating or enhancing the efficacy of pharmacologic therapies.
- Clinical gaps: **insurance coverage**; need for **more trained providers** and expansion of **remote, online or digitally based** brain–gut therapies to offset overhead and therapy costs. Cost-effectiveness research is underway and may lead to wider adoption by accountable care organizations, payors, employers and other stakeholders.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Candidacy of patients with psychiatric comorbidity — differs from [[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]].** This 2018 update states that brain–gut psychotherapies are **less effective when a patient has comorbid psychopathology**, that patients with comorbid chronic depression or anxiety are **typically not good candidates**, and that very pronounced emotional symptoms should be treated **before** GI-focused psychological treatment. The 2021 update on chronic GI pain in disorders of gut–brain interaction instead advises raising brain–gut psychotherapies **at the outset of care** for painful disorders. Both are AGA Clinical Practice Updates and both are ungraded; **the newer 2021 statement holds on the entity pages** by publication date, with the 2018 sequencing rule recorded alongside it as the guidance on comorbid psychopathology, which 2021 does not address.
- **Internal wording discrepancy:** Best Practice Advice 5 reads *augment* in Table 1 and *complement* in the body text. Best Practice Advice 1 and 2 also differ in minor wording between Table 1 and body. Recorded above; no clinical difference except the augment/complement emphasis.
- **Neuromodulator detail is deliberately absent.** The document names the three classes (SSRI, SNRI, TCA) and the clinical triggers for adding one, but gives **no agent, dose, titration or duration** — it refers the reader to two external primers. Dosing on the wiki comes from [[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]], not from this source.
- **No figure, no algorithm.** The document has four tables and no figures; there is no diagnostic or treatment flowchart to capture.
