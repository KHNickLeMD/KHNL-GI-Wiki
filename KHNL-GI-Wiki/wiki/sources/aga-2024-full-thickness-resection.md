## Summary

Subepithelial lesions (SELs) arise from the wall of the GI tract and can originate from the muscularis mucosa, submucosa, or muscularis propria. Most are small and found incidentally; **approximately 15% have the potential for malignant transformation**, with progression risk varying by size, GI-tract location, and histology. Definitive diagnosis with biopsy or EUS-FNA/FNB is not always possible — **FNA/FNB diagnostic yields range from as low as 20% to >90%** and are highly operator-dependent. Surgery has been the traditional management of select neoplastic SELs; endoscopic full-thickness resection (EFTR) has emerged as a less invasive alternative for both diagnosis and treatment.

**EFTR** = local resection of a tumor, often necessitating dissection through the adjacent muscularis propria to achieve complete resection. It is performed by an **"exposed"** technique (resection of all layers *including the mucosa*, i.e. full-thickness excision completed **before** wall-defect closure) or a **"nonexposed"** technique (either preserving an overlying mucosal flap, as in submucosal tunneling endoscopic resection [STER], or a **"close first, then cut"** device-assisted method that avoids the impending perforation).

The Update covers preoperative evaluation and setup, then compares the three technique families: exposed EFTR/ESD (any size, any layer, but complex closure and contamination/seeding risk — should be done at referral centers with extensive third-space experience), STER/POET (stable, easy closure, but size-limited to ~3 cm), and device-assisted EFTR with the full-thickness resection device (FTRD; Ovesco) — easy to use with a short learning curve but limited to small lesions (<15 mm) with substantially lower R0 rates for SELs.

Site matters: exposed EFTR is **best suited for gastric lesions** and is an alternative for **rectal** SELs; it **should be avoided in the esophagus and duodenum**, where the clinical consequences of a leak can be devastating and endoscopic closure is notoriously challenging. Laparoscopic-endoscopic cooperative surgery (LECS) is an option in which EFTR is performed with laparoscopic guidance and the surgeon closes the gastrotomy and retrieves the lesion.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Preoperative evaluation

- All patients considered for EFTR of SELs should undergo a **comprehensive evaluation before the procedure**, as outlined in the AGA Clinical Practice Update on SELs. This evaluation includes **endoscopy, endoscopic ultrasound, cytology, histopathology, and cross-sectional imaging, when indicated.**
- "Given the complexity of the management of these SELs, we strongly advise a **multidisciplinary approach**. This should include the participation of **surgeons, pathologists, radiologists, oncologists, and gastroenterologists** in the decision-making process."

### Preoperative setup (all near-verbatim)

- Advantages to performing EFTR **under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation** (as with other third-space interventions).
- **Positive pressure ventilation** can be used to reduce the adverse effects of **capnoperitoneum and capnomediastinum**.
- **Constant neuromuscular relaxation** during general anesthesia may be desirable to reduce respiratory variability and movement during the procedure.
- **Peak airway pressures** can be monitored and used as a **surrogate for increased intra-abdominal pressure**, which could signal the potential need for **needle decompression**.
- **Prophylactic antibiotics via intravenous infusion are recommended at the time of the procedure**, given the potential risk of contamination of the peritoneal or mediastinal cavity from full-thickness resection or perforation.
- **Carbon dioxide insufflation is mandatory**, given its faster absorption compared with air and lower risk for adverse events.
- Perform in **endoscopy units in the hospital setting** to allow readily available support from surgical colleagues if needed.
- Units should be fully equipped with ESD devices/accessories: **single-channel gastroscope, dual-channel gastroscope, distal attachments, dedicated electrosurgical knives, hemostatic forceps, closure devices (over-the-scope clips, through-the-scope clips, suture devices), and peritoneal needles for decompression.** Storage in a **dedicated cart** facilitates quick intraprocedural access and simplifies inventory control.

| Technique | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Exposed — EFTR/ESD** | Suitable for lesion of any size; indicated for lesions arising from any layer of the GI tract wall | High likelihood of full-thickness defect in lesions arising from the muscularis propria requiring complex defect closure; risk for peritoneal/mediastinal contamination; hypothetical risk of tumor spillage; compromised insufflation during procedure |
| **Nonexposed — STER** | Submucosal tunnel provides stability for resection; easier closure of mucosal tunnel incision as opposed to full-thickness defect; mucosal flap minimizes risk of extraluminal leakage/contamination | **Limited to smaller lesions (≤3 cm)**; not feasible if mucosal integrity overlying SEL is compromised; submucosal tunneling not feasible in all anatomic locations |
| **Nonexposed — FTRD (and other device-assisted techniques)** | Indicated for lesions arising from any layer of the GI tract wall; short learning curve | **Limited to small lesions (<15 mm in size)**; occurrence of device malfunction (eg, clip slippage, inadequate closure, and failed deployment); high rates of incomplete resection |

### Exposed EFTR

- **Technique:** free-hand endoscopic resection in which full-thickness excision is completed **before** wall-defect closure. Mucosal incision around the lesion → submucosal dissection to isolate the lesion to the muscular base → careful muscular dissection to "strip" the lesion off the wall; **often full-thickness resection with division of the muscularis propria is required** to assure complete excision and preservation of the integrity of the tumor capsule.
- **Main advantage:** allows **en bloc resection of SELs irrespective of size**.
- **Technical drawbacks:** compromised intraluminal insufflation/visualization from CO₂ leakage into the peritoneum (may necessitate **intraprocedural abdominal decompression**); risk of **peritoneal contamination** from intraluminal contents; **theoretical risk of tumor seeding** into the peritoneal cavity; large defects increase closure difficulty (new tissue-apposition techniques/devices available).
- **Referral-center statement:** given the complexity of free-hand dissection and the need for advanced endoscopic closure techniques, exposed EFTR "has a longer learning curve and the potential for serious adverse events and, therefore, **should be performed at referral centers with extensive experience in 'third-space' endoscopy**."
- **Expert-opinion indication:** "In our opinion, the exposed EFTR technique should be considered for lesions in which **other methods (ie, endoscopic mucosal resection, ESD, and nonexposed EFTR) cannot reliably and completely excise SELs due to larger size or difficult location** of the lesion."
- **Site selection:** "The exposed EFTR technique may be **best suited for gastric lesions** and as an alternative to other endoscopic approaches for SELs in the **rectum**. The technique **should be avoided in the esophagus and duodenum**, as the clinical consequences of a leak can be devastating and endoscopic closure is notoriously challenging."
- **LECS:** laparoscopic-endoscopic cooperative surgery is an option wherein EFTR is performed with laparoscopic guidance and assistance; once resection is complete, closure of the gastrotomy and retrieval of the lesion is performed by the surgeon.
- **Evidence base:** data are scarce; most literature is small retrospective cohorts and case series. **Two retrospective cohort studies comparing EFTR with surgery found no differences in en bloc resection, complete resection, or adverse event rates.** Endoscopic resection is usually **faster, with less blood loss and shorter hospital stay.** Caveat: lesions removed endoscopically were more likely to be predominantly **intraluminal and smaller** than surgical cases.

### Nonexposed EFTR — STER / POET (peroral endoscopic tunnel resection)

- **Technique:** a submucosal tunnel is created to provide working space. The tunnel is **initiated 3–5 cm proximal to the lesion**, as feasible, and dissection is **extended 1–2 cm distal to the lesion**. After resection within the tunnel, the lesion is extracted and the initial **mucosotomy is closed** to restore luminal integrity.
- **Advantages:** dissection within the confined tunnel gives **scope stability and traction**; the mucosal flap minimizes extravasation of luminal contents; endoscopic closure of the mucosal flap is generally easier than closing a large post-exposed-EFTR defect.
- **Anatomic suitability:** most suitable for lesions reachable **in a straight line** — the **esophagus, gastric cardia, certain parts of the antrum, and rectum**.
- **Size limits:** STER is limited by tumor size; **SELs >3–4 cm can be difficult to resect en bloc through the submucosal tunnel and may often require conversion to exposed EFTR** for complete excision. One small study proposed **limiting lesion size to ≤30 mm in the axis perpendicular to the cranio-caudal length of the lesion on cross-sectional imaging**.
- **Outcomes — systematic review/meta-analysis (9 studies, 305 lesions):** pooled **en bloc resection 95%**, **complete resection 98%**; **air leakage (subcutaneous emphysema and pneumomediastinum) prevalence 15%**; **perforation rate 5.6%**.
- **STER vs surgery (single prospective RCT vs video-assisted thoracoscopic enucleation):** STER superior for lesions **<20 mm** in **postoperative pain, cost, procedure time, and blood loss**. However, **incomplete en bloc resection occurred in 29% of SELs >20 mm**.
- Additional high-quality evidence is needed to identify which SELs are most suitable for STER.

### Device-assisted EFTR ("close first, then cut")

- **Technique:** usually performed with an **over-the-scope clip or ligation device fitted to the tip of the endoscope**. The lesion is **pulled or suctioned into the cap**, the clip or ligator is **deployed**, and **resection above the clip** is then performed.
- **Variants described:** "**banding without resection**" techniques using an **autoamputation** strategy, and the "**suck, ligate, unroof, biopsy**" technique.
- **Main limitation:** the **size of the lesion**.

### FTRD (over-the-scope clip with integrated snare)

- **Device:** dedicated full-thickness resection device (**FTRD; Ovesco, Tübingen, Germany**), currently **approved for use in the colon and upper GI tract**. Consists of a **23-mm deep cap** with a specially designed over-the-scope clip and **integrated cautery snare**. The **inner diameter of the cap ranges from 12 to 13 mm** across available devices, **limiting the size of SELs that can be pulled into the cap and thus resected**.
- **Adverse events — German and Dutch colorectal FTRD registry, nearly 1900 patients:** overall AE rate **11.3%**; most common **bleeding 6.2%** and **perforation (immediate 1.4%, delayed 1.1%)**; **no adverse-event–related mortality**. **57% of perforations were due to technical or procedural issues**, and **64% of those cases required surgery.**
- **Maneuverability:** the **outer diameter of the FTRD distal cap is quite wide**; maneuvering it through the upper GI tract or colon can be challenging, if not prohibitive, in selected cases.
- **Size/depth thresholds:** the FTRD "appears to be an **effective and safe option for the management of neuroendocrine tumors <10 mm in size**." Conversely, "although the FTRD is generally accepted for **mucosal lesions ≤20 mm**, studies have found that resection of **SELs ≥15 mm in size may be associated with a higher risk of incomplete resection**."
- **Bulky SELs:** firm grasp of the lesion and retrieval into the cap may be hindered by **slippage of the lesion underneath the overlying mucosa, particularly for gastric SELs**. For **gastric SELs <15 mm**, studies report **technical success up to 93%** but a **complete (R0) resection rate of only 68%–76%**.
- **Adjuncts:** **unroofing of SELs before FTRD** has been reported, but these strategies have **not been well studied**.
- Bottom line: "the FTRD has become an essential part of the EFTR toolbox, given its relative ease of use and safety. However, **careful case selection is paramount to ensure completeness of resection**."

### Conclusion (near-verbatim)

- Advances in endoscopic techniques and devices are shifting SEL management from surgery to minimally invasive endoscopic approaches.
- "A thoughtful **individualized treatment plan** is essential for patients with SELs, taking into account **size, location, and histology** of the lesion (Table 1). The 'ideal' technique will depend on various patient and lesion characteristics, as well as the **endoscopist's preference and available expertise**."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No formal ratings.** As a Commentary CPU there are no numbered Best Practice Advice statements and no GRADE quality/strength ratings — weaker instrument than [[acg-2023-subepithelial-lesions|ACG 2023]] (GRADE) and than the [[asge-2017-subepithelial-lesions|ASGE 2017]] guideline. Where they conflict, ACG 2023 (guideline, GRADE, newer than ASGE 2017) governs; this CPU adds net-new technique detail rather than overriding.
- **STER size ceiling differs across sources.** This CPU: STER limited to ≤3 cm (Table 1), with >3–4 cm often requiring conversion to exposed EFTR, and ≤30 mm perpendicular-axis on cross-sectional imaging in one study. [[acg-2023-subepithelial-lesions|ACG 2023]] frames the limit as **>3.5 cm transverse diameter** being more technically challenging. [[asge-2017-subepithelial-lesions|ASGE 2017]] cites EFTR "up to ~4 cm." These are directionally consistent (~3–4 cm) but not identical numbers; the page states the CPU's ≤3 cm with the ACG figure flagged alongside.
- **STER tunnel start distance:** this CPU says the tunnel is initiated **3–5 cm proximal**; [[asge-2017-subepithelial-lesions|ASGE 2017]] says mucosal incision **≥5 cm proximal**. Newer CPU value used, older flagged.
- **FTRD evidence for SELs is thin** — "the literature on the use of the FTRD device for SELs is quite limited"; most data derive from colorectal *mucosal* lesion registries, so the 11.3% AE rate is extrapolated to SEL practice.
- **No head-to-head data** comparing exposed EFTR with STER or with device-assisted EFTR; the comparisons against surgery are retrospective and confounded by smaller, more intraluminal lesions in the endoscopic arms.
- **Adjunctive unroofing before FTRD** and long-term oncologic outcomes after endoscopic resection of SELs are explicitly named as unresolved.
