## Present and Future Recommendations (Verbatim)

Reproduced exactly as numbered in the source. **No grades, no strength labels, no evidence levels are attached to any of them.**

> **1.** We support the current recommendation of GI societies that screening endoscopy for Barrett's esophagus should be performed only in a well-defined, high-risk population.
>
> **2.** We do not recommend the use of any alternative test to screen for Barrett's esophagus at this time.
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;**a.** Some of the alternative tests show great promise for Barrett's screening and will likely find a place in clinical practice in the near future.
>
> **3.** In addition to refinement and validation of the alternative tests, there should be a complementary focus on using readily available demographic and clinical factors as well as noninvasive tools to further define an acceptable screening population for the next step in screening procedures.
>
> **4.** The stringency with which these populations can be defined will need to be balanced with the costs and risks of the potential screening tools proposed.

## Why the Current Screening Strategy Underperforms

- Population-based endoscopic screening **has not been recommended except in those with multiple risk factors** — high body mass index, male gender, white race, chronic reflux, family history.
- **Fewer than 10%** of patients with EAC have a prior diagnosis of Barrett's esophagus; **>90%** of patients diagnosed with EAC are not known to have Barrett's before their cancer diagnosis.
- **~40%** of patients with EAC describe **no antecedent history of GERD**, and **~50%** of patients with short-segment Barrett's — a lower-risk lesion — have no GERD symptoms whatsoever. A screening program limited to GERD symptoms "must miss a considerable percentage of high-risk patients."
- Among those with recurrent GERD symptoms, only a small proportion — **estimated at 10%** — undergo endoscopy in a given year.
- Benefit of surveillance is contested:
  - Systematic review/meta-analysis of **51 studies, 11,028 subjects**: pooled relative mortality risk of **0.386** for surveillance-detected vs non-surveillance-detected EAC → a **61% decrease** in EAC mortality risk attributable to prior surveillance.
  - **29,536** patients in the US National Veterans Affairs Database: EAC found in surveillance programs was significantly more likely to be early stage, with longer survival and lower cancer-related mortality.
  - Countervailing case–control study — **38** Barrett's patients who died of EAC vs **101** age- and sex-matched living Barrett's controls — found **nearly identical** frequencies of surveillance endoscopy within a 3-year period (**55% vs 60%**), suggesting surveillance as conducted in routine practice "provides little to no benefit in preventing cancer deaths."
- Downsides of endoscopic screening named: fiscal and psychological costs, possibility of adverse events, negative impact on quality of life, higher health and life insurance premiums, and identification of innocuous lesions that lead to potentially hazardous invasive treatments.

## The Alternative Techniques, One by One

### Demographic and Clinical Risk Models

- Existing models are **only modestly discriminating** — AUROCs **0.61 to 0.75** for predicting the presence of Barrett's or future EAC risk.
- Adding **central obesity, smoking history and increasing age** to a history of weekly GERD symptoms improved the net reclassification index by **up to 25%**.
- Thrift et al extended the model with **education level, body mass index, smoking status, frequency of GERD symptoms and/or acid-suppressant use, and frequency of NSAID use** → an absolute risk-assessment matrix for EAC in men with **AUROC 0.75 (95% CI 0.66–0.84)**.
- Adding **alarm symptoms (dysphagia, unexplained weight loss)** raised discrimination to **AUROC 0.85 (95% CI 0.78–0.91)** — and EAC risk was **>100-fold higher** in 50-year-old white men with long-standing GERD symptoms *with* alarm symptoms than without. **But once alarm symptoms are added, patients are beyond a curative stage.**
- "The clinical usefulness of these models has not been established" — developed primarily as tools for managing public health risks rather than as diagnostic instruments. They have nevertheless influenced GI societies' rejection of indiscriminate screening of all GERD patients, and highlighted the **exceptionally low risk of EAC for women and young men**.

### Transnasal Endoscopy (TNE)

- Thin, sheathed endoscopes; nasal passage causes far less gagging → **performed without sedation, in an ambulatory setting, with no requirement for sterilization** of the instrument between uses.
- Community comparison of standard sedated endoscopy vs TNE:

  | Endpoint | TNE | Standard sedated endoscopy |
  |---|---|---|
  | Participation/acceptance rate (outpatient endoscopy suite) | 45.7% | 40.7% (numerically higher for TNE) |
  | Complete evaluation of the esophagus | 96% | 100% |
  | Mean recovery time | **18.5 min** | **67.3 min** (P < .001) |
  | Successful biopsy acquisition | **83%** | **100%** (P = .001) |

  - The biopsy shortfall was **technical**: investigators could not pass the larger **5.8 mm** sheath that has a biopsy channel and instead used a **4.7 mm** sheath with **no biopsy channel**.
  - **~80%** of TNE subjects were willing to undergo the procedure again.
- Primary-care feasibility: of **426** participants enrolled across 2 primary care clinics, **422 (99%)** completed TNE, well tolerated by most; **38%** had an esophageal finding that changed management (**34% erosive esophagitis, 4% Barrett's esophagus**).
- Physician extenders can be trained: anatomic landmarks identified after **18 consecutive procedures**, satisfactory esophageal intubation after **35 procedures**. "Irrespective of who is performing the procedure, however, a high level of skill and frequent performance will be required."
- A newer ultrathin endoscope with improved optical quality was validated in a Japanese population, with optical resolution sufficient to apply **narrow band imaging** with accurate classification of the mucosal pattern using the **Goda criteria** and good correlation with histology.
- **Verdict:** "TNE using an instrument with biopsy capability seems to be a reasonable alternative to standard endoscopy as a screening tool." But **TNE cannot be recommended for endoscopic surveillance for dysplasia at this time** — optical quality of most available transnasal endoscopes is inferior to high-definition endoscopes, and optical chromoendoscopy features such as narrow band imaging are not available. TNE has not been widely embraced by primary care providers or gastroenterologists, and its availability is limited.

### Video Capsule Endoscopy

- Meta-analysis of capsule endoscopy for diagnosing Barrett's in patients with GERD — **9 studies, 618 patients**: pooled **sensitivity 77%, specificity 86%**.
  - Wide variation in specificity by reference standard: **90%** when endoscopically confirmed Barrett's was the reference vs **73%** when histologically confirmed; sensitivity remained the same (**78%**).
  - Most study populations combined Barrett's *screening* and *surveillance* patients.
- Two studies of capsule endoscopy as a **screening test** in **77** and **100** patients with GERD: sensitivities **78%** and **60%**; specificities **83%** and **100%**.
- Image quality: new grading scale (anatomic landmarks visualized, esophageal transit time, image quality, illumination, artifacts) applied to **502** patients; randomized blinded scoring by 2 interpreters gave concordance **75.9%–96.8%** across parameters — capsule endoscopy produces high-quality images.
- **Major disadvantages:** limited control of the device even when tethered; **inability to obtain biopsy specimens**; risk of **esophageal capsule retention in 0.7%–2.2%** of patients, usually owing to an unsuspected esophageal stricture.

### Tethered Capsule Endomicroscopy

- Swallowing an optomechanically engineered capsule that uses optical frequency domain imaging to provide **3-dimensional, near-microscopic images**.
- Proof-of-principle study: **7 healthy patients and 6 patients with Barrett's** — produced endomicroscopic images of esophageal mucosa that could distinguish patients with and without Barrett's; **12 of the subjects preferred** tethered capsule endomicroscopy over esophagogastroduodenoscopy.
- Capsule is withdrawn and can be disinfected for reuse, making it potentially inexpensive and feasible for population screening.
- **Requires considerable further investigation**; unclear whether its primary use will be screening or surveillance; large prospective studies in the relevant populations are required.

### Cytosponge–Trefoil Factor-3

**Device mechanics (verbatim detail):** an **ingestible gelatin capsule containing a compressed mesh sponge attached to a string**. The capsule is swallowed; once in the stomach the **gelatin dissolves within 5 minutes**, releasing a **spherical mesh sponge of 3-cm diameter**. The mesh is withdrawn through the mouth by traction on the string, scraping off esophageal cells that cling to the sponge. The resulting "pseudobiopsy" cytology specimen is analyzed for cellular markers or used for histologic interpretation; **trefoil factor-3 (TFF3), a cellular marker of intestinalization, is measured** to detect Barrett's metaplasia.

**Performance:**

| Study / setting | n | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **BEST2** case–control, 11 UK hospitals | 1110 (463 controls with dyspepsia and reflux symptoms; 647 patients with Barrett's) | Intention-to-treat sensitivity (including inadequate-yield samples) **79.9%**; **87.2%** for patients with **≥3 cm of circumferential Barrett's**; **89.7% (95% CI 82.3%–94.8%)** for patients undergoing a **repeat** procedure. Highly favorable patient rating, no serious adverse events |
| Primary-care cohort of GERD patients on acid suppressants | >500 | Procedure safe; **99%** successfully swallowed the device |
| Commercial Cytosponge (Medtronic, Dublin, Ireland) + TFF3, small prospective study | 73 | Overall sensitivity **91.5%** — comparable to BEST2 **because the population was enriched and inadequate samples were excluded** |
| Acceptability | — | **82%** of participants reported low levels of anxiety before the test; Cytosponge rated favorably compared with endoscopy (**P < .001**); focus groups found it more comfortable and practical than endoscopy |
| BEST2 risk stratification | 820 patients with Barrett's | Risk-stratification biomarker panel tested to discriminate patients with and without dysplasia; recent studies have evaluated a **methylation panel** and a **multigene next-generation sequencing panel** |

- The Cytosponge-TFF3 test is **now being evaluated in a multicenter, cluster randomized trial of 9000 patients in primary care (ISRCTN68382401)** — main outcome measures are number of Barrett's cases detected, cost, and acceptability of Cytosponge compared with standard care.
- The copious cell sample is suitable for a variety of biomarker assays, with potential use in **surveillance** as well as screening.

### Liquid Biopsies

- Blood test detecting circulating abnormal cells or their components (DNA, microRNA) — "the challenge has been to develop assays of sufficient sensitivity and specificity for clinical use."
- miRNAs are small noncoding RNA molecules of approximately **21–25 nucleotides**.
- Systematic review identified **5 miRNAs (miRNA-192, -194, -203, -205, and -215)** as promising **tissue-derived** markers for diagnosing Barrett's.
- Comparing Barrett's with esophagitis: **miRNA-194, -215, and -143** significantly increased in Barrett's — **not further validated in a larger population**.
- Circulating **miRNA-194-5p and miRNA-451a significantly increased**, **miRNA-136-5p significantly decreased**, in Barrett's vs controls. In a validation study of **41 patients with Barrett's and 15 controls**, a combination of **4 circulating miRNAs (miRNA-95-3p, -136-5p, -194-5p, -451a)** distinguished Barrett's from controls with **sensitivity 78% and specificity 86%**.
- **"These promising findings require validation in larger cohorts before they can be recommended for clinical use."**
- **Circulating autoantibodies** for early detection of esophageal cancer: positive associations reported, but **test sensitivities were too low and variability between studies too high to recommend their clinical application**.

### Serum Adipokines

- Rationale: visceral fat of patients with central obesity is metabolically active, producing adipokines predictive of and mechanistically related to Barrett's and adenocarcinoma.
- Systematic review/meta-analysis of **9 studies including 1432 patients with Barrett's**: elevated serum **leptin and insulin** associated with increased risk for Barrett's; data on **low serum adiponectin were contradictory**.
- Data are **not sufficiently robust to recommend adipokine measurements alone** for Barrett's screening; investigators have used them as an **adjunct to demographic factors** — higher **interleukin-8 and leptin** and lower **interleukin-10** were associated with the presence of Barrett's in groups with high-risk demographic factors.

### Electronic Nose Breath Testing

- Exhaled volatile organic compounds — products of human and gut bacterial metabolism — profiled by an "electronic nose."
- One study: altered profile of exhaled volatile organic compounds distinguished esophageal and gastric adenocarcinomas from Barrett's metaplasia and other benign upper GI disorders with **AUROCs of up to 0.98**.
- Study of **66 patients with Barrett's and 56 patients without**: groups distinguished with **82% sensitivity, 80% specificity, 81% accuracy, AUROC 0.79**. **Patient enrollment rate 95%**, suggesting high acceptability.
- "This attractive technology requires large prospective studies in a primary care setting to evaluate its clinical usefulness."

## Cost Effectiveness

- The **present cost of endoscopic screening for Barrett's is high**; newer techniques must be judged on overall cost relative to the population studied — **high-cost tools may only be cost effective in a demographic population with clear risk factors**, whereas low-cost devices could be applied to broader populations.
- **Capsule endoscopy vs standard endoscopy** (Markov model, 50-year-old white men with GERD symptoms): costs **very similar** — the saving from performing capsule endoscopy without sedation was **negated by the high cost of the capsule and its accompanying equipment**. "Despite its attractiveness for screening because it is safe and does not require sedation, its costs currently seem prohibitive."
- **Cytosponge microsimulation** (screening 60-year-old men with GERD symptoms, with endoscopic confirmation of positive Cytosponge results; models included the consequences of findings of high-grade dysplasia and cancer):

  | Comparison | Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio |
  |---|---|
  | **Cytosponge** screening vs **no screening** | **$26,358 – $33,307** |
  | **Endoscopic** screening vs **Cytosponge** | **$107,583 – $330,361** |

  - Cytosponge yields greater benefit than no screening at a cost most societies would be willing to pay (**<$50,000 per quality-adjusted life-year gained**); the benefit of **endoscopic screening over Cytosponge could only be achieved at costs greater than those societal limits**.
  - **Major limitation:** uncertainty regarding the cost of the Cytosponge, because the device was not commercially available.
- **Unsedated TNE** — short-term direct, indirect and total costs of screening were **3 to 4 times lower** than standard, sedated endoscopy when calculated for the community setting.
- **Cost analyses for the other screening techniques have not been comparatively studied.**

## Time to Rethink Studies on Screening Tools

- The core arithmetic of the GERD-symptom entry criterion:
  - Crude incidence rates in persons with and without chronic GERD symptoms yield a **relative risk for EAC of about 6**.
  - **40% of EAC cases (4000 per year)** occur among persons **without** chronic GERD symptoms (heartburn and/or regurgitation once per week or more). Capturing them "would involve screening approximately **80% of the adult US population (120 million persons)**."
  - By contrast, **enriching the population so that screening the 10%–20% with reflux symptoms would detect >50% of cancers**. Screening all adults with and without symptoms would render endoscopic screening impractical unless other risk factors could enrich the population.
- The conditional logic the commentary sets out:
  - **If** physician and patient concerns regarding the cost, risk, or inconvenience of endoscopy are the primary factors limiting Barrett's screening from decreasing EAC deaths, **then** less invasive, less expensive alternatives have the potential to improve screening outcomes while decreasing costs.
  - Alternative tests would **enrich the patient population most likely to benefit from endoscopy**; endoscopy would then be required only for the minority who test positive.
  - **However**, "the cost saving for society could be reduced, eliminated, or even reversed if the test is used in an expanded population in which many individuals will have a positive result, and thus require endoscopy."
- The unresolved surveillance question: for patients in whom **prevalent neoplasia is not present**, definitive proof is lacking that surveillance results in fewer cancer deaths — and if it does not, any surveillance procedure must be cost ineffective. Hence "GI societies support endoscopic screening and surveillance tenuously."
- **Harm of overdiagnosis is named explicitly:** identifying asymptomatic patients with Barrett's can **cause psychological stress, result in higher premiums for health and life insurance, and might identify innocuous lesions that lead to potentially hazardous invasive treatments**.
- Proposed direction: improved methods of **choosing** patients at increased risk must go hand in hand with development of novel screening tools. **Tissue biomarkers** procured at endoscopy predict greater chances of incident neoplasia — **p53 mutation and abnormal immunostaining have been demonstrated as a marker of disease progression in Barrett's, although the sensitivity is too low for use as a single biomarker**; marker analysis has recently been applied to **Cytosponge mucosal sampling**.
- A standing assumption is flagged as possibly wrong: "it has been assumed that most if not all EAC arises in a field of Barrett's metaplasia. **If this proves untrue, or if small foci of intestinal metaplasia are sufficient for cancer development without a visible endoscopic Barrett's segment that meets the criteria for surveillance, then the future strategies of screening and surveillance may also change.**"
- Closing balance: "although these vexing uncertainties must temper enthusiasm for the unqualified endorsement of any screening test for Barrett's esophagus, the alternative of making no attempt to stem the rapidly rising incidence of a lethal malignancy also is unpalatable."

## Summary

This AGA Clinical Practice Update Commentary asks whether the newer, less invasive tools for detecting Barrett's esophagus are ready to replace screening endoscopy, and answers no — **not yet, and not for any of them.** The authors first dismantle the current strategy: fewer than 10% of patients with EAC carry a prior Barrett's diagnosis, ~40% have never had GERD symptoms, only ~10% of symptomatic GERD patients get endoscoped in a given year, and the surveillance literature is split between a meta-analysis showing a 61% relative reduction in EAC mortality and a case–control study showing nearly identical endoscopy frequencies in Barrett's patients who did and did not die of EAC.

They then walk through eight alternatives — demographic/clinical risk models, transnasal endoscopy, video capsule endoscopy, tethered capsule endomicroscopy, Cytosponge-TFF3, liquid biopsy (circulating miRNA, autoantibodies), serum adipokines, and electronic nose breath testing — and summarize them in a single qualitative grid (Table 1) scoring convenience, screening accuracy, surveillance accuracy, cost, transportability and acceptance. Two come out ahead for different reasons: **TNE with a biopsy-capable instrument is called "a reasonable alternative to standard endoscopy as a screening tool"** (but explicitly not for dysplasia surveillance, because optical quality and chromoendoscopy are inferior), and **Cytosponge-TFF3 has the strongest efficacy, acceptability and cost-effectiveness dataset** (BEST2 sensitivity 79.9%, rising to 87.2% for ≥3 cm circumferential Barrett's and 89.7% on a repeat procedure; ICER $26,358–$33,307/QALY vs no screening, against $107,583–$330,361 for endoscopy vs Cytosponge). The rest — capsule endoscopy (no biopsy capability, 0.7%–2.2% retention, cost saving negated by device cost), tethered capsule endomicroscopy, miRNA panels, adipokines, and breath testing — are framed as promising but unvalidated.

The economic argument is the pivot. Cheaper tests only save money if they are applied to an *enriched* population; deployed broadly, the positives they generate all funnel back into endoscopy and the saving can be "reduced, eliminated, or even reversed." That is why recommendation 3 pairs test refinement with a complementary push to define the screening population using demographic and clinical factors — existing risk models reach only AUROC 0.61–0.75, rising to 0.85 only once alarm symptoms are added, by which point patients are beyond cure.

The document is a **Commentary and carries no Best Practice Advice and no evidence grades**; its formal output is four numbered recommendations, the second of which is a flat statement that **no alternative test should be used to screen for Barrett's esophagus at this time**, immediately qualified by 2a — several will likely find a place in clinical practice in the near future.

## Key Findings / Claims

- **No alternative (nonendoscopic or alternative-endoscopic) test is recommended for Barrett's screening as of 2018** (Rec 2); screening endoscopy should still be confined to a well-defined, high-risk population (Rec 1).
- **Case-finding failure quantified:** <10% of EAC patients have a prior Barrett's diagnosis; >90% are unknown to have Barrett's at cancer diagnosis; ~40% of EAC patients have no antecedent GERD; ~50% of short-segment Barrett's patients have no GERD symptoms; only ~10% of patients with recurrent GERD symptoms undergo endoscopy in a given year.
- **Surveillance benefit is contested within the same document:** meta-analysis (51 studies, 11,028 subjects) relative mortality 0.386 (61% reduction) and a 29,536-patient VA cohort favoring surveillance, against a 38-case/101-control study finding 55% vs 60% surveillance-endoscopy frequency — i.e. no difference.
- **Risk models are modest:** AUROC 0.61–0.75; adding central obesity/smoking/age to weekly GERD improved net reclassification by up to 25%; Thrift model AUROC 0.75 (0.66–0.84), rising to 0.85 (0.78–0.91) with alarm symptoms — but alarm symptoms mark incurable disease. EAC risk >100-fold higher in 50-year-old white men with long-standing GERD plus alarm symptoms.
- **TNE:** recovery 18.5 vs 67.3 min (P<.001); acceptance 45.7% vs 40.7%; complete esophageal evaluation 96% vs 100%; successful biopsy 83% vs 100% (P=.001) — the shortfall caused by using a 4.7 mm sheath without a biopsy channel when the 5.8 mm biopsy-capable sheath could not be passed; ~80% would repeat. 422/426 (99%) completed TNE in primary care with 38% management-changing findings (34% erosive esophagitis, 4% Barrett's). Training: landmarks at 18 procedures, intubation at 35. **Reasonable screening alternative; not recommended for dysplasia surveillance.**
- **Video capsule endoscopy:** pooled sensitivity 77%, specificity 86% (9 studies, 618 patients); specificity 90% vs endoscopic reference and 73% vs histologic reference; dedicated screening studies sensitivity 78%/60%, specificity 83%/100%; **esophageal capsule retention 0.7%–2.2%**, usually from an unsuspected stricture; **no biopsy capability**.
- **Cytosponge-TFF3:** gelatin capsule dissolving in ~5 min to release a 3-cm mesh sponge on a string. BEST2 (1110 subjects; 463 controls, 647 Barrett's) ITT sensitivity **79.9%**, **87.2%** with ≥3 cm circumferential Barrett's, **89.7% (95% CI 82.3–94.8)** on repeat procedure; >500-patient primary-care cohort 99% swallowed it safely; commercial device + TFF3 91.5% sensitivity in an enriched, inadequate-sample-excluded population of 73; 82% reported low pre-test anxiety; rated favorably vs endoscopy (P<.001). Under evaluation in a 9000-patient primary-care cluster RCT (ISRCTN68382401).
- **Cost effectiveness:** Cytosponge ICER **$26,358–$33,307**/QALY vs no screening; endoscopy **vs Cytosponge** **$107,583–$330,361**; societal willingness-to-pay threshold cited as **<$50,000/QALY**. Unsedated TNE costs **3–4× lower** than sedated endoscopy in the community. Capsule endoscopy's sedation-free saving is **negated by device cost**.
- **Liquid biopsy:** 4-miRNA panel (miRNA-95-3p, -136-5p, -194-5p, -451a) sensitivity 78%/specificity 86% in 41 Barrett's vs 15 controls — requires validation in larger cohorts; circulating autoantibodies not recommended (sensitivity too low, between-study variability too high).
- **Serum adipokines:** elevated leptin and insulin associated with Barrett's risk (9 studies, 1432 patients); adiponectin data contradictory; **not robust enough to recommend alone** — adjunct to demographics only (IL-8, leptin high; IL-10 low).
- **Electronic nose:** 66 Barrett's vs 56 non-Barrett's — 82% sensitivity, 80% specificity, 81% accuracy, AUROC 0.79; AUROC up to 0.98 for distinguishing adenocarcinoma from metaplasia/benign disease; 95% enrollment rate; needs large primary-care prospective studies.
- **Overdiagnosis harms are stated:** psychological stress, higher health/life insurance premiums, and identification of innocuous lesions leading to potentially hazardous invasive treatment.
- **Cheap tests do not automatically save money:** applying a low-cost test to an expanded population generates positives that all require endoscopy, so the societal saving "could be reduced, eliminated, or even reversed."
- **p53 mutation/abnormal immunostaining** is a marker of disease progression but **sensitivity is too low for use as a single biomarker**; marker analysis has been applied to Cytosponge mucosal sampling.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Direct contradiction with [[acg-2022-barretts]] on nonendoscopic screening.** AGA 2018 Rec 2: "We do not recommend the use of any alternative test to screen for Barrett's esophagus at this time." ACG 2022 endorses a nonendoscopic capsule sponge device plus a biomarker (TFF3 or methylated DNA) as an **acceptable alternative screening modality** (conditional, very low quality). **ACG 2022 governs** — same tier, newer publication date — and Rec 2a of this commentary already anticipated the reversal ("will likely find a place in clinical practice in the near future"). The 2018 statement should be recorded as superseded history, not as current practice.
- **Internal tension on surveillance benefit, left unresolved by the source.** A 51-study/11,028-subject meta-analysis (relative mortality 0.386) and a 29,536-patient VA cohort support surveillance; a 38-case/101-control study finds 55% vs 60% surveillance frequency in Barrett's patients who died of EAC vs those who did not. The authors note observational studies are "susceptible to biases likely to exaggerate the benefits" and call the case–control study "controversial," but adjudicate neither. (The wiki's current position on this question rests on the later [[aga-2025-barretts-surveillance|AGA 2025]] guideline and the BOSS RCT, neither of which existed in 2018.)
- **Cytosponge sensitivity figures are not directly comparable to the ACG 2022 numbers on [[barretts-esophagus]].** This source reports BEST2 **intention-to-treat sensitivity 79.9%** (inadequate-yield samples included) and **91.5%** for the commercial device in a **73-patient enriched population with inadequate samples excluded** — and states explicitly that the higher figure reflects the enrichment and exclusion, not better performance. Any use of a Cytosponge sensitivity number must carry its qualifier.
- **No numbered Best Practice Advice, no grades.** Anything on an entity page attributed to this source must be described as a 2018 AGA Clinical Practice Update *Commentary* position, ungraded, and must not be presented alongside GRADE-rated ACG/AGA/ASGE statements as though equivalently rated.
- **Open question raised by the authors:** whether EAC always arises in a field of Barrett's metaplasia. If small foci of intestinal metaplasia suffice for cancer without a visible endoscopic segment meeting surveillance criteria, "the future strategies of screening and surveillance may also change."
- **Open question — surveillance accuracy of every alternative.** Table 1 scores liquid biopsy and electronic nose as `?` for both screening and surveillance accuracy, and rates every nonendoscopic tool `+` for surveillance. No alternative test has a surveillance role in this document.
- **Cost figures carry a stated caveat:** the Cytosponge microsimulation was run when the device was not commercially available, so "there was uncertainty regarding the cost of the Cytosponge." Cost analyses for the other techniques "have not been comparatively studied."
- **Conflicts of interest disclosed in the source:** David A. Katzka has performed a pharmaceutical trial with Shire; Rebecca C. Fitzgerald holds patents on the Cytosponge technology, licensed by MRC Technology to Covidien GI Solutions (now Medtronic), with no direct financial arrangement with Medtronic.
