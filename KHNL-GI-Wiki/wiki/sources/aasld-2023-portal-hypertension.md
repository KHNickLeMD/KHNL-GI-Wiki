## Summary

Guidance on identifying [[portal-hypertension|portal hypertension]] (PH), preventing initial hepatic decompensation, managing acute variceal hemorrhage (AVH), and reducing recurrent variceal hemorrhage in chronic liver disease.

**Three major shifts (Box 1 — "What's new"):**

1. Recognition of **compensated advanced chronic liver disease (cACLD)** — a shift away from requiring a histological or radiological diagnosis of cirrhosis for initial risk stratification.
2. Codification of methodology to use **noninvasive assessments** (liver stiffness measurement [LSM] by transient elastography [TE] + platelet count) to identify **clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH)**.
3. Endorsement of a paradigm change — **early nonselective beta-blocker (NSBB) therapy once CSPH is identified**, to decrease the risk of decompensation.

Other new content: preemptive TIPS in selected AVH subsets; updated blood-product guidance during resuscitation; guidance on [[upper-endoscopy|upper endoscopy]] before transesophageal echocardiography (TEE); cardiofundal and ectopic varices; portal hypertensive gastropathy (PHG); and PH in [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|HCC]], pregnancy, and the preoperative setting.

**Recompensation** is clinically defined (after elimination/control of the underlying etiology) as resolution of ascites and/or hepatic encephalopathy no longer requiring specific therapy, in the absence of recurrent variceal hemorrhage for over 12 months, together with stable improvement of liver function tests (albumin, INR, bilirubin).

## Guidance Statements (complete, 1–58)

Reproduced near-verbatim. The source assigns **no evidence grade or strength** to any statement.

1. Carvedilol is recommended as the preferred NSBB for the treatment of PH in patients with cirrhosis.
2. The recommended maintenance dosage of carvedilol is 6.25–12.5 mg/day. Maintenance dosage can be given as a single daily dose or divided twice daily. In patients with concomitant arterial hypertension or cardiac disease, the dose of carvedilol may be further increased to address nonhepatic indications.
3. Hepatic Venous Pressure Gradient (HVPG) measurement is the gold-standard method to assess portal pressure in patients with cirrhosis.
4. Clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH) is defined as HVPG ≥10 mm Hg.
5. HVPG may underestimate portal pressure in some patients with obesity and NASH-related cirrhosis.
6. The presence of clinical decompensation, of gastroesophageal varices on endoscopy, or portosystemic collaterals or hepatofugal flow on imaging is sufficient to diagnose CSPH.
7. CSPH can be noninvasively identified by LSM by vibration-controlled TE (or non-TE approaches when validated cutoffs exist) and platelet count. CSPH is diagnosed at LSM ≥25 kPa irrespective of platelet count, LSM 20–24.9 kPa with platelet count <150 K/mm³, or LSM 15–19.9 kPa with platelet count <110 K/mm³.
8. Annual LSM by TE (or non-TE approaches when validated cutoffs exist) and serum platelet counts may provide prognostic information in patients with cACLD without baseline CSPH in whom the underlying etiologies of cirrhosis remain active/uncontrolled.
9. Use of NSBBs in patients with cirrhosis without CSPH is not recommended for prevention of decompensation.
10. Lifestyle modification and treatment of underlying liver disease should be prioritized to prevent progression to CSPH and decompensation.
11. In patients with compensated cirrhosis and CSPH, the goal of therapy is to prevent the development of clinical decompensation.
12. NSBBs (preferably carvedilol 12.5 mg/day) should be considered for patients with cACLD with CSPH to prevent decompensation.
13. NSBBs should not be administered to patients with cACLD and evidence of CSPH with asthma, advanced heart block, and bradyarrhythmias, and caution should be used in patients with relative contraindications (Box 2). *(The statement cites "Box 2," but the contraindication list is **Box 3** — see the table below; Box 2 is the "ABCs of HVPG measurement." Cross-reference error in the source.)*
14. Patients with cACLD and evidence of CSPH (by endoscopy, TE, HVPG or imaging) who are candidates for NSBB should be considered for treatment with NSBB (in the absence of contraindications) to prevent hepatic decompensation, which would also obviate the need for further screening endoscopy.
15. Where TE is not available to diagnose CSPH, when empiric NSBB are contraindicated or not considered due to prior intolerance, endoscopic surveillance of all patients with cirrhosis is recommended. Patients with cACLD without varices on screening endoscopy should have endoscopy repeated every 2 years (with ongoing liver injury or associated conditions, such as obesity and alcohol use) or every 3 years (if liver injury is quiescent, e.g., after viral elimination, alcohol abstinence). Patients with cACLD without varices who develop decompensation should have a repeat endoscopy when this occurs. The presence of varices of any size should prompt initiation of NSBB (in absence of contraindication).
16. Where TE is not available, screening endoscopy is not necessary in patients on non-selective beta-blocker therapy; the need for screening endoscopy can be also obviated in some patients on a selective beta-blocker by switching therapy to a non-selective beta-blocker after discussion with the prescribing clinician.
17. Patients with compensated cirrhosis and CSPH without varices who have contraindications or intolerance to beta-blockers should be screened for varices needing treatment with surveillance endoscopy every 2 years when the underlying disease remains uncontrolled and every 3 years when controlled.
18. Patients with compensated cirrhosis and CSPH with varices that have not bled who have contraindications or intolerance to beta-blockers should be screened for varices needing treatment with surveillance endoscopy every 1 year when the underlying disease remains uncontrolled and every 2 years when controlled.
19. Primary prophylaxis with EVL should be performed in patients with cACLD and CSPH and high-risk varices that cannot receive NSBBs.
20. Band ligation should be repeated every 2–4 weeks until obliteration and then endoscopy repeated at 6 months and then every 12 months to assess for reappearance of varices requiring additional treatment.
21. TIPS should not be used for the prevention of decompensation of cirrhosis or as primary prophylaxis for [[variceal-upper-gi-bleeding|variceal hemorrhage]].
22. Patients with decompensated cirrhosis not taking NSBBs who have never bled from varices should undergo annual endoscopic screening.
23. If high-risk varices are detected, NSBBs or endoscopic band ligation are recommended; preference is given to NSBBs (including carvedilol) because of benefits beyond prevention of variceal hemorrhage. (If endoscopic band ligation is chosen, refer to recommendation 19).
24. NSBBs should be dose reduced or discontinued in patients who develop persistently low systolic arterial pressure <90 mm Hg or severe adverse effects. NSBB discontinuation should prompt endoscopic evaluation for presence of high-risk varices requiring band ligation.
25. All patients with known or suspected cirrhosis presenting with acute gastrointestinal bleeding should be initiated on vasoactive therapy (e.g., somatostatin, octreotide or terlipressin if available; see Table 5) and intravenous antibacterial therapy as soon as possible.
26. If portal hypertensive bleeding is confirmed at endoscopy, vasoactive therapy should be continued for 2–5 days.
27. Intravenous antibacterial treatment should be tailored to local resistance patterns and patient allergies. The most commonly used agent is ceftriaxone 1 g/24 hours up to 5 days. Antimicrobial therapy can be discontinued once bleeding is controlled and in absence of an active infection.
28. Packed red blood cell transfusions should target a hemoglobin ~7 g/dL unless higher targets required related to comorbid conditions.
29. Fresh frozen plasma and platelet transfusions should not be administered based on international normalized ratio or platelet count targets, respectively, because there is no evidence of benefit of such transfusions in AVH, and in the case of fresh frozen plasma, there is evidence of potential harm.
30. Upper endoscopy should be performed within 12 hours of presentation with AVH.
31. If esophageal variceal bleeding is confirmed, EVL should be performed.
32. In patients with CTP class B score >7 and active bleeding on endoscopy or CTP class C score 10–13, preemptive TIPS creation (within 72 hours and ideally within 24 hours of initial upper endoscopy) should be recommended in absence of absolute contraindications to TIPS. If TIPS is not locally available, transfer to a center with the capacity to intervene should be considered.
33. In patients presenting with AVH who do not undergo TIPS, NSBB should be initiated at discontinuation of vasoactive therapy.
34. Covered expandable esophageal stents (where available) or balloon tamponade should be considered in patients with uncontrolled AVH as a bridge to TIPS.
35. TIPS should be considered in patients with uncontrolled AVH ("salvage" TIPS) or who rebleed despite vasoactive therapy and EVL ("rescue" TIPS).
36. Enteral feeding should be started once AVH episode has been controlled. The presence of variceal bands does not contraindicate placement of a feeding tube if indicated.
37. Proton pump inhibitors should be discontinued once AVH has been confirmed as the bleeding source in the absence of other specific indications.
38. Patients with variceal bleeding who do not fulfill the criteria for a preemptive TIPS and/or do not undergo TIPS during admission should undergo secondary prophylaxis with NSBB and endoscopic band ligation.
39. Use of TIPS for secondary prophylaxis can be considered in patients with additional indications for TIPS (e.g., refractory [[ascites]]).
40. Patients with gastric or ectopic varices have CSPH and therefore the use of NSBBs should be considered for prevention of rebleeding and decompensation. These patients should be investigated for the presence of [[portal-vein-thrombosis|portal vein thrombosis]].
41. Patients with high-risk cardiofundal (GOV2 or IGV1) varices (≥10 mm, red wale signs, CTP class B/C) who have contraindications or intolerance to NSBBs may be considered for primary prophylaxis with endoscopic cyanoacrylate injection (ECI).
42. Neither TIPS nor BRTO (or related obliterative techniques) are recommended to prevent first hemorrhage in patients with fundal varices that have not bled.
43. Initial management of bleeding gastric or ectopic varices should be identical to the management of bleeding esophageal varices, including vasoactive therapy, antimicrobials, conservative transfusion strategy, and endoscopic evaluation, within 12 hours.
44. Patients with bleeding gastric or ectopic varices should have contrast-enhanced cross-sectional imaging to define the anatomy of portosystemic collaterals or presence of venous thrombosis that would guide therapy.
45. In patients with acute hemorrhage from gastric (GOV2/IGV1) or ectopic varices, either endoscopic cyanoacrylate therapy, TIPS, or retrograde transvenous variceal embolization/obliteration can be considered first-line options. Retrograde obliteration is preferred when TIPS is contraindicated.
46. In patients who underwent ECI as the main therapy, the addition of NSBBs is recommended to prevent rebleeding, in absence of contraindications. Additionally, repeat endoscopic treatment at intervals every 2–4 weeks until obliteration and long-term surveillance should be performed.
47. Patients with bleeding GV caused by isolated splenic vein thrombosis should be evaluated for splenectomy, splenic vein stenting, or splenic artery embolization.
48. Patients with greater than mild PHG should be presumed to have CSPH and should therefore be considered for prophylactic NSBB to prevent decompensation; this intervention may also prevent hemorrhagic complications or iron-deficiency anemia from severe PHG.
49. In acute bleeding from severe PHG, vasoactive therapy (e.g., somatostatin, somatostatin analogs such as octreotide, or terlipressin if available; see Table 5) for 2–5 days at doses used for variceal bleeding should be considered.
50. NSBB are recommended to prevent rebleeding from PHG and PH-related polyps.
51. If bleeding from PHG becomes transfusion-dependent despite NSBB, TIPS placement should be considered.
52. Prevention and treatment of AVH and hepatic decompensation in patients with HCC should follow the same principles as those for patients without HCC.
53. In the absence of contraindications, NSBB therapy is recommended for the primary prophylaxis for VH and prevention of decompensation in patients with HCC with CSPH (including varices).
54. In the presence of occlusive bland or malignant PVT, upper endoscopy is recommended to investigate the presence of gastroesophageal varices. If varices are detected, NSBB or endoscopic band ligation are recommended; preference is given to NSBB (including carvedilol) because of benefits beyond prevention of variceal hemorrhage.
55. All patients with cirrhosis or noncirrhotic PH planning pregnancy should undergo upper endoscopy within 1 year of conception.
56. Unscreened pregnant patients with cirrhosis or noncirrhotic PH should undergo EGD early in the second trimester.
57. Routine upper endoscopy prior to TEE in patients with cirrhosis is not recommended.
58. Preoperative TIPS can be considered on a case-by-case basis after careful consideration of potential surgical benefits relative to potential harms related to the procedure (encephalopathy, worsening of liver failure).

## Key Findings / Claims

### Classification of portal hypertension (Table 1)

| Class | Level | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Prehepatic | Portal vein and branches | [[portal-vein-thrombosis\|Portal vein thrombosis]] |
| Intrahepatic — presinusoidal | Portal triads | Schistosomiasis, [[primary-biliary-cholangitis\|primary biliary cholangitis]], sarcoidosis, [[porto-sinusoidal-vascular-disorder\|portosinusoidal vascular disorder]] |
| Intrahepatic — sinusoidal | Sinusoids | [[cirrhosis]] (all causes), alcohol-associated hepatitis |
| Intrahepatic — postsinusoidal | Central veins | Sinusoidal obstruction syndrome |
| Posthepatic | Hepatic veins, IVC | [[budd-chiari-syndrome\|Budd-Chiari syndrome]], congestive hepatopathy (pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, constrictive pericarditis, others) |

### Noninvasive staging — cACLD and the "Rule of Five" (Figures 1B and 3)

| Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| LSM by TE **<10 kPa** | **Rules out cACLD** |
| LSM by TE **≥15 kPa** | **Rules in cACLD** |
| LSM **<15 kPa AND platelets >150 K/mm³** | **CSPH excluded** |
| LSM **15–19.9 kPa AND platelets <110 K/mm³** | CSPH presumed |
| LSM **20–24.9 kPa AND platelets <150 K/mm³** | CSPH presumed |
| LSM **≥25 kPa** (irrespective of platelet count; in non-obese patients) | **CSPH ruled in** |

- Positive predictive value **>90%** for LSM ≥25 kPa; **>60%** for the LSM + platelet combinations. Validated for HBV, HCV, alcohol-associated liver disease, and **lean** NAFLD (BMI <30 kg/m²) via the ANTICIPATE model.
- **Caution applying the Rule of Five** in obesity and NAFLD/NASH (PPV of the 25-kPa cutoff falls to **63%**), ALT >3× ULN, and PSC with dominant stricture — all calibrate poorly.
- Many patients fall in an unclassified **"gray zone."** LSM must not drive decisions without confirmed high study quality and adequately trained operators.
- Platelet count alone or enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) are insufficient to exclude CSPH and skip variceal screening.
- **Spleen stiffness:** liver stiffness–spleen size–to–platelet ratio >2.65 corresponds to CSPH risk >80%; SSM ≤46 kPa is an alternative rule-out. **Baveno VI criteria** for skipping variceal screening = LSM <20 kPa **and** platelets >150 K/mm³.
- Non-TE cutoffs are not solidly validated: MRE <3.5 kPa generally rules out cACLD, ≥5.0 kPa rules in.

### High-risk varices — the definition behind GS 19, 23, and 24

**High-risk varices = moderate/large varices, OR varices of any size with red wale marks, OR varices of any size in a CTP class C patient.** Increasing CTP class, variceal size, and red wale marks each raise first-bleed risk.

- If the high-risk varices are **small**, NSBB is the only technically feasible option.
- If **large**, both NSBB and EVL are possible.

### Nonselective beta-blockers in PH (Table 3)

| Drug | Mechanism | Starting dose | Titration | Maximal dose | Goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Propranolol | ↓cardiac output (β-1 blockade: ↓HR and contractility) **plus** splanchnic arterial vasoconstriction (β-2 blockade → unopposed α-adrenergic vasoconstriction) | 20–40 mg **twice daily** | Increase the dose every 2–3 days until treatment goal | Without ascites **320 mg/day**; with ascites **160 mg/day** | HR **55–60 bpm** if tolerated; SBP maintained **≥90 mm Hg** |
| Nadolol | As above | 20–40 mg **at bedtime** | As above | Without ascites **160 mg/day**; with ascites **80 mg/day** | HR **55–60 bpm** if tolerated; SBP **≥90 mm Hg** |
| Carvedilol | Above **plus** ↓intrahepatic vascular resistance via anti-α-1-adrenergic activity and nitric oxide release | **6.25 mg once daily** | Increase to 6.25 mg twice daily after 3 days (body text: increase to 12.5 mg/day after **2–3 days**, as a single dose or divided) | **12.5 mg/day**; higher doses may be considered for nonhepatic indications — **up to 25 mg/day** for blood pressure control | **No HR goal**; SBP maintained **≥90 mm Hg** |

- Common adverse effects (all three): fatigue, bradycardia, dyspnea, orthostasis, hypotension, constipation.
- **Maintenance:** indefinitely, or until TIPS or liver transplant. No indication for routine upper endoscopy while on therapy.
- **Down-titrate carvedilol to 6.25 mg daily** if not tolerated or if SBP falls <90 mm Hg in compensated cirrhosis. **Lower starting doses may be more appropriate in CTP class B and C.**
- If arterial pressure is low even on low-dose carvedilol, consider switching to propranolol or nadolol — they have lesser effects on arterial pressure.
- About one third of patients with compensated cirrhosis have arterial hypertension.

### Lifestyle and etiologic therapy (Table 2) — the content behind GS 10

- **Etiological therapy:** antiviral therapy (HCV, HBV), immunosuppression (AIH), alcohol abstinence and relapse-prevention therapy.
- **Healthy lifestyle:** alcohol elimination; regular moderate aerobic exercise; body weight at **BMI 18–29 kg/m²**; adequate protein intake **>1 g/kg per day**; avoidance of processed foods, sugar and high-fructose-corn-syrup–sweetened products, and salty foods; tobacco avoidance; **high-protein nocturnal snack**.

### Vasoactive agents for acute variceal bleeding (Table 5)

| Agent | Dosing | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Octreotide | Initial IV bolus **50 mcg**, then infusion **25–50 mcg/hour** | 2–5 days |
| Somatostatin | Initial IV bolus **250 mcg**, then infusion **250–500 mcg/hour** | 2–5 days |
| Terlipressin* | Initial 24–48 hours **2 mg IV every 4–6 hours**, then **1 mg IV every 4–6 hours** | 2–5 days |

*Not approved for this indication in North America.

### Preemptive TIPS and secondary prophylaxis

- **Preemptive TIPS (GS 32):** CTP class **B score >7 with active bleeding at initial endoscopy**, OR CTP class **C score 10–13**. Within 72 hours, ideally within 24 hours. Absolute contraindications to TIPS apply. Transfer if TIPS is unavailable locally.
- **Rebleeding risk without prophylaxis is up to 60% at 1 year**; the highest-risk window is the **first 6 weeks**. Start secondary prophylaxis immediately after control of the index bleed, **within 7 days of admission**. No further measures are needed after preemptive TIPS.
- **First-line secondary prophylaxis = NSBB + EVL.** TIPS as first-line secondary prophylaxis lowers rebleeding vs EVL+NSBB but does **not** improve survival and causes more hepatic encephalopathy — reserve it for patients with another TIPS indication (e.g. recurrent/refractory ascites), or for rebleeding despite adequate secondary prophylaxis, especially within the first 6 weeks.
- If AVH occurs **despite** primary prophylaxis: check adherence and NSBB dose; options include adding isosorbide mononitrate, switching NSBB to carvedilol, or adding simvastatin.

### Statins — what the guidance actually says

- A single multicenter double-anonymized RCT found **reduced mortality when simvastatin was added to propranolol + EVL** in survivors of AVH, attributed to preventing deaths after acute-on-chronic liver failure — **with no effect on variceal rebleeding**. These findings "await clinical confirmation."
- Retrospective data suggest less progression to cirrhosis, decompensation, and death on statins, with greater HCC-risk reduction from lipophilic statins (simvastatin, atorvastatin).
- **There are insufficient data to recommend routine statin use** to prevent decompensation. Where used: **simvastatin should not exceed 20 mg/day in decompensated cirrhosis**, should be used with **caution if total bilirubin >3 mg/dL**, and only at **10–20 mg/day in CTP B–C** (rhabdomyolysis risk). Atorvastatin metabolism is altered in cirrhosis — prudent to use 10–20 mg pending data. At least four prospective RCTs are ongoing in CTP A/B cirrhosis.

### PREDESCI — the evidence behind early NSBB

201 patients with compensated cirrhosis and HVPG-confirmed CSPH **without high-risk varices**, randomized to beta-blocker (propranolol or carvedilol by acute hemodynamic response to propranolol) vs placebo. Titrated to tolerance, pulse ≥55 bpm and SBP ≥90 mm Hg, with planned upper limits of 160 mg propranolol / 25 mg carvedilol; **mean achieved doses were 95 mg/day propranolol and 19 mg/day carvedilol**. At 2 years, NSBB lowered decompensation risk (**HR 0.51; 95% CI 0.26–0.97**), predominantly by preventing ascites.

Caveats the guidance attaches: unique selection criteria (all had HVPG-confirmed CSPH, none selected by noninvasive testing); most had untreated hepatitis C predating all-oral DAAs; ongoing alcohol use was not assessed.

### Gastric and ectopic varices — Sarin classification

- **GOV1** — continuation of esophageal varices along the **lesser** curvature; shares natural history with esophageal varices and is treated comparably.
- **GOV2** — continuation along the **greater** curvature (cardiofundal).
- **IGV1** — isolated gastric varices in the **fundus**.
- **IGV2** — isolated gastric varices elsewhere in the stomach.
- If initial bleeding control fails, balloon tamponade — preferentially the **Linton-Nachlas** tube or the **gastric balloon of the Minnesota tube** — bridges to definitive therapy. Refer to a tertiary center if local expertise in bleeding GV is unavailable.

### Portal hypertensive gastropathy — Baveno III grading (Table 6)

| Feature | Score |
|---|---|
| Mucosal mosaic pattern — mild | 1 |
| Mucosal mosaic pattern — severe | 2 |
| Red markings — isolated | 1 |
| Red markings — confluent | 2 |
| Gastric antral vascular ectasia (GAVE) — absent | 1 |
| GAVE — present | 2 |

**Mild PHG ≤3; severe PHG ≥4.** This is the threshold behind GS 48's "greater than mild PHG."

- Prevalence of PHG in compensated cirrhosis **49%–80%**; 11% without varices, 35% with small varices, 80%–97% with medium/large varices. PHG development usually requires CSPH.
- Intramucosal hemorrhage (cherry red spots, black-brown spots, red point lesions) separates severe from mild PHG; concomitant **GAVE confers higher hemorrhage risk**.
- Acute bleeding from PHG is uncommon (**2.5%–5%**) and stops spontaneously in over half with supportive care. **Chronic blood loss** — defined as a **2 g/dL fall in hemoglobin over 6 months** — is commoner, at **4%–12%**.
- Exclude *[[helicobacter-pylori-infection|H. pylori]]* as an alternative cause of mucosal granularity before attributing findings to PHG.
- **PH-related polyps:** antral (occasionally duodenal) in ~1%–10% of cirrhosis; predominantly hyperplastic with negligible malignant potential. **Routine biopsy should be discouraged** — benign nature plus risk of significant bleeding from deep submucosal feeding vessels.
- [[argon-plasma-coagulation|Argon plasma coagulation]] may attenuate chronic PHG blood loss (nonrandomized data).

### PH in pregnancy — detail beyond GS 55–56

- Primary prophylaxis with NSBB **or** EBL is recommended for **medium and large varices**, with **preference for EBL when cherry red spots or red wale signs are present**.
- In AVH, **avoid terlipressin** (stimulates uterine contraction); somatostatin or octreotide may be used.
- Case series document band ligation and TIPS for secondary prophylaxis or refractory hemorrhage in pregnancy.
- Weak evidence suggests carvedilol causes **less fetal growth retardation** than propranolol (from cardiac-indication use).

### PH in HCC

Variceal bleeding risk appears increased with portal vein thrombosis. Recent anti-VEGF HCC trials required endoscopy within 6 months of enrollment, but recent data show **poor correlation between endoscopic findings and variceal bleeding and no benefit of EBL over NSBB prophylaxis**. Rebleeding rates after AVH are higher in HCC, but secondary prophylaxis significantly reduces that risk.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Not a graded document.** No GRADE evidence quality or strength attaches to any of the 58 statements.
- **Internal cross-reference error.** GS 13 cites "Box 2" for relative contraindications to NSBBs; the contraindication list is actually **Box 3** (Box 2 is "ABCs of HVPG measurement").
- **Internal dosing inconsistency.** Table 3 says increase carvedilol to 6.25 mg twice daily **after 3 days**; the body text says increase to 12.5 mg/day **after 2–3 days**. GS 2 caps maintenance at 12.5 mg/day, while the body text permits **up to 25 mg/day** when treating concomitant arterial hypertension.
- **"Rule of Five" requires prospective external validation** — explicitly listed in Box 4, and calibration is poor in obesity/NASH, ALT >3× ULN, and PSC with dominant stricture.
- **Non-TE elastography cutoffs** (MRE, pSWE, 2D-SWE) and laboratory-based tests are **not solidly validated** for CSPH or high-risk varices — Box 4 calls for cross-platform validation.
- **NSBB safety in ascites/refractory ascites** — the original signal of increased mortality came from a study using unusually high doses (160 mg long-acting propranolol/day); subsequent studies find NSBBs safe and potentially beneficial, but **SBP <90 mm Hg may attenuate the survival advantage**, possibly by reducing renal perfusion pressure and raising HRS-AKI risk.
- **Statins** — benefit awaits confirmation (Box 4h); at least four RCTs ongoing as of 2023.
- **Other Box 4 gaps:** noninvasive monitoring of 10%–20% HVPG changes; thresholds for recompensation after which endoscopy/NSBB can be stopped; external validation of PREDESCI in NASH; which patients benefit from earlier TIPS; nutritional intervention in sarcopenia/frailty; larger prospective studies of self-expanding esophageal stents.
