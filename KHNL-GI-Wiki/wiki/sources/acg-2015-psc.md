## Summary

First American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) clinical guideline for primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), a chronic cholestatic biliary disease defined by beading and stricture formation of intra- and/or extrahepatic bile ducts not ascribable to another cause. Diagnosis of exclusion; strong [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]] association (up to 80% of PSC patients, possibly as high as 90% when rectosigmoid biopsies are routinely taken), with elevated risks of [[cholangiocarcinoma]] (CCA), gallbladder cancer, [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer (CRC)]] in PSC-IBD, hepatobiliary failure, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency, and metabolic bone disease. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the preferred method of cholangiography for suspected PSC; [[ercp|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]] is reserved for tissue sampling or therapeutic dilation of dominant strictures. There is no established medical therapy — ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA) in doses >28 mg/kg/day is harmful and is recommended against (strong recommendation, high quality of evidence). [[liver-transplantation|Liver transplantation]] is the definitive treatment for decompensated cirrhosis.

The guideline carries 24 sequentially numbered recommendations, each with a GRADE strength (strong / conditional / weak) and quality of evidence (high / moderate / low / very low), collected in its Summary of Recommendations and repeated section by section.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Definition and epidemiology**

- Diagnosis of exclusion — beading and stricture formation of intra and/or extrahepatic bile ducts that cannot be ascribed to another cause, distinguishing PSC from secondary sclerosing cholangitis.
- Prevalence of PSC in ulcerative colitis ~5%; prevalence of IBD in PSC up to 80% (possibly 90%).
- United States prevalence estimated at 1 to over 16 per 100,000. Approximately 60–70% of patients with PSC and ulcerative colitis are men; age at diagnosis usually 30–40 y. Slight female predominance among patients without ulcerative colitis.
- Small-duct PSC ≈5% of the spectrum; macroscopic ducts normal, so cholangiography is not revealing and liver biopsy is required. Risk of progression to classic large-duct disease unknown.
- Periductal concentric "onion skin" fibrosis is seldom found; biopsy is usually reported only as "compatible" with PSC.

**Biochemistry and autoantibodies**

- Cholestatic profile with predominant elevation of alkaline phosphatase (ALP); aminotransferases often only modestly elevated. Some patients with PSC have normal serum ALP.
- Elevated serum immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) in ~10% of PSC patients; marked elevations defined as >140 mg/dL. IgG4-associated disease, unlike typical PSC, responds to corticosteroids.
- Antimitochondrial antibody (AMA) characteristic of primary biliary cirrhosis is not found in PSC. Anti-smooth muscle, antinuclear, and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies occur (>50% in some series) but are not specific.

*Secondary sclerosing cholangitis most often arises from prior biliary surgery, choledocholithiasis, bacterial cholangitis, intra-arterial floxuridine, or HIV/AIDS-related cholangiopathy.*

**Medical treatment**

- No established medical treatment. UDCA 13–15 mg/kg/day (the primary biliary cirrhosis dose) gave biochemical but not clinical improvement in 105 patients; 17–23 mg/kg/day was inconclusive (study enrolled only 63% of planned patients); 28–30 mg/kg/day produced worse outcomes than placebo (more transplantation, more varices) and is recommended against.
- High-dose UDCA has also been associated with increased risk of colonic neoplasia in PSC and ulcerative colitis.
- Many practitioners use ~20 mg/kg/day despite the absence of well-controlled trial data.
- Agents tested without proven clinical benefit: azathioprine, budesonide, docosahexaenoic acid, methotrexate, metronidazole, minocycline, mycophenolate mofetil, nicotine, pentoxifylline, pirfenidone, prednisolone, tacrolimus, vancomycin.
- Oral vancomycin: case series and anecdotal reports of marked improvement, particularly in the pediatric age group; no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of antibacterial therapy to date.

**Pruritus — doses**

- Mild: skin emollients ± antihistamines.
- Moderate: cholestyramine, total daily dose 4–16 g.
- Second line: rifampin 150–300 mg twice daily (monitor liver tests and serum bilirubin for hepatotoxicity); naltrexone up to 50 mg/day; sertraline 75–100 mg/day; phenobarbital 90 mg at bedtime.

**Dominant strictures and endoscopic therapy**

- Dominant stricture defined at cholangiography as a stenosis measuring <1.5 mm in the common bile duct or <1.0 mm in the hepatic ducts.
- Detected in 45% of patients at time of diagnosis in one retrospective series (56/125); new dominant strictures in 51% (77/151) over a median 7.1 y.
- Brush cytology specificity is very high but sensitivity is reported below 30%. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) increases sensitivity to 64% on one cytology specimen; polysomy on two sequential specimens raises positive predictive value to 69%. Cholangioscopy with directed biopsy reports sensitivity and specificity >90%.
- Preoperative antibiotics followed by a 3- to 5-day post-ERCP course have been advised; no prospective study has determined the best antibiotic choice.
- Percutaneous cholangiography is second line (hepatic arterial injury, hemobilia, cholangitis) — reserved for altered anatomy, inaccessible intrahepatic strictures, or very tight strictures.

**Cholangiocarcinoma and hepatobiliary malignancy**

- 10-year cumulative risk of CCA ~8%; risk is several hundred-fold higher than in patients without PSC.
- Among PSC patients who develop CCA, almost half of the malignancies are diagnosed within the first 4 months after the initial diagnosis of PSC.
- In a series of 128 PSC patients, 63% had dominant strictures and 26% of those (21/80) developed CCA.
- CA 19-9 has limited predictive value (elevated without CCA; normal/low with CCA). Positron emission tomography is problematic and not widely adopted.
- Gallstones occur in a quarter of patients with PSC; gallbladder polyps >8 mm carry a risk of being or becoming malignant. Gallbladder surveillance is advised at least annually with ultrasound.

**Liver transplantation**

- Median time from diagnosis to death or liver transplantation (LT) in the absence of effective therapy is 10–12 y; deceased-donor 5-year survival after LT for PSC is 80–85%.
- Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) exception points may be approved by the United Network for Organ Sharing Regional Review Board for: (i) recurrent cholangitis with >2 episodes of bacteremia or >1 episode of sepsis; (ii) CCA <3 cm in diameter without evidence of metastasis, undergoing treatment through an institutional review board-approved clinical trial; (iii) intractable pruritus.
- MELD exception points are **not** offered for PSC with non-cirrhotic portal hypertension.
- Recurrent PSC after LT affects as many as 20% at 5 years; progressive disease occurs in as many as one-third of those with recurrence.

**PSC and IBD**

- Risk of colorectal dysplasia and cancer is approximately four- to fivefold higher in PSC-IBD than in IBD without PSC.
- PSC-IBD phenotype: presents earlier in life, often quiescent/asymptomatic, infrequent rectal bleeding, predilection for right-sided colitis with relative rectal sparing, common "backwash ileitis", more pouchitis after ileo-anal anastomosis. Isolated small-bowel Crohn's disease is usually not associated with PSC.
- Meta-analysis of low-dose UDCA (8–13 mg/kg/day) suggested possible benefit against colonic neoplasia, but with significant heterogeneity; no prospective controlled trial demonstrates benefit for chemoprevention. 5-Acetylsalicylic acid may also reduce the risk of colonic malignancy.
- Portal hypertension in PSC-IBD increases the risk of stomal and peristomal varices; peristomal varices develop in 25% of patients who have ileal stomas after colectomy.

**Portal hypertension, bone, and vitamins**

- Pre-cirrhotic [[portal-hypertension|portal hypertension]] from nodular regenerative hyperplasia or obliterative venopathy occurred in 11/306 (3.3%) PSC patients transplanted at 2 institutions.
- Osteoporosis reported in 13–60% of patients with cholestatic liver disease; in a cohort of 237 PSC patients followed 10 y, osteoporosis in 36/237 (15%) and osteopenia in 41%. Risk rises with greater age, low body mass index, and longstanding IBD.
- World Health Organization bone mineral density (BMD) thresholds: T-score < −2.5 diagnostic of osteoporosis; T-score < −1.0 diagnostic of osteopenia. Each 1-point fall in T-score doubles to triples fracture risk.
- Bisphosphonates are not proven in PSC; alendronate significantly improved lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD in osteopenia with primary biliary cirrhosis, suggesting possible usefulness in PSC.
- Fat-soluble vitamin deficiency occurs in late-stage PSC once the patient becomes jaundiced; assess vitamins A, D, and E in advanced disease. Supplemental vitamin D and calcium have not been shown to reduce metabolic bone disease in this group.

**Monitoring intervals (Figure 1, Management of PSC)**

- Measure IgG4 in all patients at diagnosis.
- Assess for varices in patients with platelet count <150×10³/dL or cirrhosis.
- Assess bone density every 2–4 y by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry.
- Assess for fat-soluble vitamin deficiency in advanced disease/jaundice.
- Monitor liver biochemistries every 3–4 months for signs of strictures, tumors, or symptoms of autoimmune hepatitis.
- Consider CCA screening every 6–12 months with cross-sectional imaging (ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging) and CA 19-9.
- Colonoscopic screening annually in patients with co-existent IBD.
- In patients with deterioration and a dominant stricture, perform ERCP with peri-procedure antibiotics and consider balloon dilation, with stent if needed.
- Refer for LT if MELD exceeds 14, and consider referral for cases suspicious for complicating CCA.

| 3 | Liver biopsy is recommended to make a diagnosis in patients with suspected small duct PSC or to exclude other conditions such as suspected overlap with autoimmune hepatitis. | Conditional | Moderate |

| 21 | Bile acid sequestrants such as cholestyramine should be taken (prescribed) in patients with PSC and moderate pruritus, to reduce symptoms. Second-line treatment such as rifampin and naltrexone can be considered if cholestyramine is ineffective or poorly tolerated. | Conditional | Very low |

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Optimal interval and modality for CCA surveillance are unsettled; CA 19-9 has limited predictive value in both directions.
- Oral vancomycin has case-series support only; no RCT of antibacterial therapy has been performed.
- Low-dose UDCA (8–13 mg/kg/day) showed possible benefit against colonic neoplasia in meta-analysis with significant heterogeneity, while the guideline makes no recommendation for UDCA at any dose for hepatic benefit.
- IgG4 elevation overlaps between PSC (mild increases common) and IgG4-associated sclerosing cholangitis (marked elevations >140 mg/dL); the distinction is not fully standardized.
- Recommendation 15 (repeat colonoscopy every 3–5 y without prior colitis) is graded weak/low and is stated as what "some advocate" rather than as guideline practice.
- Endoscopic therapy for dominant strictures rests on retrospective series; one retrospective study of 125 patients found no significant difference in cholestasis 1 year after cholangiography between patients with and without dominant strictures.
