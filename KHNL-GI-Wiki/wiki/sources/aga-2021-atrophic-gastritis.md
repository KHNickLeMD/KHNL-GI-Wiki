## Summary

- Purpose: practical US framework for atrophic gastritis (AG) — a common, under-recognized preneoplastic gastric condition — due to chronic *Helicobacter pylori* infection (H. pylori–associated AG [HpAG], most common) or autoimmunity (autoimmune gastritis [AIG])
- Complements the 2020 AGA guideline on gastric intestinal metaplasia (GIM), which did not address AG
- Central message: intestinal metaplasia (IM) on gastric histology almost always means AG, but pathology reports rarely say so → underdiagnosis and poor risk stratification
- Covers histology (nonmetaplastic vs metaplastic atrophy; Operative Link for Gastritis Assessment [OLGA] / Operative Link for Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia Assessment [OLGIM] staging), endoscopic recognition (high-definition white-light endoscopy [HD-WLE], narrow-band imaging [NBI], Kimura-Takemoto), updated Sydney biopsies, serology, H. pylori test-and-treat, surveillance, type 1 gastric neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), micronutrient deficiency, autoimmune comorbidity
- Methods: published evidence + expert consensus; statements commissioned and approved by the AGA Institute Clinical Practice Updates Committee and Governing Board; external peer review

## Key Findings / Claims

### Epidemiology and natural history

- AG prevalence up to 15% in US populations; higher where H. pylori or gastric cancer is more common — non-White racial/ethnic groups, early-generation immigrants from high-risk countries
- Usually asymptomatic or nonspecific symptoms late; underdiagnosed (inconsistent pathology reporting)
- Meta-analysis: AG incidence rate ratio with vs without H. pylori **5.0 (95% confidence interval [CI] 3.1–8.3)**; AG incidence <1%/year without H. pylori
- Other risk factors for nonautoimmune AG: age, tobacco, high-salt diet, possibly chronic bile acid reflux
- AIG prevalence ~0.5–2% (may be overestimated); rises with age and other autoimmune disease (autoimmune thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes); up to one-third of autoimmune thyroid disease patients have AIG; women > men; little racial/ethnic variation
- Pernicious anemia (PA; megaloblastic anemia from B-12 deficiency, late-stage AIG): prevalence 0.15–1%
- Progression AG → gastric adenocarcinoma **0.1–0.3%/year** (similar to nondysplastic Barrett's esophagus or low-risk colorectal adenomas); higher with greater severity, extent, concomitant IM
- PA: meta-analysis of 27 studies ~7-fold relative risk of gastric cancer; most individual studies 2–4-fold
- Type 1 gastric NETs (hypergastrinemia → enterochromaffin-like [ECL] cell hyperplasia → dysplasia → NET): incidence 0.4–0.7%/year in chronic AG

### Histopathology

- Gland loss replaced by connective tissue (**nonmetaplastic** atrophy) or non-native epithelium — IM or pseudopyloric metaplasia (**metaplastic** atrophy); IM is the most frequent histologic manifestation
- HpAG: starts at incisura and antrocorpus transition as small foci → coalesces along lesser curvature and antrum → spreads to corpus/fundus
- AIG: corpus-predominant with antral sparing; lymphocytic destruction of oxyntic glands; antral atrophy in AIG → consider concomitant HpAG
- Uncertain biopsy site: gastrin (antrum) / pepsinogen I (corpus) stains can separate antral mucosa from pseudopyloric metaplasia — avoidable by separate jars
- OLGA (atrophy) and OLGIM (IM) stage = combination of severity score 0–3 in antrum/incisura and corpus; **stages III–IV carry significantly higher gastric cancer risk than 0–II**; OLGIM more reproducible but downstages some high-risk patients; rarely reported in the US

### Endoscopy

- High-quality systematic exam: adequate insufflation and cleansing; simethicone and 1% N-acetylcysteine may be considered; photo-document cardia, fundus, lesser and greater curvature of corpus and antrum, incisura, pylorus
- AG: pale mucosa, visible submucosal vessels, loss of folds, often a visible atrophic border; absent corpus rugal folds — sensitivity 67%, specificity 85% for moderate–severe corpus AG (Swedish study)
- **Kimura-Takemoto:** closed (C-1 antrum only; C-2 to lesser curvature of distal body; C-3 to lesser curvature of proximal body) vs open (O-1 anterior wall → O-3 greater curvature); **O2–O3 (severe/extensive) → significantly higher cumulative gastric cancer risk than C1–C2**; concordant with OLGA/OLGIM
- AIG early phase: often only nonspecific erythema — diagnosis missed without biopsies
- IM on HD-WLE: mildly nodular, ridged or tubulovillous pattern
  - HD-WLE + NBI (prospective multicenter, no magnification): IM sensitivity 87%, specificity 97%; dysplasia 92% / 99%
  - **Light blue crest (LBC)** — fine blue-white lines on epithelial crests: sensitivity and specificity ~90%; positive likelihood ratio 8.98, negative 0.12 (meta-analysis)
  - **White opaque field / substance (WOF/WOS)** — lipid droplets: specificity 100% (95% CI 85–100%), sensitivity 50% (95% CI 40–50%), 1 study
  - Magnifying endoscopy rarely available in the US; near-focus on newer HD endoscopes helps

### Updated Sydney biopsy protocol

- ~100% sensitivity for H. pylori colonization
- **5 biopsies:** antrum lesser and greater curvature within 2–3 cm of pylorus (2); corpus lesser curvature 4 cm proximal to incisura and greater curvature mid-body ~8 cm from cardia (2); incisura angularis (1) — **do not skip the incisura**
- Separately labeled jars; if cost is a concern, **at minimum 2 jars: antrum/incisura and body**
- Targeted biopsies of visible abnormalities in separately labeled jars

### Serology

- Serum pepsinogens (PG): PG I <70 µg/L and PG I:II ratio <3.0 → high sensitivity/specificity for severe corpus atrophy (mostly East Asian data); **not available for routine US use**
- Parietal cell antibody (PCA): most sensitive AIG marker; false positives (H. pylori, other autoimmune disease)
- Intrinsic factor antibody (IFA): low sensitivity (<30% in many studies), high specificity; more often positive later in disease
- Autoantibodies may precede clinical AIG, especially with other autoimmune disorders

### H. pylori and surveillance

- Most AG patients have current or prior H. pylori
- Mucosa may normalize after eradication in some, but most have passed a "point of no return"; risk stays elevated especially with extensive or moderate–severe atrophy (e.g., OLGA/OLGIM III/IV) → surveillance even after successful eradication; eradication still appears to reduce gastric cancer risk
- No randomized trials of surveillance; observational data link severe AG (histology, anatomic distribution, OLGA/OLGIM III/IV) with cancer → rationale for surveillance
- Factors to tailor interval: baseline endoscopy quality, family history of gastric cancer, immigration from high-incidence region, persistent H. pylori, smoking, diet; comorbidity and patient values
- AIG: European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) advocates surveillance every 3–5 years
- PA: gastric cancer risk may be highest in the first year after diagnosis; American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) advocates upper endoscopy within 6 months of PA diagnosis; new upper gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms in PA → endoscopy

### Type 1 gastric NETs

- AG-associated NETs ≈ 80–90% of gastric NETs, overwhelmingly type 1; usually small (<10 mm), corpus/fundus, well differentiated, indolent; polypoid, slightly yellow or red on HD-WLE
- Prognosis set by size, depth, mitotic activity; metastasis **<10% if ≤2 cm, approaching 20% if >2 cm**

- <1 cm: resect endoscopically, then surveillance endoscopy every 1–2 years by NET burden (optimal interval not well defined)
- >1–2 cm: consider endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) for depth of invasion and local metastasis to guide management
- >2 cm, invasion past the submucosa, or lymph node metastasis: surgical resection

### Coexisting conditions

- Corpus-predominant AG (any etiology): iron and B-12 deficiency from reduced acid and intrinsic factor; iron deficiency in up to 50% of corpus-predominant AG, often well before B-12 deficiency
- AIG: established association with autoimmune thyroid disease (shared susceptibility loci); low threshold to evaluate type 1 diabetes and Addison's disease if the clinical picture fits

## Recommendations

All 12 BPA statements, numbered as in the document. No grades (the CPU states no formal rating of evidence was done). Short statements kept near-verbatim; longer ones condensed without changing force or qualifiers.

| BPA | Statement |
|---|---|
| **1** | AG = loss of gastric glands, with or without metaplasia, in the setting of chronic inflammation mainly due to H. pylori infection or autoimmunity. Whatever the cause, **the diagnosis should be confirmed by histopathology**. |
| **2** | Providers should be aware that **IM on gastric histology almost invariably implies AG**. Gastroenterologists and pathologists should coordinate to document AG extent and severity more consistently, particularly if marked atrophy is present. |
| **3** | Providers should recognize typical endoscopic features of AG — pale mucosa, increased visibility of vasculature from thinned mucosa, loss of gastric folds — and, with concomitant IM, light blue crests and white opaque fields. Because these changes are often subtle, techniques to optimize evaluation of the gastric mucosa should be used. |
| **4** | When endoscopic features of AG are present, assess its extent endoscopically. Biopsy suspected atrophic/metaplastic areas for histologic confirmation and risk stratification — **at a minimum, biopsies from the body and from the antrum/incisura, in separately labeled jars**. Targeted biopsies of any other mucosal abnormality should also be taken. |
| **5** | With histology compatible with AIG, **consider checking antiparietal cell and anti-intrinsic factor antibodies** to assist diagnosis. Also evaluate for anemia due to vitamin B-12 and iron deficiency. |
| **6** | **All individuals with AG should be assessed for H. pylori.** If positive, treat, and confirm successful eradication with **nonserological** testing. |
| **7** | The optimal surveillance interval for AG is not well defined; decide by individual risk assessment and shared decision-making. **Surveillance endoscopy every 3 years should be considered in advanced AG**, defined by anatomic extent and histologic grade. |
| **8** | The optimal surveillance interval for **AIG is unclear**; consider interval endoscopic surveillance based on individualized assessment and shared decision-making. |
| **9** | Recognize PA as a late-stage manifestation of AIG (vitamin B-12 deficiency, macrocytic anemia). **Patients with newly diagnosed PA who have not had a recent endoscopy should undergo endoscopy with topographic biopsies** — to confirm corpus-predominant AG for risk stratification and rule out prevalent gastric neoplasia, including NETs. |
| **10** | **Individuals with AIG should be screened for type 1 gastric NETs with upper endoscopy.** Small NETs should be removed endoscopically, then surveillance endoscopy **every 1–2 years**, depending on NET burden. |
| **11** | Evaluate for **iron and vitamin B-12 deficiency** in AG of any etiology, especially if corpus-predominant. Conversely, in **unexplained iron or B-12 deficiency, consider AG** in the differential and pursue appropriate evaluation. |
| **12** | In AIG, recognize that concomitant autoimmune disorders — particularly **autoimmune thyroid disease** — are common. **Screening for autoimmune thyroid disease should be performed.** |

**Figure 3 — management algorithm** (embedded on [[atrophic-gastritis]]):

- Endoscopic: topographic biopsies for anatomic extent + histologic severity; consider surveillance — advanced AG every 3 years, AIG by individualized assessment (both by shared decision-making); newly diagnosed PA → consider upper endoscopy for risk stratification and prevalent neoplasia/NETs; evaluate for NETs
- Non-endoscopic: test and treat H. pylori and confirm eradication; evaluate for anemia; evaluate iron and B-12 irrespective of anemia; in AIG screen for autoimmune thyroid disease and keep a low threshold for other autoimmune disease (e.g., type 1 diabetes) by presentation; check PCA and IFA when endoscopic/histologic findings fit AIG (corpus-predominant with antral sparing; oxyntic atrophy with lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate)

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Surveillance of AG/GIM — newer, more specific guidance:** AGA 2021 says surveillance every 3 years "should be considered" for advanced AG (extent + grade), interval individualized. [[acg-2025-gastric-premalignant|American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2025]] recommends every 3 years for severe GIM or AG in antrum or corpus and for high-risk GIM, and recommends against surveillance for low-risk GIM or mild AG (conditional, very low). [[aga-2025-gastric-cancer-screening|AGA 2025]] BPA 9: moderate–severe AG and/or multifocal or incomplete GIM → every 3 years (more often with multiple risk factors). The newer documents govern the wiki; all three agree on ~3 years for high-risk disease.
- **GIM surveillance, earlier AGA guideline:** [[aga-2020-gastric-intestinal-metaplasia|AGA 2020 GIM]] suggests *against* routine GIM surveillance (conditional, very low), with shared decision-making for higher-risk patients — in tension with 3-yearly surveillance for advanced AG.
- **Small type 1 NETs:** AGA 2021 — resect all <1 cm endoscopically; >1–2 cm consider EUS. AGA 2025 BPA 13 (newer) — *may consider* resecting <1 cm, *should* endoscopically resect 1–2 cm; >2 cm cross-sectional imaging + surgical referral. AGA 2025 governs.
- **Gastric adenocarcinoma risk in AIG/PA:** AGA 2021 cites ~7-fold (meta-analysis) / 2–4-fold higher gastric cancer risk in PA. ACG 2025 notes that without H. pylori, adenocarcinoma risk in AIG appears similar to the general population (older studies did not control for H. pylori). ACG 2025 is newer.
- **Endoscopy at PA diagnosis:** AGA 2021 BPA 9 — endoscopy if no recent one (no time frame); [[asge-2015-gastric-premalignant|ASGE 2015]] — within 6 months; ACG 2025 — consider at new PA diagnosis, especially women ≥50.
- **AIG surveillance:** no interval in AGA 2021 or ACG 2025 (individualized); ESGE 2019 and the Italian society suggest 3–5 years.
- Serum pepsinogens unavailable in the US; ACG 2025 recommends against noninvasive biomarkers for screening/surveillance in the US.
