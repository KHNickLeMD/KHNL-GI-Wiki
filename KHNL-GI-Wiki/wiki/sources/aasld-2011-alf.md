## Summary
- 2011 AASLD position paper on [[acute-liver-failure|acute liver failure]] (ALF): rapid deterioration of liver function producing altered mentation and coagulopathy in a patient without known pre-existing liver disease.
- Builds a cause-directed workup (acetaminophen level, viral serologies, ceruloplasmin and copper for [[wilson-disease|Wilson disease]], autoantibodies for [[autoimmune-hepatitis|autoimmune hepatitis]], pregnancy test) on top of intensive supportive care, with 48 self-numbered recommendations.
- Etiology-specific therapy anchors the document: N-acetylcysteine (NAC) for acetaminophen and for [[drug-induced-liver-injury|drug-induced liver injury (DILI)]]; penicillin G ± silibinin plus NAC for Amanita mushroom poisoning; nucleos(t)ide analogues for [[chronic-hepatitis-b|hepatitis B]]; acyclovir for herpesvirus; early transplant listing for Wilson disease and mushroom poisoning.
- Neurologic management of [[hepatic-encephalopathy|hepatic encephalopathy (HE)]] and cerebral edema is the longest section — intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring in centers with expertise, mannitol first-line for intracranial hypertension, prophylactic hypertonic saline in the highest-risk group, and explicit avoidance of corticosteroids for ICP control.
- Emphasizes contact with a transplant center early and states plainly that available prognostic scores do not adequately select [[liver-transplantation|liver transplant]] candidates.
- Superseded for current practice by the [[acg-2023-alf|ACG Clinical Guideline: Acute Liver Failure (2023)]]; retained here for its dosing detail, its intensive-care tables, and the 2011 form of the King's College Criteria.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definition, Admission, and Transfer
- **ALF definition:** coagulation abnormality, usually international normalized ratio (INR) **≥1.5**, *and* any degree of mental alteration (encephalopathy), in a patient **without preexisting [[cirrhosis]]** and with an illness of **<26 weeks** duration.
  - Prothrombin time prolonged by **~4–6 seconds or more** (INR ≥1.5) plus any altered sensorium = diagnosis established; hospital admission mandatory.
  - Wilson disease, vertically-acquired hepatitis B virus (HBV), or autoimmune hepatitis may be included despite possible cirrhosis **if recognized for <26 weeks**.
- **Timing terms** — hyperacute <7 days, acute 7–21 days, subacute >21 days and <26 weeks. Popular but **not prognostically useful in themselves**: hyperacute disease does better only because it is usually acetaminophen or ischemic hepatopathy.
- **Prognostic criteria below do not apply to [[acute-on-chronic-liver-failure|acute-on-chronic]] disease.**
- **Transfer at grade I or II encephalopathy** — risks of transport rise, and transfer may be precluded, once stage III or IV develops. Any degree of encephalopathy → intensive care unit (ICU) admission and contact with a transplant center.
- Epidemiology: ~2,000 US cases/year; 1–8 per million in the United Kingdom; **~15% have no discernible cause**; overall 1-year survival including transplanted patients is **>65%**.

| Chemistries | Sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, calcium, magnesium, phosphate; glucose; aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase, gamma-glutamyl transferase, total bilirubin, albumin, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen |
| Blood gas / perfusion | Arterial blood gas; arterial lactate |
| Hematology | Complete blood count; blood type and screen |
| Toxicology | Acetaminophen level; toxicology screen |
| Viral serologies | Anti-hepatitis A virus IgM, hepatitis B surface antigen, anti-hepatitis B core IgM, anti-hepatitis E virus IgM\*, anti-hepatitis C virus, hepatitis C virus RNA†, herpes simplex virus 1 IgM, varicella-zoster virus |
| Metabolic | Ceruloplasmin‡ |
| Pregnancy | Pregnancy test (females) |
| Ammonia | Arterial if possible |
| Autoimmune markers | Antinuclear antibody, anti-smooth muscle antibody, immunoglobulin levels |
| Other | Human immunodeficiency virus 1 and 2§; amylase and lipase |

- \* If clinically indicated. † Done to recognize potential underlying infection. § Implications for potential liver transplantation.
- ‡ Ceruloplasmin only if Wilson disease is a consideration (e.g., age <40 years without another obvious explanation for ALF); in that case **uric acid** and the **bilirubin-to-alkaline-phosphatase ratio** are also helpful.
- **Plasma ammonia, preferably arterial**, may also be helpful. [[liver-biopsy|Liver biopsy]] — usually transjugular because of coagulopathy — is indicated when autoimmune hepatitis, metastatic liver disease, lymphoma, or herpes simplex hepatitis is suspected, but is **not required to determine prognosis**.
- A **nodular liver contour** can be seen in ALF and should **not** be read as cirrhosis in this setting.

### Etiology-Specific Therapy — Doses and Diagnostic Cues
| Etiology | What the paper specifies |
|---|---|
| Acetaminophen | Ingestions causing ALF usually **>10 gm/day (~150 mg/kg)**; rarely injury at **3–4 gm/day**. Aminotransferases **>3,500 IU/L** correlate highly with acetaminophen poisoning even without a history. Activated charcoal **1 gm/kg orally** as a slurry — most effective within 1 hour, may still benefit at 3–4 hours — given just before NAC, which it does not blunt. **NAC oral:** 140 mg/kg load, then **70 mg/kg every 4 hours × 17 doses**. **NAC intravenous (IV):** loading **150 mg/kg in 5% dextrose over 15 minutes** → **50 mg/kg over 4 hours** → **100 mg/kg over 16 hours** (or 6 mg/kg/hr). Give as early as possible but still of value **≥48 hours** after ingestion. When to stop is unsettled — a standard 72-hour course vs continuing until liver chemistries improve. |
| Mushroom (*Amanita phalloides*) | No confirmatory blood test; suspect on severe gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, cramping) **within hours to a day** of ingestion. Gastric lavage + activated charcoal by nasogastric tube if early. **Penicillin G 300,000 to 1 million units/kg/day IV**; **silibinin (milk thistle) 30–40 mg/kg/day IV or orally for 3–4 days** — generally reported more successful than penicillin G but **not licensed in the United States** (available in Europe and South America; emergency application possible). NAC often combined. |
| Drug-induced liver injury | **No specific antidote.** Stop every presumed or possible offending agent. Corticosteroids **not indicated** unless hypersensitivity (drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, DRESS) or an autoimmune reaction is suspected. Most idiosyncratic injury occurs **within the first 6 months**; a drug taken continuously **>1–2 years** is unlikely to cause de novo damage. Antibiotics, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents, and anticonvulsants are the commonest classes. |
| Hepatitis A / E | Supportive care only — no virus-specific treatment. Hepatitis E more severe in pregnancy; consider in travelers to endemic areas. |
| Hepatitis B | Lamivudine (and possibly other nucleos(t)ide analogues) may be considered, though **evidence of efficacy is equivocal**. Hepatitis B surface antigen–positive patients starting chemotherapy or immunosuppression should get prophylaxis, continued **6 months after** immunosuppressive therapy ends. |
| Herpes simplex / varicella-zoster | **Acyclovir 5–10 mg/kg every 8 hours for at least 7 days.** Patients are often **anicteric and appear septic**; skin lesions present in only about **50%**. Biopsy helps make the diagnosis. |
| Wilson disease | 2–3% of US ALF cases; fulminant presentation **uniformly fatal without transplantation**. Coombs-negative hemolytic anemia with bilirubin **>20 mg/dL**; Kayser-Fleischer rings in **~50%**; ceruloplasmin low but **normal in up to 15%**; very low alkaline phosphatase or uric acid; **bilirubin (mg/dL) to alkaline phosphatase (IU/L) ratio >2.0** is a rapid, reliable indirect indicator. Lower copper with albumin dialysis, continuous hemofiltration, plasmapheresis, or plasma exchange. **Penicillamine is not recommended in ALF** — risk of hypersensitivity. |
| Autoimmune hepatitis | Autoantibodies **absent in up to 30%** → biopsy if suspected and antibodies negative. Steroids (**prednisone 40–60 mg/day**) may be tried in early-stage ALF **without multi-organ failure**, but may be deleterious; do **not** delay transplant evaluation awaiting a response. |
| Acute fatty liver of pregnancy / hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets (HELLP) syndrome | Generally confined to the last trimester. Triad of jaundice, coagulopathy, low platelets, often with hypoglycemia; pre-eclampsia features common; steatosis on imaging, Oil-red O staining on biopsy. Intrahepatic hemorrhage or hepatic rupture are rare emergencies. Recovery is usually rapid after delivery; postpartum deterioration needing transplantation is recognized. |
| Acute ischemic injury ("shock liver") | After cardiac arrest, significant hypovolemia/hypotension, or severe congestive heart failure; documented hypotension not always found. Aminotransferases and lactate dehydrogenase markedly elevated, improving rapidly once the circulation stabilizes. Simultaneous renal dysfunction and muscle necrosis. **Transplantation is seldom indicated.** |
| Budd-Chiari syndrome | Abdominal pain, [[ascites]], striking hepatomegaly. Confirm with imaging (computerized tomography (CT), Doppler ultrasonography, venography, magnetic resonance venography) and test for hypercoagulable conditions. Transplantation rather than venous decompression when hepatic failure is present. |
| Malignant infiltration | Massive hepatic enlargement; diagnose by imaging and biopsy. **Transplantation is not an option.** Breast cancer, small cell lung cancer, lymphoma, melanoma, myeloma. |

### Hepatic Encephalopathy Grades (Table 5)
| Grade | Definition |
|---|---|
| I | Changes in behavior with minimal change in level of consciousness |
| II | Gross disorientation, drowsiness, possibly asterixis, inappropriate behavior |
| III | Marked confusion; incoherent speech, sleeping most of the time but arousable to vocal stimuli |
| IV | Comatose, unresponsive to pain, decorticate or decerebrate posturing |

- Grades overlap; clinical judgment is required.
- Cerebral edema is **seldom seen in grade I–II**, rises to **25–35% with grade III**, and **65–75% or more in grade IV** coma.

### Intracranial Hypertension — Targets, Agents, Numbers
- **Prophylaxis:** head elevated **30 degrees**; minimize stimulation and pain; avoid Valsalva manoeuvres (endotracheal suctioning); endotracheal lidocaine advocated.
- **Ammonia:** arterial ammonia **>200 ug/dL** is strongly associated with cerebral herniation; serum ammonia **<75 ug/dL** is rarely associated with encephalopathy. (Recommendation 32 below uses a different measure, **>150 μM**, to define the highest-risk group.)
- **Targets once an ICP monitor is placed:** keep **ICP below 20–25 mm Hg** and **cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) above 50–60 mm Hg** (CPP = mean arterial pressure (MAP) − ICP). Trauma data suggest CPP above 70 mm Hg may further improve neurologic outcome where achievable.
- **Mannitol:** bolus **0.5–1.0 g/kg** IV, repeatable once or twice **as long as serum osmolality is <320 mOsm/L**. Does not bring ICP down to <25 mm Hg in severe intracranial hypertension (**ICP >60 mm Hg**). Volume overload with renal impairment may require dialysis.
- **Hypertonic saline:** prophylactic induction of hypernatremia to **serum sodium 145–155 mEq/L** lowered the incidence of intracranial hypertension; **not studied as treatment of established** intracranial hypertension.
- **Hyperventilation:** to **PaCO2 25–30 mm Hg**; effect is short-lived. Acceptable acutely to delay impending herniation; **not** for prophylaxis or routine management.
- **Barbiturates** (thiopental or pentobarbital) when severe intracranial hypertension does not respond to other measures; significant systemic hypotension limits use and clearance is markedly reduced, precluding neurologic assessment for prolonged periods.
- **Hypothermia:** human experience is cooling to a core temperature of **33–34°C** as a bridge to transplantation or to control ICP during surgery; recommendation 33 below states **34–35°C**. Not compared with normothermia in a controlled trial. Risks: infection, coagulation disturbance, cardiac arrhythmias, possible impairment of hepatic regeneration.
- **Corticosteroids** failed to improve cerebral edema or survival in a controlled ALF trial.
- **Seizures:** control promptly with phenytoin; short-acting benzodiazepines in phenytoin-refractory cases. Prophylactic phenytoin is **not** recommended — one trial showed less cerebral edema at autopsy but no survival difference, and a subsequent trial showed no benefit at all.
- **Sedation / paralysis:** avoid sedation in grade I–II where possible; unmanageable agitation → small doses of short-acting benzodiazepines. In grade III–IV, intubation and ventilation are mandatory; **cis-atracurium** is preferable (no muscle contraction, does not raise ICP), and **propofol** is often chosen for sedation because it may reduce cerebral blood flow — use small doses given its long half-life in liver failure.
- **Lactulose:** a retrospective comparison found a **small increase in survival time** but **no difference in encephalopathy severity or overall outcome**; bowel distension may complicate the surgical field. L-ornithine L-aspartate failed to lower ammonia or improve survival.
- **ICP monitoring:** practice varies widely; the rationale is earlier recognition of intracranial hypertension, since Cushing's triad is not uniformly present and other neurologic changes appear late. CT of the brain does **not** reliably demonstrate edema, especially early. Complication rate **3.8% (1% fatal hemorrhage)** with epidural catheters in an early series of 262 patients; a more recent series reported **2 of 58** bleeding complications. No series has shown improved outcome from monitoring itself. Refractory intracranial hypertension and/or decreased CPP are relative contraindications to transplantation at many centers.

### Intensive Care of Acute Liver Failure (Table 4)
| Domain | Measures |
|---|---|
| Cerebral edema — grade I/II encephalopathy | Consider transfer to liver transplant facility and listing for transplantation · Brain CT: rule out other causes of decreased mental status, little utility to identify cerebral edema · Avoid stimulation; avoid sedation if possible · Antibiotics: surveillance and treatment of infection required, prophylaxis possibly helpful · Lactulose, possibly helpful |
| Cerebral edema — grade III/IV encephalopathy | Continue the above · Intubate trachea (may require sedation) · Elevate head of bed · Consider placement of ICP monitoring device · Immediate treatment of seizures required; prophylaxis of unclear value · Mannitol: use for severe elevation of ICP or first clinical signs of herniation · Hypertonic saline to raise serum sodium to 145–155 mmol/L · Hyperventilation: effects short-lived; may use for impending herniation |
| Infection | Surveillance for and prompt antimicrobial treatment of infection required · Antibiotic prophylaxis possibly helpful but not proven |
| Coagulopathy | Vitamin K: give at least one dose · Fresh frozen plasma (FFP): give only for invasive procedures or active bleeding · Platelets: give only for invasive procedures or active bleeding · Recombinant activated factor VII: possibly effective for invasive procedures · Prophylaxis for stress ulceration: give histamine-2 (H2) blocker or proton pump inhibitor (PPI) |
| Hemodynamics / renal failure | Volume replacement · Pressor support (dopamine, epinephrine, norepinephrine) as needed to maintain adequate MAP · Avoid nephrotoxic agents · Continuous modes of hemodialysis if needed · Vasopressin recommended in hypotension refractory to volume resuscitation and no epinephrine |
| Metabolic concerns | Follow closely: glucose, potassium, magnesium, phosphate · Consider nutrition: enteral feedings if possible or total parenteral nutrition |

### Coagulopathy, Bleeding, Hemodynamics, Metabolic
- **Vitamin K 5–10 mg subcutaneously** routinely — vitamin K deficiency is reported in ALF.
- Platelet counts frequently fall to **≤150,000/mm³ (50–70% of patients)**. Do **not** correct the INR with plasma in the absence of bleeding: clinically significant blood loss is rare and correction obscures the INR trend, an important prognostic marker.
- **Platelet transfusion thresholds** (none studied specifically in ALF): **10,000/mm³** is the non-ALF trigger; some experts use **15,000–20,000/mm³** with infection or sepsis; **50,000–70,000/mm³** has been considered adequate before invasive procedures, while thromboelastography suggests **100,000/mm³** may be more appropriate; transfuse patients with significant bleeding and platelets below **~50,000/mm³** unless thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura or heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is present.
- Recombinant activated factor VII with FFP gives effective temporary correction without volume overload, but is very costly and has been linked to serious thromboembolism (myocardial infarction, portal vein thrombosis). Plasmapheresis is an alternative when plasma alone cannot correct a severely elevated INR.
- Spontaneous bleeding is capillary-type (stomach, lungs, genitourinary); **variceal bleeding almost never occurs** and spontaneous intracranial bleeding without an ICP monitor is reported in **<1%**.
- **Hypotension:** resuscitate with **normal saline** first; change to **half-normal saline containing 75 mEq/L sodium bicarbonate if acidotic**; add **dextrose** to crystalloid to prevent hypoglycemia.
- **Circulatory targets:** MAP at least **75 mm Hg** or CPP **60–80 mm Hg**. **Norepinephrine** is the preferred first agent (preserves splanchnic/hepatic flow); vasopressin or terlipressin may be added in norepinephrine-refractory cases.
- **Renal failure:** avoid aminoglycosides and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; use caution with IV contrast. **Continuous** renal replacement improves cardiovascular and intracranial stability vs intermittent hemodialysis. Fluid-overloaded patients in renal failure can have **~500 mL of plasma volume** removed by continuous renal replacement to support CPP.
- **Metabolic / nutrition:** hypoglycemia managed with continuous glucose infusion — symptoms are masked by encephalopathy. Phosphate, magnesium, and potassium are frequently low and need repeated supplementation. **Start enteral feeding early**; avoid severe protein restriction — **60 grams per day of protein is reasonable** in most cases. Branched-chain amino acids are **not** superior. Parenteral nutrition if enteral feeding is contraindicated.

### Prognosis and Transplantation
- **Transplant-free survival was ≥50%** for ALF due to acetaminophen, hepatitis A, shock liver, or pregnancy-related disease; **all other etiologies showed <25%** transplant-free survival.
- Overall mortality has improved to **30–40%**; spontaneous survival has risen from **10–20% to about 40%**; 1-year survival after transplantation is **>60%**.
- **King's College Hospital criteria** derived from a retrospective cohort of nearly 600 patients: positive predictive values **70% to nearly 100%**, negative predictive values **25–94%**; meta-analysis pooled **sensitivity 68–69%, specificity 82–92%**.
- **Clichy criteria** (acute hepatitis B cohort): **factor V <20% in patients <30 years**, or **factor V <30% in any patient with grade 3–4 encephalopathy**, predicted mortality with positive predictive value **82%** and negative predictive value **98%**. Factor V is less available and subsequent studies found it **less accurate** than the King's College criteria.
- **Listing and outcomes:** ALF is one of few conditions allowing **United Network for Organ Sharing status 1A** ("super urgent" in the United Kingdom). About half of ALF patients undergo transplantation; ALF is <10% of US transplants and ~11% in Europe. Roughly **35% of listed patients recover spontaneously**, so as many as **20% of ALF patients may be transplanted needlessly**. In the largest US study **44% were listed, 29% received a graft, and 10% of the overall group (25% of those listed) died on the waiting list**.
- **Living donor liver transplantation** accounted for ~2% of ALF transplants (1988–2010); 1-year survival after right-lobe living donation **~75%**; **donor death risk 0.2%**; ABO-incompatible grafts have **30–60% 1-year graft survival**. **Auxiliary transplantation** (partial graft, native liver left in place): overall survival **~60–65%**, with immunosuppression successfully withdrawn in **65–85%** by 1 year post-transplant.
- **Liver support systems:** no artificial system reliably reduces mortality; a meta-analysis of bioartificial devices demonstrated no efficacy; exchange transfusion, charcoal hemoperfusion, extracorporeal liver perfusion, and intra-portal hepatocyte infusion remain experimental.

**King's College Criteria — acetaminophen-induced ALF**

|---|---|

| Decision | Criteria |
|---|---|
| List for OLT if | INR **>6.5** **and** encephalopathy present (irrespective of grade) |

## Recommendations
All 48 recommendations as the document numbers and grades them; condensed only where the original is long, with every qualifier kept.

1. Patients with ALF should be hospitalized and monitored frequently, preferably in an ICU (III)
2. Contact with a transplant center and plans to transfer appropriate patients with ALF should be initiated early in the evaluation process (III)
3. The precise etiology of ALF should be sought to guide further management decisions (III)
4. For patients with known or suspected acetaminophen overdose within 4 hours of presentation, give activated charcoal just prior to starting NAC dosing (I)
5. Begin NAC promptly in all patients where the quantity of acetaminophen ingested, serum drug level or rising aminotransferases indicate impending or evolving liver injury (II-1)
6. NAC may be used in cases of acute liver failure in which acetaminophen ingestion is possible or when knowledge of circumstances surrounding admission is inadequate but aminotransferases suggest acetaminophen poisoning (III)
7. In ALF patients with known or suspected mushroom poisoning, consider administration of penicillin G and N-acetylcysteine (III)
8. Patients with acute liver failure secondary to mushroom poisoning should be listed for transplantation, as this procedure is often the only lifesaving option (III)
9. Obtain details (including onset of ingestion, amount and timing of last dose) concerning all prescription and non-prescription drugs, herbs and dietary supplements taken over the past year (III)
10. Determine ingredients of non-prescription medications whenever possible (III)
11. In the setting of acute liver failure due to possible drug hepatotoxicity, discontinue all but essential medications (III)
12. N-acetylcysteine may be beneficial for acute liver failure due to drug-induced liver injury (I)
13. Viral hepatitis A- (and E-) related acute liver failure must be treated with supportive care as no virus-specific treatment has proven to be effective (III)
14. Nucleos(t)ide analogues should be considered for hepatitis B-associated acute liver failure and for prevention of post-transplant recurrence.(III)
15. Patients with known or suspected herpes virus or varicella zoster as the cause of acute liver failure should be treated with acyclovir (5-10 mg/kg IV every 8 hours) and may be considered for transplantation (III)
16. To exclude Wilson disease one should obtain ceruloplasmin, serum and urinary copper levels, slit lamp examination for Kayser-Fleischer rings, hepatic copper levels when liver biopsy is feasible, and total bilirubin/alkaline phosphatase ratio (III)
17. Patients in whom Wilson disease is the likely cause of acute liver failure must be promptly considered for liver transplantation (III)
18. Liver biopsy is recommended when autoimmune hepatitis is suspected as the cause of acute liver failure, and autoantibodies are negative (III)
19. Patients with coagulopathy and mild hepatic encephalopathy due to autoimmune hepatitis may be considered for corticosteroid treatment (prednisone, 40-60 mg/day) (III)
20. Patients with autoimmune hepatitis should be considered for transplantation even while corticosteroids are being administered (III)
21. For acute fatty liver of pregnancy or the HELLP syndrome, expeditious delivery of the infant is recommended. Transplantation may need to be considered if hepatic failure does not resolve quickly following delivery (III)
22. In ALF patients with evidence of ischemic injury, cardiovascular support is the treatment of choice (III)
23. Hepatic vein thrombosis with acute hepatic failure is an indication for liver transplantation, provided underlying malignancy is excluded (II-3)
24. In patients with acute liver failure who have a previous cancer history or massive hepatomegaly, consider underlying malignancy and obtain imaging and liver biopsy to confirm or exclude the diagnosis (III)
25. If the etiological diagnosis remains elusive after extensive initial evaluation, liver biopsy may be appropriate to attempt to identify a specific etiology that might influence treatment strategy (III)
26. In early stages of encephalopathy, lactulose may be used either orally or rectally to effect a bowel purge, but should not be administered to the point of diarrhea, and may interfere with the surgical field by increasing bowel distention during liver transplantation (III)
27. Patients who progress to high-grade hepatic encephalopathy (grade III or IV) should undergo endotracheal intubation (III)
28. Seizure activity should be treated with phenytoin and benzodiazepines with short half-lives. Prophylactic phenytoin is not recommended (III)
29. Intracranial pressure monitoring is recommended in ALF patients with high grade hepatic encephalopathy, in centers with expertise in ICP monitoring, in patients awaiting and undergoing liver transplantation (III)
30. In the absence of ICP monitoring, frequent (hourly) neurological evaluation is recommended to identify early evidence of intracranial hypertension (III)
31. In the event of intracranial hypertension, a mannitol bolus (0.5-1.0 gm/kg body weight) is recommended as first-line therapy; however, the prophylactic administration of mannitol is not recommended (II-2)
32. In ALF patients at highest risk for cerebral edema (serum ammonia >150 μM, grade 3/4 hepatic encephalopathy, acute renal failure, requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP), the prophylactic induction of hypernatremia with hypertonic saline to a sodium level of 145-155 mEq/L is recommended (I)
33. Short-acting barbiturates and the induction of hypothermia to a core body temperature of 3435 C may be considered for intracranial hypertension refractory to osmotic agents as a bridge to liver transplantation (II-3)
34. Corticosteroids should not be used to control elevated ICP in patients with ALF (I)
35. Periodic surveillance cultures are recommended to detect bacterial and fungal pathogens as early as possible. Antibiotic treatment should be initiated promptly according to surveillance culture results at the earliest sign of active infection or deterioration (progression to high grade hepatic encephalopathy or elements of the SIRS [systemic inflammatory response syndrome]) (III)
36. Prophylactic antibiotics and antifungals have not been shown to improve overall outcomes in ALF and therefore cannot be advocated in all patients, particularly those with mild hepatic encephalopathy (III)
37. Replacement therapy for thrombocytopenia and/or prolonged prothrombin time is recommended only in the setting of hemorrhage or prior to invasive procedures (III)
38. Patients with ALF in the ICU should receive prophylaxis with H2 blocking agents or proton pump inhibitors (or sucralfate as a second-line agent) for acid-related gastrointestinal bleeding associated with stress (I)
39. Fluid resuscitation and maintenance of adequate intravascular volume are recommended on presentation in patients with ALF. The initial treatment of hypotension should be with intravenous normal saline (III)
40. If dialysis support is needed for acute renal failure, it is recommended that a continuous mode rather than an intermittent mode be used (I)
41. Pulmonary artery catheterization is rarely necessary in patients with ALF and is associated with significant morbidity. Instead, appropriate volume status should be ensured with a volume challenge (III)
42. Systemic vasopressor support with agents such as norepinephrine should be administered in volume-refractory hypotension or to ensure adequate CPP. Vasopressin or terlipressin can be added to norepinephrine in norepinephrine-refractory cases, but should be used cautiously in severely encephalopathic patients with intracranial hypertension (II-1)
43. Goals of circulatory support in patients with ALF are a MAP 75 mmHg and CPP 60-80 mmHg (II)
44. Metabolic homeostasis must be carefully maintained in ALF patients. Overall nutritional status as well as glucose, phosphate, potassium and magnesium levels should be monitored frequently, with expeditious correction of derangements (III)
45. Currently available prognostic scoring systems do not adequately predict outcome and determine candidacy for liver transplantation. Reliance entirely upon these guidelines is thus not recommended.(III)
46. Urgent hepatic transplantation is indicated in acute liver failure where prognostic indicators suggest a high likelihood of death (II-3)
47. Living donor or auxiliary liver transplantation may be considered in the setting of limited organ supply, but its use remains controversial (II-3)
48. Currently available liver support systems are not recommended outside of clinical trials; their future in the management of acute liver failure remains unclear (II-1)
