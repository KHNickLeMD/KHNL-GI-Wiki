## Summary

SCENIC (**S**urveillance for **C**olorectal **E**ndoscopic **N**eoplasia **D**etection and Management **i**n **I**nflammatory Bowel Disease Patients: **I**nternational **C**onsensus) is a joint ASGE/AGA international consensus addressing two questions: (1) how surveillance colonoscopy for dysplasia should be performed, and (2) how dysplasia found at colonoscopy should be managed. It marks the paradigm shift from the older model (where most dysplasia was thought invisible, justifying ≥32 random biopsies) to the modern recognition that **most dysplasia in IBD is visible** with current video endoscopy.

The panel standardized terminology (modified Paris classification): dysplasia is described as **visible** (polypoid — pedunculated/sessile; or nonpolypoid — superficial elevated/flat/depressed, with descriptors for ulceration and distinct vs indistinct border) or **invisible** (found on random/non-targeted biopsy). The terms **DALM, adenoma-like, and non-adenoma-like were explicitly abandoned**. "Endoscopically resectable" requires distinct margins, apparent complete removal, histologic confirmation of complete removal, and dysplasia-free biopsies of adjacent mucosa.

For detection, the panel favored **high-definition over standard-definition** white-light colonoscopy and **chromoendoscopy** (methylene blue or indigo carmine dye spray) over white-light, while finding insufficient evidence to recommend narrow-band imaging (NBI) over white-light or over chromoendoscopy. For management, the major shift is away from reflexive colectomy: after complete endoscopic removal of resectable polypoid or nonpolypoid visible dysplasia, **continued surveillance colonoscopy is favored over colectomy**. Endoscopically invisible dysplasia confirmed by a GI pathologist warrants referral to an endoscopist with IBD-surveillance/chromoendoscopy expertise.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Recommendations (verbatim, Table 2 — strength; quality of evidence):**

*Detection of dysplasia on surveillance colonoscopy*
1. When performing surveillance with white-light colonoscopy, **high definition is recommended rather than standard definition** (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence).
2. When performing surveillance with standard-definition colonoscopy, **chromoendoscopy is recommended rather than white-light colonoscopy** (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence).
3. When performing surveillance with high-definition colonoscopy, **chromoendoscopy is suggested rather than white-light colonoscopy** (conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence).
4. When performing surveillance with standard-definition colonoscopy, **narrow-band imaging is not suggested in place of white-light colonoscopy** (conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence).
5. When performing surveillance with high-definition colonoscopy, **narrow-band imaging is not suggested in place of white-light colonoscopy** (conditional recommendation, moderate-quality evidence).
6. When performing surveillance with image-enhanced high-definition colonoscopy, **narrow-band imaging is not suggested in place of chromoendoscopy** (conditional recommendation, moderate-quality evidence).

*Management of dysplasia discovered on surveillance colonoscopy*
7. After complete removal of endoscopically resectable **polypoid** dysplastic lesions, **surveillance colonoscopy is recommended rather than colectomy** (strong recommendation, very low-quality evidence).
8. After complete removal of endoscopically resectable **nonpolypoid** dysplastic lesions, **surveillance colonoscopy is suggested rather than colectomy** (conditional recommendation, very low-quality evidence).
9. For patients with endoscopically **invisible dysplasia** (confirmed by a GI pathologist), **referral is suggested to an endoscopist with expertise in IBD surveillance using chromoendoscopy with high-definition colonoscopy** (conditional recommendation, very low-quality evidence).

**Supporting data points:**
- HD vs SD colonoscopy: dysplasia found in ~2× as many patients (adjusted prevalence ratio 2.2, 95% CI 1.1–4.5).
- Chromoendoscopy vs SD white-light: meta-analysis RR 1.8 (1.2–2.6) for proportion of patients with dysplasia, absolute increase 6% (3%–9%); chromoendoscopy adds a mean ~11 min to the procedure.
- HD chromoendoscopy vs HD white-light (tandem): dysplasia in 21% vs 9% (P = .007).
- IBD interval cancers (6–36 mo post-colonoscopy) far exceed non-IBD patients (15.1% Crohn's, 15.8% UC vs 5.8% non-IBD) — current surveillance misses clinically relevant neoplasia.

**Terminology (Table 1):** visible (polypoid: pedunculated/sessile; nonpolypoid: superficial elevated/flat/depressed; descriptors: ulcerated, distinct/indistinct border) vs invisible dysplasia; DALM/adenoma-like/non-adenoma-like abandoned.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- SCENIC did not address **surveillance intervals** or risk stratification (timing left to other guidelines, e.g. ACG/AGA).
- Whether the extra lesions found by chromoendoscopy carry the same CRC risk as historically described dysplasia is unknown.
- The role of **random biopsies** when HD chromoendoscopy is used was not resolved (panel did not eliminate random biopsies); subsequent guidelines continue to debate targeted-only vs targeted + random.
