## Summary

International consensus guideline on hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), an autosomal dominant disease with an estimated prevalence of **1 in 5000**, characterized by vascular malformations (VMs) of skin, mucous membranes of the nose, and the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, as well as brain, lung, and liver, with complications arising from chronic bleeding, acute hemorrhage, and shunting. It is underdiagnosed, and diagnostic delay is common. In **97%** of patients with a definite clinical diagnosis, a causative mutation is identified in **endoglin (*ENG*, HHT type 1)**, **activin receptor-like kinase-1 (*ACVRL1*, HHT type 2)**, or ***SMAD4*** (juvenile polyposis–HHT overlap).

The Guidelines Working Group — clinical and genetic experts from 15 countries, plus methodologists, health care workers and administrators, patient advocates, and persons with HHT — ran a systematic literature search to June 2019 (221 articles into evidence tables), drafted recommendations by GRADE, and voted at a consensus conference in Toronto in November 2019. **Consensus of 80% agreement was required** for a recommendation to be included.

**Six new priority topic areas, 6 recommendations each = 36 new recommendations:** epistaxis, GI bleeding, anemia and iron deficiency, liver VMs, pediatric care, and pregnancy and delivery. Anemia, pediatrics, and pregnancy/delivery had not been addressed previously. A further **20 recommendations from the First International HHT Guidelines were not reassessed but remain currently recommended** and are reproduced in the document's Table — epistaxis A7–A10, liver VMs D7–D8, diagnosis of HHT G1–G5, brain VMs H1–H4, and pulmonary arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) I1–I5. **56 recommendations in total.**

**Two different rating vocabularies appear in this document, and they must not be merged.** The 36 new recommendations carry *Quality of evidence* (high / moderate / low / very low; "low [expert consensus]" where applicable) plus *Strength of recommendation* (strong / weak), each with its consensus agreement percentage. The 20 carried-forward First-Guideline recommendations instead carry *Level of evidence* (I / II / III) plus *Strength of recommendation* (strong / weak), and many report only an agreement percentage. Numbering (A1…I5) is **the document's own**, from its Table.

### A. Epistaxis management

**A1–A6 [new]**

| # | Recommendation | Quality | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A1** | Patients with HHT-related epistaxis use **moisturizing topical therapies that humidify the nasal mucosa** to reduce epistaxis | Moderate (98%) | Strong (100%) |
| **A2** | Consider **oral tranexamic acid** for epistaxis that does not respond to moisturizing topical therapies | High (92%) | Strong (94%) |
| **A3** | Consider **ablative therapies for nasal telangiectasias — laser, radiofrequency ablation, electrosurgery, sclerotherapy** — in patients who have failed to respond to moisturizing topical therapies | Moderate (83%) | Weak (94%) |
| **A4** | Consider **systemic antiangiogenic agents** for epistaxis that has failed to respond to moisturizing topical therapies, ablative therapies, and/or tranexamic acid | Moderate (92%) | Strong (82%) |
| **A5** | Consider **septodermoplasty** for epistaxis that has failed to respond sufficiently to moisturizing topical therapies, ablative therapies, and/or tranexamic acid | Low (92%) | Weak (88%) |
| **A6** | Consider **nasal closure** for patients whose epistaxis has failed to respond sufficiently to moisturizing topical therapies, ablative therapies, and/or tranexamic acid | Moderate (86%) | Strong (82%) |

**A7–A10 [First Guidelines, not reassessed]**

| # | Recommendation | Level | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A7** | Physicians advise patients with HHT-related epistaxis to **use agents that humidify the nasal mucosa to prevent epistaxis** (agreement 94%) | III | Weak |
| **A8** | Refer HHT patients with epistaxis who desire treatment to **otorhinolaryngologists with HHT expertise** for evaluation and treatment (87%) | III | Weak |
| **A9** | When considering **nasal surgery for reasons other than epistaxis**, patient and clinician obtain consultation from an otorhinolaryngologist with expertise in HHT-related epistaxis (100%) | III | Weak |
| **A10** | Treatment of **acute epistaxis requiring intervention** include packing with material or products that have a **low likelihood of causing rebleeding with removal** (e.g., lubricated low-pressure pneumatic packing) (93%) | III | Weak |

**Supporting commentary (condensed):**
- Topical saline reduced epistaxis severity score vs other topical therapies in one randomized controlled trial (RCT); saline spray or gel typically used twice daily.
- Two RCTs of oral tranexamic acid showed a significant decrease in epistaxis severity with minimal adverse events.
- One RCT showed reduced epistaxis severity score with sclerotherapy; multiple uncontrolled series showed various ablative therapies **temporarily** reduce epistaxis. Choose the ablative modality by local expertise; ablative therapy is **temporizing**, and **septal perforation is a known complication of all techniques**.
- Intravenous (IV) bevacizumab in uncontrolled series reduced epistaxis, improved anemia, reduced transfusion requirements, and improved quality of life (QOL).
- Septodermoplasty series showed reduced epistaxis, improved anemia, reduced surgical reintervention, or improved QOL; nasal closure series showed reduced epistaxis. For both, consider when epistaxis affects QOL or is life-threatening, and weigh alternatives.

### B. GI bleeding management [new]

| # | Recommendation | Quality | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **B1** | **Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) is the first-line diagnostic test for suspected HHT-related bleeding.** Patients who meet colorectal cancer screening criteria, **and patients with *SMAD4*-HHT (genetically proven or suspected), should also undergo colonoscopy** | Low (82%) | Strong (94%) |
| **B2** | Consider **capsule endoscopy** for suspected HHT-related bleeding when EGD does not reveal significant HHT-related telangiectasia | Low (92%) | Strong (88%) |
| **B3** | **Grade the severity of HHT-related GI bleeding** using the framework below | Low (expert consensus) (96%) | Strong (96%) |
| **B4** | **Endoscopic argon plasma coagulation (APC) be used only sparingly** during endoscopy | Low (expert consensus) (88%) | Weak (81%) |
| **B5** | Consider treating **mild** HHT-related GI bleeding with **oral antifibrinolytics** | Low (94%) | Weak (90%) |
| **B6** | Consider treating **moderate to severe** HHT-related GI bleeding with **IV bevacizumab or other systemic antiangiogenic therapy** | Moderate (94%) | Strong (98%) |

**B3 severity framework — the decision input for B5 vs B6:**

| Stratum | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Mild** HHT-related GI bleeding | Patient who **meets their hemoglobin goal\*** with **oral iron replacement** |
| **Moderate** HHT-related GI bleeding | Patient who **meets their hemoglobin goal\*** with **intravenous iron treatment** |
| **Severe** HHT-related GI bleeding | Patient who **does not meet their hemoglobin goal\*** despite adequate iron replacement, **or requires blood transfusions** |

\* *Hemoglobin goals should reflect age, gender, symptoms, and comorbidities.* Hemoglobin levels are deliberately **not** specified — the classification reflects the patient's individual physiologic needs, and **applies to patients who have had at least 3 months of iron therapy**.

**Supporting commentary (condensed):**
- Several cross-sectional studies showed high diagnostic yield from EGD for upper GI telangiectasias in HHT with suspected GI bleeding; consider performing EGD in an experienced center given potential unusual complications during the procedure (such as massive epistaxis), and be aware of the precautions required for patients with HHT and pulmonary AVMs.
- **In suspected or proven *SMAD4*-HHT, screening colonoscopy is recommended starting at age 15 years and repeated every 3 years if no polyps are found, or every year along with EGD if colonic polyps are found.** Other patients with HHT (non-*SMAD4*) are screened for colon cancer following general population guidelines.
- Capsule endoscopy has high diagnostic yield and an excellent safety profile for small-bowel telangiectases; it **remains complementary to EGD** — use when anemia is unexplained by the severity of epistaxis and gastric involvement, or when EGD findings are negative.
- Case series describe a severity range for HHT-related GI bleeding with secondary anemia, reduced QOL, blood transfusion requirements, hospitalization, morbidity, and mortality.
- APC is best administered **concurrent with the initial endoscopic evaluation** and for **significant (1- to 3-mm) nonbleeding lesions**. **Repeated sessions are discouraged** to avoid repeated iatrogenic injury to the intestinal mucosa.
- One case series reported reduced need for endoscopic management with oral tranexamic acid, with a good safety profile.
- Small uncontrolled series showed systemic antiangiogenic therapies alleviated anemia, reduced transfusion requirements, or improved QOL.

### C. Anemia and anticoagulation [new]

| # | Recommendation | Quality | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **C1** | **Test for iron deficiency and anemia** in: **all adults, regardless of symptoms**; and **all children with recurrent bleeding and/or symptoms of anemia** | High (98%) | Strong (96%) |
| **C2** | **Iron replacement** for iron deficiency and anemia: **initial therapy with oral iron**; **IV iron** for patients in whom oral is not effective, not absorbed, or not tolerated, or who are presenting with severe anemia | Moderate (88%) | Strong (100%) |
| **C3** | **Red blood cell transfusion** in these settings: hemodynamic instability/shock; comorbidities requiring a higher hemoglobin target; need to increase the hemoglobin acutely (such as prior to surgery or during pregnancy); inability to maintain an adequate hemoglobin despite frequent iron infusions | Low (92%) | Strong (96%) |
| **C4** | **Evaluate for additional causes of anemia** when the response to iron replacement is inadequate | Low (100%) | Strong (100%) |
| **C5** | HHT patients **receive anticoagulation (prophylactic or therapeutic) or antiplatelet therapy when there is an indication**, with consideration of their individualized bleeding risks; **bleeding in HHT is not an absolute contraindication to these therapies** | Low (98%) | Strong (98%) |
| **C6** | **Avoid dual antiplatelet therapy and/or the combination of antiplatelet therapy and anticoagulation**, where possible, in patients with HHT | Low (expert consensus) (83%) | Weak (92%) |

**Supporting commentary (condensed):**
- Testing typically includes complete blood count and ferritin. **If a patient is anemic but ferritin is not reduced, measure serum iron, total iron-binding capacity, and transferrin saturation, and consider hematology consultation.**
- Oral iron typically starts at **35 to 65 mg of elemental iron once daily, 2 hours before or 1 hour after meals**. A hemoglobin increase of **less than 10 g/L is considered inadequate** in anemic patients; then try **every-other-day dosing or an alternate preparation**. In refractory anemia or severe chronic bleeding, **regularly scheduled iron infusions** may be required. Initial IV iron dosing can be calculated, or a **total initial dose of 1 g of IV iron** can be given as a single infusion or in divided doses.
- Hemoglobin targets and transfusion thresholds should be **individualized** by symptoms, severity of ongoing HHT-related bleeding, response to other therapies, comorbidity, and acuity.
- C4 evaluation should include folate, vitamin B₁₂, mean corpuscular volume, thyroid-stimulating hormone, smear, reticulocyte count, and work-up for hemolysis; refer to hematology in unresolved cases.
- Two case series plus expert consensus showed anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy is **well tolerated by most patients with HHT**. When anticoagulation is pursued, **unfractionated heparin, low-molecular-weight heparin, and vitamin K antagonists are preferred over direct-acting oral anticoagulants**, which are less well tolerated in HHT. In atrial fibrillation, if anticoagulation is not tolerated, alternate approaches such as left atrial appendage closure can be considered.
- If dual or combination therapy is required, **minimize duration and monitor closely**.

### D. Liver VMs in HHT

**D1–D6 [new]**

| # | Recommendation | Quality | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D1** | **Screening for liver VMs be offered to adults with definite or suspected HHT** | Low (84%) | Weak (93%) |
| **D2** | **Diagnostic testing for liver VMs** in HHT patients with symptoms and/or signs suggestive of **complicated liver VMs** — heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, abnormal cardiac biomarkers, abnormal liver function tests, abdominal pain, portal hypertension, or encephalopathy — **using Doppler ultrasound, multiphase contrast computed tomography (CT) scan, or contrast abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** | High (98%) | Strong (100%) |
| **D3** | **Intensive first-line management only for patients with complicated and/or symptomatic liver VMs, tailored to the type of liver VM complication(s)** | Moderate (88%) | Strong (88%) |
| **D3 (cont.)** | HHT patients with **high-output cardiac failure and pulmonary hypertension be comanaged by the HHT Center of Excellence and an HHT cardiologist or a pulmonary hypertension specialty clinic** | Moderate (88%) | Strong (88%) |
| **D4** | **Estimate prognosis of liver VMs using available predictors**, to identify patients in need of closer monitoring | Moderate (89%) | Strong (82%) |
| **D5** | Consider **IV bevacizumab** for patients with **symptomatic high-output cardiac failure due to liver VMs who have failed to respond sufficiently to first-line management** | Moderate (98%) | Strong (98%) |
| **D6** | **Referral for consideration of liver transplantation** for patients with symptomatic complications of liver VMs — specifically **refractory high-output cardiac failure, biliary ischemia, or complicated portal hypertension** | Moderate (83%) | Strong (92%) |

**D7–D8 [First Guidelines, not reassessed]**

| # | Recommendation | Level | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **D7** | **Liver biopsy be avoided in any patient with proven or suspected HHT** (agreement 97%) | III | Strong |
| **D8** | **Hepatic artery embolization be avoided in patients with liver VMs**, as it is **only a temporizing procedure associated with significant morbidity and mortality** (94%) | III | Strong |

**Supporting commentary (condensed):**
- Several cross-sectional diagnostic studies showed high yield and accuracy of Doppler ultrasonography, multiphase contrast CT, and MRI for detection of liver VMs. **Doppler ultrasonography severity grading was predictive of outcomes.** Anicteric cholestasis, reported in **one third** of patients with liver VMs, correlated with severity of liver VMs and complications.
- Rationale for screening (D1): awareness of liver VMs could improve subsequent patient management or help confirm the diagnosis of HHT. **Doppler ultrasonography is the imaging test of choice** because of its accuracy, safety, tolerability, low cost, and operating characteristics. Depending on local availability and expertise, and on patient preference, patients may instead be **screened clinically** (history, physical examination, blood work), or alternative imaging considered (multiphase contrast CT or MRI).
- Choice of imaging for D2 should be informed by the risk–benefit balance, local expertise, and availability or cost. **Contrast studies (CT and MRI) should be avoided if kidney dysfunction is present.** Echocardiography provides additional information about the hemodynamic effect of liver VMs. These tests are most informative when done in the context of a clinical assessment at an HHT center of excellence.
- One large series showed moderate response to first-line therapy tailored to the liver VM complication; expert consensus supported specialized center management. Symptomatic liver VMs are typically managed by an expert team at an HHT center of excellence, **with at least annual follow-up**.
- Three observational studies identified clinical predictors of complications from liver VMs (D4); monitor on the basis of estimated prognosis.
- Small uncontrolled series showed IV bevacizumab improved cardiac output or clinical symptoms in **80% of patients with severe liver VMs**, primarily in those with high-output cardiac failure. The **adverse event rate was 50 per 100 person-years, including 1 fatal event probably related to bevacizumab**.
- Small uncontrolled series of orthotopic liver transplantation for liver VMs in HHT showed **excellent 5- to 10-year survival (82% to 92%)**, with asymptomatic rare and late recurrence of liver VMs after transplantation. Timing for listing a symptomatic patient should be based on prognostic predictors and the severity of liver VM complications, including pulmonary hypertension. **Liver transplantation can be undertaken in the presence of pulmonary hypertension if pulmonary vascular resistance, estimated by right heart catheterization, is less than 3 Woods units.**

### E. Pediatric care [new]

| # | Recommendation | Quality | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **E1** | **Diagnostic genetic testing be offered for asymptomatic children of a parent with HHT** (the panel *advises*) | High (96%) | Strong (94%) |
| **E2** | **Screening for pulmonary AVMs in asymptomatic children with HHT or at risk for HHT at the time of presentation/diagnosis** | Moderate (94%) | Strong (94%) |
| **E3** | **Large pulmonary AVMs, and pulmonary AVMs associated with reduced oxygen saturation, be treated in children** to avoid serious complications | Moderate (98%) | Strong (98%) |
| **E4** | **Repeat pulmonary AVM screening in asymptomatic children** with HHT or at risk for HHT, **typically at 5-year intervals** | Low (92%) | Strong (86%) |
| **E5** | **Screening for brain VM in asymptomatic children** with HHT or at risk for HHT, **at the time of presentation/diagnosis** | Low (86%) | Strong (86%) |
| **E6** | **Brain VMs with high-risk features be treated** | Low (100%) | Strong (98%) |

**Supporting commentary (condensed):**
- Two cross-sectional diagnostic studies showed genetic testing can identify subclinical or presymptomatic disease in children of HHT families with a known mutation. **An affected family member should be tested first to determine the causative mutation, before testing an asymptomatic child who does not meet the clinical diagnostic criteria for HHT (Curaçao criteria).** Benefits, alternatives, pros, and cons should be discussed with children or — as appropriate — their parents.
- Pediatric case series showed a prevalence of pulmonary AVMs similar to that in adults and a risk for life-threatening complications with good outcomes from embolization. **Screening may be performed with either chest radiography and pulse oximetry or transthoracic contrast echocardiography. Screening with CT is not recommended, although chest CT remains the confirmatory diagnostic test when screening tests have positive findings.**
- **Pulmonary AVMs with feeding arteries at least 3 mm in diameter are suitable for embolotherapy.** Follow-up is indicated to detect recanalization and reperfusion of treated AVMs and growth of small untreated AVMs; specific protocols (CT, oximetry, or transthoracic contrast echocardiography) vary among centers, as do intervals.
- Screening is typically repeated **every 5 years after negative findings**. In children with indeterminate or borderline screening results based on either imaging or oximetry, **screening should be repeated sooner**. One case series showed growth of pulmonary AVMs during childhood.
- Case series showed risk for intracranial hemorrhage from brain VMs in children; MRI is a sensitive diagnostic test, and surgical and endovascular management carry benefits but also significant risk. **First-line screening is MRI (contrast-enhanced is more sensitive)** to identify brain VMs and determine subtype and risk factors for hemorrhage; this typically requires sedation or anesthesia in young children. The decision to treat versus observe is based on risk of treatment versus risk for hemorrhage, so the decision to screen the child should be shared among clinicians, caregivers, and the child where possible. Practice differs across countries. **Patient representatives felt strongly that children should be screened for brain VMs** and cited anecdotal evidence of disastrous outcomes in unscreened patients.
- Children with HHT who have brain VMs should be referred to a center with multidisciplinary expertise in neurovascular disease management. Treated brain VMs require close follow-up; **follow-up for small (untreated) brain VMs is not well defined.**

### F. Pregnancy and delivery [new]

| # | Recommendation | Quality | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **F1** | **Discuss preconception and prenatal diagnostic options, including preimplantation genetic diagnosis, with HHT-affected individuals** | Very low (86%) | Strong (83%) |
| **F2** | **Testing with unenhanced MRI in pregnant women with symptoms suggestive of brain VMs** | Very low (98%) | Strong (92%) |
| **F3** | **Pregnant women with HHT who have not been recently screened and/or treated for pulmonary AVM** be approached as below | Moderate (88%) | Strong (83%) |
| **F4** | **Pregnant women with HHT be managed at a tertiary care center by a multidisciplinary team** if they have untreated pulmonary AVMs and/or brain VMs, or have not been recently screened for pulmonary AVMs | Very low (94%) | Strong (85%) |
| **F5** | **Not withholding an epidural because of a diagnosis of HHT**, and that **screening for spinal vascular malformations is not required** | Low (98%) | Strong (92%) |
| **F6** | **Women with known, non–high-risk brain VMs can labor and proceed with vaginal delivery.** Patients may require an **assisted second stage on a case-by-case basis** | Moderate (94%) | Strong (94%) |

**F3 — the three-part approach:**
- **Asymptomatic patients:** initial pulmonary AVM screening performed using **either agitated saline transthoracic contrast echocardiography (TTCE) or low-dose noncontrast chest CT**, depending on local expertise. **Chest CT, when performed, should be done early in the second trimester.**
- **Patients with symptoms suggestive of pulmonary AVM:** diagnostic testing performed using **low-dose noncontrast chest CT**. This testing **can be performed at any gestational age**, as clinically indicated.
- **Pulmonary AVMs should be treated starting in the second trimester** unless otherwise clinically indicated.

**Supporting commentary (condensed):**
- Once the causative familial mutation is identified in an affected parent, it can be screened for in future offspring. Available options — preimplantation, postconception, and postdelivery testing — vary internationally; the discussion is influenced by local legislation on preimplantation diagnosis and termination of pregnancy.
- For symptomatic patients, including those with previous cerebral hemorrhage, **MRI without gadolinium should be planned in the second trimester. Asymptomatic patients do not require routine screening during pregnancy.**
- Case series showed elevated risk for complications from pulmonary AVMs during pregnancy and low risk of imaging and embolization in the second trimester. Technique for embolization should include measures to reduce fetal radiation exposure: **avoidance of fluoroscopy over the abdomen and pelvis, use of pulsed or low-dose fluoroscopy, minimization of angiography runs, and tight collimation. For both CT and angiography, abdominal shielding is not helpful and may in fact increase scattered radiation to the fetus.**
- Pregnant women with untreated pulmonary AVMs or brain VMs, as well as those who have not been screened, should be considered high risk for hemorrhagic and neurologic complications and managed accordingly by a high-risk team with HHT expertise.
- Two case series showed no evidence of hemorrhagic complications from epidural or spinal anesthesia. **Patients should meet with an anesthetist during the early third trimester** to discuss anesthesia options; risks for complications from spinal VMs during epidural anesthesia are unsubstantiated and only theoretical.
- Two case series showed no intracranial hemorrhage during delivery from brain VMs in patients with HHT. If a brain VM has not previously ruptured, patients may proceed with method of delivery based on obstetric indications and discussion with the obstetric care provider; **vaginal delivery is not contraindicated. Patients with "high-risk" brain VMs should be considered for cesarean section or epidural to allow passive descent of the presenting part**, with consideration of an assisted second stage. **Diligent management of blood pressure is imperative** in these higher-risk cases, and obtaining the opinion of a multidisciplinary neurovascular team is prudent.

### G. Diagnosis of HHT [First Guidelines, not reassessed]

| # | Recommendation | Level | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| **G1** | **Diagnose HHT using the Curaçao criteria or by identification of a causative mutation** (agreement 82%) | III | Weak |
| **G2** | **Consider the diagnosis of HHT in patients with one or more Curaçao criteria** (91%) | III | Weak |
| **G3** | **Asymptomatic children of a parent with HHT be considered to have possible HHT, unless excluded by genetic testing** (87%) | III | Weak |
| **G4** | **Refer patients for diagnostic genetic testing for HHT** (80%) — (1) to identify the causative mutation in a family with clinically confirmed HHT; (2) to establish a diagnosis in relatives of a person with a known causative mutation, including (a) individuals who are asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic and (b) individuals who desire prenatal testing; and (3) to assist in establishing a diagnosis of HHT in individuals who **do not meet clinical diagnostic criteria** | III | Weak |
| **G5** | For individuals who **test negative for *ENG* and *ACVRL1* coding sequence mutations, *SMAD4* testing should be considered** to identify the causative mutation (93%) | III | Weak |

> **This document does not print the four Curaçao criteria themselves.** It states that they were published in 2000 and reproduces them only in Table 1 of its Supplement 1, which is not part of this file; the primary paper is Shovlin et al. (reference 4 of this guideline: *Am J Med Genet* 2000;91:66–7). What this document *does* supply is the operative decision rule — **diagnosis by Curaçao criteria OR by a causative mutation (G1); one or more criteria should prompt consideration of the diagnosis (G2)** — plus the genetic-testing pathway in G3–G5.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Liver VM screening — a live disagreement with [[acg-2020-hepatic-mesenteric-circulation]], and both documents are 2020.** HHT 2020 **D1 offers screening for liver VMs to all adults with definite or suspected HHT** (weak, low quality); ACG 2020 recommends **against** routinely screening asymptomatic patients with HHT for liver VMs, holding that no evidence shows benefit to outcome or mortality (strong, low quality). Both are tier-1; ACG appeared in *Am J Gastroenterol* 2020;115:18–40 and HHT in *Ann Intern Med* 2020;173:989–1001 — **same publication year, so neither supersedes the other by date.** The two are carried side by side on [[hereditary-hemorrhagic-telangiectasia]] rather than collapsed. Note HHT 2020's own hedge: the screen may be **clinical** (history, examination, blood work) where Doppler ultrasonography is not available, and its stated rationale is partly diagnostic confirmation rather than outcome improvement.
- **Hepatic artery embolization.** HHT 2020 **D8 avoids it in any patient with liver VMs**, as only a temporizing procedure with significant morbidity and mortality. ACG 2020 is narrower — it proscribes embolization/ligation specifically in patients with biliary disease or portal hypertension and places it on an escalation ladder before transplantation. HHT 2020 is the broader prohibition; both are stated on the entity page.

- **Hemoglobin goals are deliberately unspecified** in the B3 severity framework and in C3 — the document states this is intentional, to reflect the individual patient's physiology, not an omission.
- Most carried-forward First-Guideline recommendations (A7–A10, D7–D8, G1–G5, H1–H4, I1–I5) rest on **level III evidence**; the panel did not reassess them in this cycle and flags them for updating in the next process.
