## Summary

The ASGE Standards of Practice Committee document that sets the **framework for granting, renewing and withdrawing privileges to perform gastrointestinal endoscopy**, and the one place the society prints a **minimum procedure number for each endoscopic skill** before competency is assessed. It replaces a previous ASGE statement on competency and privileging by nonphysician endoscopists; pediatric modifications are in a separate ASGE guideline.

Its governing tenet is stated twice and matters more than the table: **performance of a specific number of procedures does not guarantee competency.** Competency is to be assessed by **objective criteria and direct observation**, using validated assessment tools where applicable, **once the trainee has reached the evidence-based threshold**. The numbers are therefore **floors below which competency cannot be assessed**, not targets at which it is conferred — and the guideline says explicitly that evidence-based thresholds **should not be assessed below**.

Evidence is graded on **GRADE quality of evidence only** (high ⊕⊕⊕⊕ / moderate ⊕⊕⊕○ / low ⊕⊕○○ / very low ⊕○○○); strength is conveyed by wording — **"we recommend"** for stronger, **"we suggest"** for weaker.

### Per-procedure competency detail

- **Upper endoscopy** — the 130 should include **25 for treatment of nonvariceal hemorrhage and 20 for treatment of variceal hemorrhage**. Minimum performance goal: **successful intubation of the esophagus and pylorus in over 95% of examinations**. Beyond mucosal inspection and biopsy, the practitioner is expected to **competently control variceal and nonvariceal hemorrhage and perform esophageal dilation, snare polypectomy and PEG tube placement**. **Separate credentialing and monitoring are required** for pneumatic balloon dilation, stent placement, radiofrequency ablation, complex polypectomy, cap- or band-assisted EMR, endoscopic submucosal dissection and per-oral endoscopic myotomy.
- **Colonoscopy** — **275** recommended, but many endoscopists do not reach technical competency despite completing this number, and **some suggest 500 may be required to consistently achieve 90% cecal intubation for some trainees**. Technical competency = **cecum reached in over 90% of examinations**. **Cecal intubation rate alone may be an inadequate marker of overall technical competence** because it does not reflect examination quality. Expected basic skills: full colonic examination **including a retroflexed view of the rectum**, evidenced by an **adenoma detection rate above recommended thresholds**; targeted biopsy; snare polypectomy; hemostasis. **Stricture dilation, stent placement and endoscopic submucosal dissection require separate credentialing.**
- **Flexible sigmoidoscopy** — **30**; competency implies full examination of the left colon, retroflexed rectal view, targeted biopsies. For endoscopists who **do not routinely perform colonoscopy, at least 20 of these should be under direct supervision**, with **consistent insertion depth beyond 50 cm**. **Competency in colonoscopy implies competency in flexible sigmoidoscopy.** Hemorrhoidal banding and stent placement need further training and separate credentialing.
- **ERCP** — at least **200 supervised independent procedures** before competency is assessed; in addition a trainee should perform **at least 80 independent sphincterotomies and 60 biliary stent placements**. Competency-based decisions rest on **selective cannulation in at least 90% of procedures**, accurate interpretation of endoscopic and radiologic images, and successful sphincterotomy and stent placement when necessary. Learning curves are substantial, especially for **selective biliary cannulation of the native papilla**; at least one study suggests consistent cannulation of the native papilla **may not occur before the end of a 3-year fellowship, or even after a year of advanced endoscopy training**. A **grading scale for ERCP by procedural difficulty** has been adopted by the ASGE in its quality assessment document.
- **EUS** — current training guidelines recommend **at least 150 supervised procedures**, further recommended to include **a minimum of 50 EUS-guided FNAs, 75 pancreaticobiliary cases, 75 mucosal cancer staging cases, and evaluation of 40 subepithelial lesions**. However, one study concluded **150 cases might be inadequate and recommended at least 225 hands-on cases** — the figure carried in Table 3.
- **Capsule endoscopy** — consensus minimum **20 supervised procedures** to interpret small intestine capsule examinations independently. The endoscopist should already have substantial experience in recognising and managing endoscopic findings, plus familiarity with the hardware and software. Direct observation or review of saved cases by a qualified preceptor can aid; **passing a formalized in-service examination, or achieving a 90% or greater correlation rate of significant findings with a credentialed provider, is a reasonable expectation**.
- **Deep enteroscopy** — no validated means of determining competency, but a recommended core curriculum exists. Endoscopists should **complete formal training requirements in upper endoscopy and colonoscopy before learning deep enteroscopy**, and competency in standard therapeutic maneuvers (argon plasma coagulation, injection sclerotherapy, tattooing, polypectomy, hydrostatic dilation) is expected. Learning-curve analysis among experts suggests **measurable improvement after 10 upper and 20 retrograde double-balloon enteroscopy cases** is required to ensure stable overtube intubation of the ileum.
- **EMR** — one study found **more than 20 EMR resections in the upper GI tract** are required to achieve competency; the recommendation is limited to Barrett's neoplasia. **Similar data for colonic EMR are lacking**, although **competency with colonoscopy and snare polypectomy for smaller lesions is required before training in advanced polypectomy**. Endoscopists should understand the role of EMR, management of adverse events, specimen retrieval, defect closure, and when further staging of neoplasia is required, before attempting resection; and should recognise the advantages of one technique over another among injection, cap-assisted and ligation-assisted resection.
- **Ablative techniques** (radiofrequency ablation, cryotherapy, argon plasma coagulation) — **no published minimum procedure threshold criteria for competency in any of these techniques.** Recommendations in the document apply specifically to endoscopic ablation in the esophagus. An initial study of radiofrequency ablation volume among 7 endoscopists suggested that **increased procedure volume was associated with higher complete eradication rates of intestinal metaplasia**; a more recent multicenter registry found that performance of ablation in **up to 30 patients reduced the number of sessions per patient** required for complete eradication of Barrett's esophagus.
- **Enteral stent placement** — **no standardized minimum number before assessing competency**, but higher procedure volumes with congruent skill acquisition during training are expected to produce improved outcomes. The endoscopist should have knowledge of available self-expandable metal stents and **basic knowledge in the use and interpretation of fluoroscopy** — institutions may require **separate fluoroscopy credentialing and privileging**. Must be able to identify and manage early, immediate and delayed adverse events (bleeding, perforation, stent migration, stent occlusion with tissue/tumor ingrowth or overgrowth), and have expertise in stricture dilation, ablation with therapies such as argon plasma coagulation, placement of additional stents, and retrieving or repositioning migrated stents.
- **Enteral feeding tube placement** — a minimum of **20 supervised PEG tubes** before competency can be assessed. **No established guidelines for a minimum number of PEG with jejunal extension or direct percutaneous endoscopic jejunostomy.** Direct percutaneous endoscopic jejunostomy is **technically more difficult, with increased risk of adverse events and overall lower technical success** than PEG. PEG with jejunal extension may require fluoroscopy, so fluoroscopy competency and credentialing are recommended.
- **Moderate sedation** — **20**. Competency assessed by direct observation of technical skills and airway management, and by web-based programs for cognitive skills. Credentialing and recredentialing should follow **individual site-of-service or state requirements**. Granting privileges may also require **Basic Life Support and Advanced Cardiac Life Support** documentation.

### Principles of initial credentialing (Box 1)

Credentialing should begin **after successful completion of a GI endoscopy training program**:

1. Determination of endoscopic competence should involve assessment of **both cognitive and technical components** of each procedure.
2. **Appropriate documentation** should be required for determination of competency in each procedure.
3. **Credentials and privileges should be determined independently for each type of endoscopic procedure.**
4. Credentialing for all procedures should require the ability to **perform common associated therapeutic modalities**.
5. **Documentation of continued competence** should be required for the renewal of endoscopic privileges.

### Uniformity of standards

- **Uniform standards should apply to all hospital staff requesting privileges to perform endoscopy, regardless of medical specialty**, and to all areas where endoscopy is performed. Criteria must be medically sound and applicable to all wishing to obtain privileges for each specific procedure.
- **Privileges should be granted independently for each major category of endoscopy.** The ability to perform one endoscopic procedure well **does not imply adequate competency to perform others**; associated skills considered integral to a category may be required before privileges for that category are granted.
- The credentialing process should **focus on the assurance of high-quality patient care and be free from political or economic pressures**. It is the responsibility of the service chief, or an individual in a comparable role, to recommend individuals for privileges.

### Terms (Table 2)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Clinical privileges** | Authorization by an institution to perform a particular procedure or clinical service |
| **Competence** | The minimum level of skill, knowledge and/or expertise derived through training and experience required to safely and proficiently perform a task or procedure |
| **Credentialing** | A process designed to assess and validate the qualifications of a licensed independent practitioner to provide patient care |
| **Credentials** | Documents provided after successful completion of a period of education or training as an indication of clinical competence |
| **Preceptor** | An endoscopist with clinical experience and appropriate credentials to train a preceptee in new techniques |
| **Preceptee** | An endoscopist who possesses sufficient experience to master a new procedure cognitively and technically |
| **Proctor** | An independent and unbiased endoscopist in a position to evaluate and monitor the skills and ability of another endoscopist |

### Emerging technologies — minor versus major skills

- **Minor skill** — a nonexperimental development that is a **minor extension of an accepted and widely available technique or procedure** (e.g. radiofrequency ablation of Barrett's esophagus, over-the-scope clips). Competency should involve **education and practical exposure** — short courses, training videos, endoscopic simulators, interactive computer programs. **Duration of training should not be fixed but should reflect the time needed for the participant to master it.** Technologic refinements in commonly used equipment (endoscopes, biopsy forceps, snares) **do not require formal training**.
- **Major skill** — a new technique or procedure that by its nature involves **a high level of complexity, interpretative ability, and/or new type of technology**. In the initial phases of dissemination, acquisition of competency in emerging technologies involving major skills — **per-oral endoscopic myotomy, endoscopic submucosal dissection** are the named examples — **should be confined to teaching centers and should require formal training**.
- **Completion of a short course or workshop offering limited exposure to cognitive background data or technical skills will not, by itself, result in clinical competency, and therefore should not be the sole mechanism for the acquisition of new major skills.** A **preceptorship or other vehicle of formal instruction will generally be considered mandatory**; the preceptor should supply **written documentation of its successful completion** for credentialing purposes. Preceptorship is complete when the preceptee achieves a level of competency allowing fully independent performance.
- The **US Food and Drug Administration does not regulate new procedures as it does new drugs and devices.** Significant procedural innovations should be **incorporated into institutional review board or regulatory oversight at an early stage** so patients are protected under federal research regulations; evaluation and implementation of new techniques is typically handled at a local institutional level.

### Proctoring

- **A proctor evaluates and monitors — a preceptor teaches.** The proctor's role is to act as **an independent and unbiased monitor**, not to teach. A proctor **has no physician or patient relationship with the patient being treated and does not participate directly in patient care**, so remains responsible to the institution and the credentialing process.
- The proctor should be **credentialed in the procedure being observed**, possess sufficient expertise to judge quality, and be **free of actual or perceived conflicts of interest** that could bias for or against the applicant. A proctor **should not receive a fee related directly to patient care while proctoring**, but may be compensated by the institution for time spent providing proctoring services.
- Direct observation for a **prespecified period of time or number of cases, as determined by institution-specific bylaws**. Applicants must demonstrate competency in **both diagnostic and therapeutic maneuvers** common to the procedures sought.
- To minimize liability the proctor should **(1) not interfere with the proctored endoscopist; (2) not offer advice or interact with the patient, other than personal introduction and stating the proctoring role; and (3) only report to the institution or regulatory body that the proctor is representing unless substandard medical care harmful to the patient is witnessed.**
- If substandard and detrimental care is witnessed, the proctor should first **contact an appropriate superior and ask the proctored endoscopist to stop** if possible. **Only as a last resort should the proctor actually intervene; when this occurs, appropriate documentation is imperative.** Where the proctored individual has an associate who also holds privileges in the procedure, it may be appropriate to ask the associate to be present and assist, to avoid any need for the proctor to intervene.
- **Any direct patient intervention by the proctor must be disclosed in the patient's chart** and in a confidential report to the credentials committee. The presence of a proctor **may or may not be discussed during informed consent**, and the proctor may not be present when informed consent is obtained; legal counsel should be consulted to take greatest advantage of **peer-review immunity available under state law, which varies from state to state**.
- Once complete, a **confidential written report** goes to the institution. Applicants subject to proctoring **retain all rights of appeal** under the credentialing process set by the institution.
- **When proctoring is appropriate:** applicants for new staff appointments; incumbent staff trained in additional or novel procedures; staff undergoing routine recredentialing or remediation; and **privileged incumbent medical staff who have insufficient procedural volume** for a given procedure. It may also be one of several actions when a potential practice problem is identified by quality improvement or risk management. **Provisions for failure to meet the minimum expected competency during proctorship should be outlined**, including recommendations for additional training or restriction of certain privileges and potential avenues for remediation.
- Guidelines for proctoring **should be specifically included in institutional bylaws** as an integral part of the credentialing and privileging process; in departmentalized institutions, bylaws may specify protocols at department level.

### Components to be evaluated during assessment of endoscopy competence (Table 4)

Reviews patient records and x-ray films · identifies potential risk factors · understands indications and contraindications · believes findings will influence management · obtains proper informed consent · uses appropriate sedation · intubates the GI tract with good technique · correctly identifies landmarks · conducts thorough examination · detects and identifies all pathologic conditions · completes the examination within a reasonable period · obtains tissue sample properly · performs therapeutic maneuvers successfully and effectively · recognizes and manages procedure-related adverse events · prepares an accurate report · plans correct management and disposition · discusses findings with patient, family and other healthcare providers · arranges proper follow-up, review of pathologic findings, and case outcome.

### Recredentialing and renewal

- Recredentialing **generally follows regional or state regulations but has been mandated by national accrediting organizations to occur every 2 to 3 years**.
- Institutions should **track outcome data and adverse event rates of individual endoscopists**, and require evidence of engagement in educational and clinical activities focused on continuous quality improvement, with **documentation of continued cognitive training**.
- Where minimal competency cannot be assured, mechanisms must exist for performance improvement and repriviliging — **proctoring, participation in continuing medical education, retraining, or limitation of privileges**.
- **Decredentialing** may be required for those who fail to meet accepted national and institutional requirements. A previously employed or decredentialed endoscopist may wish to pursue recredentialing later; although **there are no data on this situation**, the guideline **suggests** that a physician who has **not performed endoscopy for at least 12 months**, or was previously decredentialed for failure to meet appropriate national or institutional requirements, **should be proctored by at least 1 credentialed endoscopist** to document competency in the procedures requested.

### The six numbered recommendations

| # | Recommendation | Quality |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **We recommend** that training in endoscopic techniques must be adequate for each major category of endoscopy for which privileges are requested | high ⊕⊕⊕⊕ |
| 2 | **We recommend** that whenever possible competence should be determined by objective criteria and direct observations, and that **an arbitrary number of procedures does not guarantee competency**. However, minimal threshold numbers for initial credentialing may be set below which competency cannot be assessed | high ⊕⊕⊕⊕ |
| 3 | **We suggest** that uniform standards be developed that apply to all hospital staff requesting privileges to perform endoscopy, regardless of medical specialty | low ⊕⊕○○ |
| 4 | **We recommend** documentation of continuing satisfactory performance using quality benchmark data that should allow an institution to renew the privileges of an endoscopist for that procedure | moderate ⊕⊕⊕○ |
| 5 | **We recommend** that guidelines for proctoring be included in institutional bylaws to assist in the credentialing and privileging process | moderate ⊕⊕⊕○ |
| 6 | **We recommend** that individual institutions have mechanisms in place for addressing instances when minimal competence cannot be ensured, including proctoring, participation in continuing medical education offerings, retraining, or limitation of privileges | moderate ⊕⊕⊕○ |

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Internally acknowledged tension between the table and the text.** Table 3 gives **275 for colonoscopy** while the text states **500 may be required for some trainees** to consistently reach 90% cecal intubation; and gives **225 for EUS** while noting current training guidelines recommend **150**. Both figures are the document's and both are on the page — the table number is the credentialing floor, the higher number the realistic learning curve.
- **The whole table is deliberately not a competency standard.** Recommendation 2 states that an arbitrary number **does not guarantee competency**; the numbers are floors below which competency cannot be assessed. Rendering any of them as "the number needed to be competent" would reverse the document's meaning.
- **Four skills have no minimum number at all** — ablative techniques (no published threshold criteria for any of them), enteral stent placement (no standardized minimum), PEG with jejunal extension and direct percutaneous endoscopic jejunostomy. The page says this plainly rather than borrowing a number.
- **No contradiction with [[asge-acg-2024-common-endoscopy-quality-indicators]] or the procedure-specific quality-indicator documents** — those measure performance after privileging; this one governs the grant of privileges. The ERCP ≥90% selective cannulation figure and the colonoscopy ≥90% cecal intubation figure are consistent with the quality-indicator series, though the 2024 colonoscopy document raised its own cecal intubation benchmark to **≥95%**; where a unit is auditing performance, the newer quality-indicator target governs.
