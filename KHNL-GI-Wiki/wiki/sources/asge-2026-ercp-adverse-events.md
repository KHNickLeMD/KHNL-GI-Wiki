## Summary

An update of the 2017 ASGE document "Adverse Events Associated with ERCP". Its purpose is to provide evidence-based summaries of adverse event (AE) rates and risk factors — **it is descriptive and makes no graded recommendations**, and it explicitly does not cover diagnosis or management of AEs in detail, nor the evidence-based mitigation interventions, both of which ASGE has published separately.

AEs are presented across four procedure categories — **routine (primarily biliary) ERCP · ERCP with pancreatic interventions · ERCP with cholangioscopy or pancreatoscopy · ERCP in special populations** — and within each, by AE type: **pancreatitis, bleeding, cholangitis, cholecystitis, perforation**, plus technique- or indication-specific events. Sedation-related and cardiorespiratory AEs were deliberately excluded, because rates specific to ERCP are hard to separate from those of advanced endoscopy generally.

Three additional topics were added at the request of the ASGE Governing Board: **unplanned healthcare encounters (UHEs) not related to traditional AEs**, **patient-reported outcome and experience measures (PROMs/PREMs)**, and **duodenoscope-related infections**. The UHE and PROM sections carry the document's most striking claim — that physician-defined AE definitions substantially **underestimate** the true burden of ERCP, with post-ERCP abdominal pain in **10% or more** of patients who have no pancreatitis, cholangitis or perforation at all.

The framing message is that ERCP has high AE and UHE rates relative to most other widely performed endoscopic procedures; a **clear indication should always be present**, safer or noninvasive alternatives always considered, and informed consent is key.

### Post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP)

- **Definition matters and varies.** Contemporary definitions use **2 of 3 Revised Atlanta criteria** — typical abdominal pain, pancreatic enzymes **≥3× the upper limit of normal**, characteristic imaging. The **1991 consensus definition additionally requires admission (or prolongation) of at least 2 nights**; the **2010 ASGE Lexicon does not**. This difference is itself a major source of heterogeneity in reported incidence.
- ⚠ **Asymptomatic enzyme elevation or incidental imaging findings occur in up to 20%–25% after ERCP.** PEP should be suspected or investigated **only when informed by the patient's clinical status**, except where the patient cannot communicate symptoms (critical care, incapacitated).
- Incidence: **5.5% all-comers, 7.0% first-time** (meta-analysis of 53,550 patients in 190 RCTs); lower in observational data (**4.1% and 5.2%**, 109 studies, 1,502,279 patients). Severity split across 128 trials: **mild 65.9%, moderate 27.9%, severe 10.2%**. **PEP leads to death in 2.6%**, concentrated in severe cases.
- Cumulative post-ERCP AE incidence approached **10.0% in all-comers and 12.0% in first-time patients** (244 RCTs, 136 observational studies); **death directly attributable to ERCP was 0.5%** from RCT data.

- **Difficult cannulation** is most commonly defined as **>5 papillary engagements, >5 minutes spent cannulating, >1 inadvertent pancreatic cannulation, and/or pancreatic contrast opacification**.
- **Prediction models exist but none is in wide use**; the document attributes this to generalizability, cumbersome data inputs, and a lack of actionable recommendations tied to predicted risk.
- **Trainee participation has not been consistently shown to be a risk factor.** Competence data: cannulation success ≥80% consistently achieved only after **400–500 native-papilla procedures**; in UK data, trainees completing ≥200 ERCPs succeeded without physical assistance in only **66%**; in a US multicenter study, at a median of **361** ERCPs only **55%** of trainees met competence metrics for native papilla cannulation.

### Bleeding

- Defined as **clinically significant** bleeding — hematemesis, melena or hematochezia with a **hemoglobin drop of ≥2 g/dL** or transfusion. Intraprocedural bleeding is not necessarily a true AE unless it alters the clinical trajectory.
- Incidence **1.5% all-comers, 1.9% first-time**. Mean onset **3.2 days**; **77.5% moderate, 9.3% severe, 2.3% fatal**.
- **Risk factors:** anticoagulant or thienopyridine antiplatelet use · active coagulopathy · cholangitis leading up to ERCP · chronic kidney disease · lower endoscopist volume. **Direct oral anticoagulants carry risk comparable to vitamin K antagonists.** Delayed bleeding is associated with sphincterotomy but **not** sphincteroplasty.
- **Not associated with higher bleeding odds:** aspirin (alone or with other antithrombotics), NSAIDs, prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin doses.
- ⚠ **The underestimated agent is antiplatelet therapy** — recent prospective data suggest its risks are underappreciated and its preprocedural management suboptimal, even where anticoagulants are managed appropriately.
- **Emergent ERCP in a coagulopathic patient:** **avoid sphincterotomy and/or sphincteroplasty in the index session** — biliary **stent placement without sphincterotomy** has been shown to be generally safe.
- Endoscopic options for significant intraprocedural bleeding: covered metal biliary stent, epinephrine, cauterization (probes or forceps), clips, hemostatic powder — **with care to avoid causing pancreatitis or biliary obstruction**. Otherwise principles mirror other upper GI bleeding.
- **Resumption of held antithrombotics after ERCP is highly variable practice with no high-quality evidence to guide it.**

### Cholangitis

- Incidence **2.5% all-comers** (71 studies, >120,000 patients); **1.5%** in a 2025 prospective study of >8,500 using strict definitions — of which **29.1% severe and 8.2% fatal**. Symptom onset most commonly **1–3 days** after ERCP; procedure-related cholangitis can theoretically occur **up to 14 days** out, beyond which it overlaps with stent dysfunction or underlying pathology.
- ⚠ **The best-established risk factor is incomplete biliary drainage** — surrogates being a biliary stricture, performance of sphincterotomy, and placement of a biliary stent (the latter two protective).
- Other risk factors: advanced age · male sex · post-liver-transplant status · [[primary-sclerosing-cholangitis|PSC]] · cholangioscopy · [[cholangiocarcinoma|cholangiocarcinoma]] · increasing procedure time · inpatient status. With CBD stones, **greater stone numbers**; with strictures, **more proximal location** (intrahepatic, hilar, or common hepatic vs distal CBD) and **malignant vs benign** type.
- **Prevention:** minimize nonsterile contrast injection into the biliary tree, **especially proximal to visualized strictures**; establish biliary drainage.
- **Antibiotics are currently recommended for patients with established risk factors for cholangitis and/or suspected incomplete decompression after ERCP** — with high suspicion, prompt recognition and **early broad-spectrum coverage** essential in such patients. ⚠ A recent meta-analysis of randomized trials demonstrated benefits of prophylactic antibiotics in **all** patients with biliary obstruction.
- **Management:** medical management and observation · repeat ERCP · percutaneous biliary drainage, depending on indication, biliary anatomy and presentation. **Culturing bile has been suggested** to guide targeted therapy. Choledochoduodenostomy with a lumen-apposing metal stent is increasingly considered but not specifically studied for this indication in any high-volume series.

### Cholecystitis

- Pooled incidence **0.8% all-comers, 1.1% first-time**; **0.4% and 1.5%** respectively in a 2024 prospective study of >4,400 with native gallbladders and strict definitions. Historically reported up to **15%** with SEMS placement.
- ⚠ **Meta-analysis of SEMS vs plastic stents in malignant distal biliary obstruction found no difference in cholecystitis rates.**
- Mechanism: intrinsic obstruction or extrinsic compression of the cystic duct, nonsterile contrast into the cystic duct/gallbladder, altered gallbladder motility. Proposed risk factors: **opacification of the gallbladder**, remnant gallstones, biliary and/or cystic duct tumor involvement. **Any** biliary stent — including side-by-side plastic stents — could theoretically precipitate it.
- **Open question the document poses:** whether SEMS or side-by-side plastic stents should be placed in patients with **native gallbladders** at all.
- **Management:** remove or replace indwelling stent(s) via repeat ERCP if present; **transpapillary, [[eus-guided-gallbladder-drainage|endosonography-guided]], or percutaneous gallbladder drainage**; cholecystectomy.

### Perforation

- Incidence **0.5% all-comers** (82 studies, >300,000 patients) and **0.6% first-time** (98 studies, >42,000). Guidewire penetrations are common and generally benign.
- **Stapfer classification** is the most widely used schema — I lateral/medial duodenal wall (duodenoscope tip) · II periampullary (after sphincterotomy or sphincteroplasty) · III distal bile duct · IV **retroperitoneal gas alone**. Full detail: [[endoscopic-management-of-perforation]].
- **Risk factors:** sphincter of Oddi dysfunction · sphincterotomy and sphincteroplasty — particularly **sphincterotomy/sphincteroplasty in patients with previous sphincterotomy** or other reasons for periampullary fibrosis · altered/postsurgical upper GI anatomy · luminal strictures · difficult biliary cannulation · intraductal balloon stricturoplasty.
- ⚠ **No specific technique has been reliably shown to reduce the risk of ERCP-related perforation** beyond general principles — early recognition is the lever.
- **Standard good practice:** careful examination of the sphincterotomy/sphincteroplasty and duodenal wall for perforation **before completing the ERCP**.
- **Management:** resuscitate and support → confirm or exclude with imaging, **preferably CT** (noncontrast may be more readily obtained depending on clinical status) → multidisciplinary approach (conservative, endoscopic, or surgical). When endoscopic management is attempted:
  - Use **CO₂, not room air**; **position the patient to divert intraluminal contents away from the defect**; consider a **nasogastric tube to suction**.
  - **Periampullary or intraductal perforation → biliary stent placement** can be effective; consider **PD stent placement to prevent PEP if a SEMS is being used in a patient with a normal pancreas**.
  - **Nonampullary luminal perforation ~10 mm or less → through-the-scope clips**; larger → **over-the-scope clips or suturing devices**; **defects ≥30 mm** may also be targeted with suturing or over-the-scope devices where availability, familiarity and expertise are present, though historically these are managed surgically.

### ERCP with pancreatic interventions

- PEP **16%–21%** in the ERCP arms of trials comparing early surgery with endoscopic therapy for symptomatic main PD stones (<100 patients total — the document warns of overestimation from small samples).
- **Stent-related AEs** (obstruction, migration, stent-related injury) occurred in **26%** after an ERCP-based approach in one RCT; stent migration **4.9%**, PD obstruction causing inflammation **3.7%** in another. **Chronic PD injury or stricture is well described, especially where stents are larger than the caliber of the existing PD.**
- SEMS vs plastic stents for chronic pancreatitis PD strictures: PEP similar (19.2% vs 20.4%), cholangitis 3.8% vs 3.7%, perforation 0.0% vs 1.9%, no clinically significant bleeding in either; stent dysfunction 7.7% vs 3.7%; spontaneous migration **26.9% vs 3.7%**. ⚠ **De novo main PD strictures developed in 23.1% of the fully covered SEMS group and in none of the plastic stent group** — a proposed mechanism being radial force causing ischemic injury.
- ⚠ **Pancreatic SEMS placement is generally not recommended**, since higher rates of most AEs occur with fully covered SEMS compared with single or multiple plastic PD stents.
- **Pancreas divisum:** PEP **18.1%**, bleeding 1.4%, delayed AEs including stent occlusion **19.3%** and papillary restenosis **10.7%**; a 2025 meta-analysis of 27 studies put the overall combined AE rate at **71%**. ⚠ These figures rest on composite per-patient outcomes and overestimate risk — a methodologically rigorous recent randomized trial found serious AEs within 30 days in only **15%** after minor papillotomy. See [[pancreas-divisum]].

### ERCP with cholangioscopy or pancreatoscopy

- **Cholangioscopy is considered safe overall, but its AE profile is distinct from routine ERCP — notably infection and pancreatitis.**
- PEP **2% (95% CI, 2–3)** within an overall AE rate of **7%** for difficult stones or indeterminate strictures; **7.1%** among PSC patients in a prospective study; **4.6%** and **2.5%** in pancreatoscopy cohorts for PD stones. Combined AEs **12%–14%** in two recent meta-analyses of pancreatoscopy with lithotripsy, of which 8.7% PEP.
- **Cholangitis is the signature risk.** Infectious AE incidence **1%–12%**, bacteremia of uncertain significance **13.9%**. Cholangitis was **1.0% vs 0.2% (OR 4.98)** for cholangiopancreatoscopy vs conventional ERCP in ~4,000 chronic pancreatitis patients. ⚠ **Antibiotic prophylaxis was associated with lower cholangitis rates after cholangioscopy — 12% vs 1%.** Evidence is conflicting, but patients undergoing cholangioscopy are generally considered high risk, and **periprocedural antibiotics are currently suggested by some endoscopic societies** in these patients.
- **Bleeding:** no compelling data showing higher clinically significant bleeding than standard ERCP, despite the plausible mechanism of direct duct-wall trauma with lithotripsy.
- **Perforation rates appear similar to conventional ERCP.**

### ERCP in special populations

- **Pediatric:** pooled PEP **4% (95% CI, 3–6)** — similar to adults. Plausible contributors to a higher expected rate: anatomically smaller ducts, lack of dedicated pediatric scopes and devices, and a smaller evidence base for NSAID safety in children leading to reduced use.
- **Pregnancy:** **pregnancy is an independent risk factor for PEP — 12% vs 5%, P < .001** (matched study, >3,600 patients). Pooled combined maternal and fetal ERCP-related AEs **15.9% (95% CI, 13.2–19.1)**. ⚠ **Rectal NSAIDs have traditionally been avoided** in pregnancy because of potential ductus arteriosus constriction and premature closure; patient positioning may also differ because of the gravid uterus. **Despite these risks, endoscopists should not avoid ERCP in pregnant patients when there is an appropriate indication** — gallstone pancreatitis and cholangitis can themselves cause significant maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality.
- **Surgically altered anatomy:** balloon-assisted ERCP (single- or double-balloon enteroscope) helps reach the papilla but is typically performed with forward-viewing scopes; **despite the difficulty of cannulating an often-reversed or distorted papilla, the overall AE profile is comparable to standard ERCP**. Other options are laparoscopic-assisted ERCP and endosonography-directed transgastric ERCP.

### Unplanned healthcare encounters (UHEs)

- **UHE = any emergency department visit and/or unplanned hospital admission**, measurable at 7, 14 or 30 days after the index ERCP. It captures what AE definitions miss: cardiopulmonary AEs, abdominal pain not attributable to a defined AE, nausea without discernible cause, unplanned repeat ERCPs, unplanned procedures for missed pathology.
- ⚠ **Post-ERCP abdominal pain occurs in 10% or more of patients in the absence of pancreatitis, cholangitis or perforation.** Up to **12%** reported moderate or severe pain at discharge without any clearly defined AE. Causes include gas insufflation (**especially at centers using room air instead of CO₂**), musculoskeletal pain from positioning during long procedures, residual sedation effects, exacerbation of pre-existing functional pain, peritoneal irritation from bile leakage or guidewire penetration, and stent-related pain.
- **Unplanned repeat ERCP** is most often due to premature stent occlusion or migration, or retained/newly migrated stones. Premature occlusion windows used in studies: **30, 60 or 90 days for metal stents; 30 days for smaller-caliber plastic stents**.

### PROMs and PREMs

- **Physician-defined AEs in aggregate fail to capture the morbidity associated with ERCP.** Imaging-based PEP diagnosis concords poorly with the consensus definition, and physician-defined criteria miss patients with shorter hospital stays for abdominal pain — up to **10%** of patients undergoing ERCP.
- **PAN-PROMISE** — 7 questions (pain, abdominal distention, difficulty eating, difficulty with bowel movements, thirst, nausea/vomiting, weakness). An increase of **≥7 points** on the 7-day patient-reported tolerability scale over baseline quality-of-life status was strongly correlated with UHE.
- **PRO-STEP** — a simple Likert-based PREM applied at discharge, measuring intraprocedural pain and awareness and postprocedural pain, nausea and distention. **Elevated PRO-STEP scores correlate with UHE and with post-ERCP AEs including PEP and perforation.**
- **Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy–Hepatobiliary** is the only validated biliary-specific PROM, but measures quality of life in hepatobiliary cancers.

### Duodenoscope-related infections

- The rate of **clinically significant** duodenoscope-related infection is estimated at only **0.01%**, but outbreak potential has made infection control in ERCP a declared research priority. Duodenoscopes and linear echoendoscopes share an elevator mechanism that is extremely difficult to disinfect by standard reprocessing. Novel designs have emerged; **cost-effectiveness of routine use remains uncertain**. See [[endoscope-reprocessing]].

### Sustainability (ASGE suggestions)

1. **Avoiding ERCPs that are not clinically indicated** reduces both AEs and environmental burden — ERCP generates substantially more waste and a higher carbon footprint than routine procedures.
2. Among high-risk outpatients presenting for ERCP, **1 in 5 requires unplanned hospitalization within 30 days**; a hospital admission is estimated at **30–50 kg CO₂e per patient per day**, roughly equivalent to driving 100 miles in a gasoline-powered car.
3. **Single-use duodenoscopes** mitigate infection risk but increase net waste by **~40%** and carry a **25- to 70-fold higher carbon footprint** than reusable duodenoscopes; the upfront safety benefit is offset by indirect environmental and public-health harms.

## Key Findings / Claims

- **ERCP AE rates have not improved.** Despite advances in technique, early recognition and management, **rates of pancreatitis have remained relatively consistent or even increased over recent decades**.
- **The three evidence-based PEP mitigation strategies and their effect sizes** — prolonged IV hydration (8–24 h, RR 0.50), rectal NSAIDs (0.49), PD stent (0.56, **benefit present regardless of NSAID use**).
- **Absence of chronic pancreatitis is a PEP risk factor** (OR 1.73–1.87) — a counterintuitive point worth carrying.
- **Incomplete biliary drainage is the dominant cholangitis risk factor**, and sphincterotomy and biliary stent placement are both protective.
- **Pancreatic fully covered SEMS caused de novo main PD strictures in 23.1% vs 0% with plastic stents**, and pancreatic SEMS placement is generally not recommended.
- **ERCP's true burden is larger than its AE rate suggests** — ≥10% have post-ERCP abdominal pain with no defined AE, and 1 in 5 high-risk outpatients has an unplanned hospitalization within 30 days.
- Costs of post-ERCP AEs often exceed **$10,000 per admission**, totalling an estimated **$200+ million in annual US healthcare spending**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- ⚠ **Antibiotic prophylaxis before ERCP for biliary obstruction.** This document reports a **recent meta-analysis of randomized trials demonstrating benefit in *all* patients with biliary obstruction**, while stating that antibiotics are *currently* recommended only for those with established cholangitis risk factors or suspected incomplete decompression. [[asge-2015-antibiotic-prophylaxis]] restricts prophylaxis to anticipated incomplete drainage. Prophylaxis targeted at risk factors and suspected incomplete decompression is the position carried on [[ercp]] and [[acute-cholangitis]], with the newer meta-analytic signal noted beside it.
- **Cholangioscopy antibiotic prophylaxis is described as conflicting evidence** — one retrospective study showed 12% vs 1% cholangitis — with "some endoscopic societies" suggesting it. No unified position.
- **SEMS and cholecystitis:** historical reports up to 15% versus a meta-analysis finding no difference from plastic stents. The document leaves open whether SEMS or side-by-side plastic stents should be used in patients with native gallbladders.
- **Resumption of antithrombotics after ERCP has no high-quality evidence** and practice is highly variable — an explicit non-recommendation.
- **PEP definitions differ between the 1991 consensus (requires ≥2-night admission) and the 2010 ASGE Lexicon (does not)**, which changes measured incidence. Compare [[suzuki-2021-pep-acute-pancreatitis-criteria]], which quantified the same problem for Cotton vs Revised Atlanta criteria.
- No PEP risk-prediction model is in clinical use despite several being developed; the document names this a research priority rather than endorsing one.
