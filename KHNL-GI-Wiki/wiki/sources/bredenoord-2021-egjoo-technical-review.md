## Summary

Technical review supporting the esophagogastric junction outflow obstruction (EGJOO) section of the Chicago Classification version 4.0 (CCv4.0). It reviews the literature behind the CCv4.0 changes and presents eight statements voted on and endorsed by the International High-Resolution Manometry (HRM) Working Group, each with percent agreement, strength of recommendation and (for most) level of evidence.

The problem the document sets out to solve: under CCv3.0, EGJOO rested on a single metric — a median integrated relaxation pressure (IRP) above the 95th percentile of healthy controls — with preserved peristalsis. By construction, 5% of *all* patients studied by HRM therefore carry a numerically elevated IRP of no clinical meaning. Reported EGJOO prevalence across cohorts totalling >20,000 HRM studies ranges 5%–24%; inter-rater agreement on the HRM diagnosis was only fair (κ = 0.45, 95% CI 0.41–0.49); 32%–94% of EGJOO patients in follow-up series needed no therapy at all, and symptoms often resolved with observation. Patients were nonetheless undergoing computed tomography (CT), endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and even lower esophageal sphincter (LES)–ablative procedures on the strength of the number.

CCv4.0's response is to make the diagnosis **harder to reach and explicitly two-tiered**. A manometric diagnosis of EGJOO is *always* clinically inconclusive on its own. It now requires three things together — elevated median IRP, ≥20% of supine wet swallows with elevated intrabolus pressure (IBP), and persistently elevated median IRP after a change of position — with evidence of peristalsis. Any one of those findings in isolation is explicitly inconclusive. A *clinically relevant conclusive* diagnosis additionally requires compatible symptoms (dysphagia and/or non-cardiac chest pain — reflux symptoms do not count) plus an abnormal supportive test (timed barium esophagram, preferably with a tablet, and/or functional lumen imaging probe).

Much of the review is an account of **why an isolated elevated IRP is so often wrong**: solid-state catheter pressure drift that thermal compensation can make worse rather than better; catheter impingement and sensor angulation inside a hiatal hernia sac; cardiac and vascular compression artifact, which is reduced in the upright position. It also sets out the natural history that governs management — progression to overt achalasia occurs in under 10%, bolus clearance is usually unaffected when peristalsis is intact, and 40% of patients respond to *sham* botulinum toxin injection and dilation — and concludes that irreversible treatment should be withheld, partly because myotomy or dilation alters the IRP and destroys the ability to prove achalasia later.

Supporting commentary the document attaches to the statements:

- **5.1.1** — manometry cannot distinguish idiopathic from secondary EGJOO; history and prior upper endoscopy are what raise suspicion of a secondary cause. With no secondary cause identified, the condition is called *idiopathic* EGJOO.
- **5.1.2** — the majority of isolated elevated IRPs are unrelated to the patient's symptoms, do not impair esophageal emptying, and have a favourable prognosis.
- **5.1.4** — **reflux symptoms are not clinically relevant** here. An elevated IRP is very unlikely to produce reflux symptoms; EGJOO found in a patient with reflux symptoms is an incidental, irrelevant finding.
- **5.2.1** — these isolated findings suggest the elevated IRP is a **positional artifact and can be ignored**.
- **5.3.1 / 5.3.2** — both rest on single-centre case series with no long-term follow-up or gold standard.

### Thresholds and test characteristics

- **Intrabolus pressure.** Normative studies put the upper limit of normal supine IBP (95th percentile of healthy volunteers) at **16–19.5 mmHg** across Manoscan, InSIGHT and MMS systems. Consistency with the historical perfused-manometry threshold makes a **conservative supine IBP threshold of 20 mmHg** a reliable value for clinical use. The threshold may vary with system technology (solid-state vs perfused), catheter diameter, posture and measurement methodology.
- **IBP is measured differently by each software.** Manoview defines IBPmax as the mean of the maximum pressure in the 2 adjacent sensors above the upper border of the LES, over a non-continuous 3 s, excluding regions of significant pressure gradient (>2 mmHg), referenced to atmospheric pressure. Laborie defines IBP as a polygon bounded above by the transition zone and below by the upper border of the LES, temporally preceding the contractile front velocity and contractile deceleration point. A third definition measures pressure at the time of nadir impedance.
- **Upright IRP as a screen.** Upright **IRP >12 mmHg** identified radiographic evidence of EGJOO with **98% sensitivity, 16% specificity**; for symptomatic dysphagia, **88% sensitivity, 23% specificity** (Triggs, 155 patients). High sensitivity means few patients with stasis and dysphagia are missed; the very low specificity means the upright IRP is **at best a screening tool to discard** supine-elevated IRPs that normalize upright.
- **16%–32% of cases with elevated supine IRP normalize** when repositioned and given 5 upright wet swallows.
- **21%–28% of elevated IRP cases are attributable to structural abnormalities.**
- EGJOO prevalence **5%–24%** of patients undergoing HRM across cohorts totalling >20,000 studies; inter-rater agreement **κ = 0.45 (95% CI 0.41–0.49)**.

### Secondary (structural) EGJOO

EGJOO splits into **primary (idiopathic/functional)** and **secondary**, the latter due to structural or mechanical causes. Reported secondary causes of an elevated IRP: postoperative anatomic distortion after **fundoplication or bariatric surgery**, **cancer or other infiltrative processes**, **luminal stricture**, **extraluminal compression** from a paraesophageal hernia or cardiovascular structures. **In case of dysphagia, endoscopy should always be performed first.** Where open-access HRM ordering means endoscopy and esophagram may not be available at the time of interpretation, caution is advised because secondary causes cannot be excluded.

### Why an isolated elevated IRP is so often artifact

- **Pressure drift in solid-state systems**, not always corrected by the manufacturer's post-procedural thermal compensation algorithm — thermal compensation can itself introduce an inaccurate modification that *produces* an erroneously elevated IRP.
- **Catheter impingement and sensor angulation**, particularly in the EGJ region. In hiatal hernia, impingement in the hernia sac angulates an EGJ sensor and falsely raises the recorded pressure. **If such artifacts are seen during live acquisition, the catheter can be repositioned to resolve them.** In hiatal hernia the EGJ is typically a *weakened* barrier rather than an obstruction, so an elevated IRP there is often artifact to be ignored, and certainly not treated with an invasive procedure.
- **Cardiac and vascular compression artifact**, and the physiologic range of IRP itself, are **reduced in the upright position** — the rationale for the position-change requirement.
- Perfused systems develop pressure hysteresis and hydrostatic artifacts.
- Psychological stress (not uncommon during manometry) and mid-expiratory interruption of breathing raise residual deglutitive EGJ pressure and are associated with incomplete liquid bolus clearance.

### Provocative maneuvers

- **Rapid drink challenge.** Healthy controls show complete inhibition of the distal esophageal smooth muscle and LES; achalasia patients show esophageal pressurization and elevated IRP. The overwhelming majority of patients with elevated IRP and **normal** IBP show a complete inhibitory response on RDC with normalization of IRP — indicating the single-swallow elevated IRP is devoid of clinical relevance. A minority with elevated IRP **and** IBP show abnormal pressurization and impaired LES inhibition resembling achalasia.
- **Multiple rapid swallows** = five 2 mL water swallows separated by 2–3 s.
- **Pharmacologic provocation.** **Amyl nitrite** is a potent nitric oxide donor stored refrigerated (2–8°C) that profoundly inhibits esophageal smooth muscle including the LES. In achalasia and EGJOO — but not healthy controls — amyl-nitrite-induced LES relaxation is followed by a robust LES **rebound contraction**. In Babaei's series of 258 patients, amyl-nitrite responders (IRP drop >10 mmHg below deglutitive IRP) who then underwent LES ablation improved significantly in dysphagia and regurgitation (p < 0.01); 11 treated medically improved in dysphagia (p < 0.05); **neither treatment helped chest pain** (p > 0.2).

### Bolus clearance — why it is not part of the definition

Impedance studies (Jodorkovsky, Jain, Zheng) consistently show an elevated IRP alone is **not** associated with impaired bolus clearance when peristalsis is intact: 28% of subjects with intact motility *and* elevated IRP had abnormal bolus transit versus 23% with intact motility and *normal* IRP. The combination of abnormal esophageal body motility with elevated IRP is what raises impaired transit. **On this basis impaired clearance was deliberately excluded from the EGJOO definition.**

### Natural history and clinical considerations

- **Progression to overt achalasia is rare — under 10%**; 90% or more never develop achalasia or any other severe motility disorder. Only 9% of one series developed achalasia within 2 years; 2 of 107 in another over a mean 463 days.
- Many symptomatic cases are likely **functional** in nature, given that bolus clearance is preserved.
- **No clinical characteristics identify who will progress**, and provocation testing does not resolve prognosis.
- **CT and EUS may not be required** when alarm symptoms are absent — Liu and DeLay found no additional relevant findings; Okeke found EUS frequently normal and the barium findings (hiatal hernia, Schatzki ring, malrotation, gastric volvulus, masses) would have been picked up by an adequate endoscopy. Krishnan did find an anatomic etiology (intramural mass, aortic compression, congenital muscular ring) by EUS in 3/23. The literature is contradictory; a methodologically sound diagnostic study is warranted.
- **In morbid obesity the EGJOO finding can probably be ignored** even when it persists upright — of 230 morbidly obese patients, 50 (22%) were diagnosed with EGJOO, only 8 of the 32 who were studied in both positions remained EGJOO upright, and **none** was clinically significant on all tests.
- **Avoid invasive irreversible treatment.** Two reasons: it does not help when symptoms are functional, and myotomy or pneumatic dilation **alters the IRP, making it much harder to prove later that achalasia has developed**.
- **Expectative management carries little potential for harm** and is the best option when symptoms are mild.
- **If intervention is demanded** by very severe symptoms and strong patient preference, **botulinum toxin injection is probably the best option — safe, reversible, and it allows 6–12 months of waiting before re-analysis.** Reported relief from a single injection: **55% at 1 year, 50% at 1.5 years** (Porter), more prolonged than in achalasia when repeat injections were given on demand.
- **Pneumatic dilation** can be performed where there are very strong indications that symptoms are obstructive — stasis on timed barium esophagram, impaired LES distensibility on FLIP, or progression of manometric abnormalities. Clayton reported a **67% symptomatic response** in a selected retrospective series; Blonski's group reported 67% symptomatic improvement after pneumatic dilation of EGJOO patients with obstruction on timed barium esophagram.
- **Sham response is 40%** — that is the benchmark any uncontrolled treatment series must beat.

### Timed barium esophagram — the evidence is mixed

- Blonski: timed barium esophagram separates untreated achalasia from the heterogeneous EGJOO group (27% of whom had abnormal study/tablet findings) and from non-achalasia, but does **not** separate EGJOO from non-achalasia. The abnormal-TBE EGJOO pattern was retention at 1 min but not at 5 min, plus tablet retention.
- Triggs: 30% (47/155) of manometric EGJOO had radiologic evidence of EGJOO; 52% (119/155) had symptomatic dysphagia.
- van Hoeij: barium retention did **not** predict treatment response or progression.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Timed barium esophagram's value is contested within this document.** Blonski found it cannot separate EGJOO from non-achalasia, and van Hoeij found retention did not predict treatment response or progression; Triggs found 30% had radiologic EGJOO and the same group reported 67% symptomatic improvement after pneumatic dilation of EGJOO patients with TBE obstruction. CCv4.0 nonetheless makes an abnormal TBE (preferably with tablet) one of the two acceptable supportive tests.
- **Whether CT and EUS are needed in presumed idiopathic EGJOO is unresolved** — Liu, DeLay and Okeke found no yield; Krishnan found an anatomic etiology in 3/23. The document calls the literature contradictory.
- **Every published EGJOO study is retrospective**, using measurements made for clinical care, so the evidence base cannot answer what "meaningful" EGJOO is or what IBP adds.
- **No numeric cut-off is given for an abnormal timed barium esophagram** in this document or in CCv4.0.
- The document's **Table 1 wording of statement 5.3.3** ("EGJOO should be described in the context of the pattern of peristalsis…") and its **section 5.3.3 wording** ("a manometric diagnosis of EGJOO can be seen without a disorder of peristalsis, or can coexist with disorders of peristalsis…") differ in emphasis; both are recorded above.
