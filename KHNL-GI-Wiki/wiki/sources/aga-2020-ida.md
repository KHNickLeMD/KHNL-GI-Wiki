# AGA Clinical Practice Guidelines on the Gastrointestinal Evaluation of Iron Deficiency Anemia (2020)

## Summary

This guideline standardizes the gastrointestinal (GI) workup of **iron-deficiency anemia (IDA)** — defining iron deficiency, when to scope, and adjunctive testing.

## Recommendations

Table 3 — full recommendation statements with strength and quality of evidence (verbatim, with the guideline's own Comments). The guideline does not number its recommendations; the numbers 1–7 are the wiki's.

**Definitions and scope**
- Anemia: hemoglobin <13 g/dL in men, <12 g/dL in nonpregnant women.
- Ferritin <45 ng/mL: sensitivity 85% (95% CI 82%–87%), specificity 92% (95% CI 91%–94%); <15 ng/mL: sensitivity 59%, specificity 99%.
- Out of scope: refractory IDA (despite initial evaluation and iron), recurrent IDA after repletion, obscure GI bleeding. Refractory IDA → hematology consultation may be appropriate.

**1.** In patients with anemia, the AGA **recommends** using a cutoff of **45 ng/mL over 15 ng/mL** when using ferritin to diagnose iron deficiency. *(Strong recommendation, high-quality evidence.)*
> *Comment:* In patients with inflammatory conditions or chronic kidney disease, other laboratory tests such as C-reactive protein, transferrin saturation, or soluble transferrin saturation, may be needed in conjunction with ferritin to diagnose iron deficiency anemia.

**2.** In asymptomatic postmenopausal women and men with iron-deficiency anemia, the AGA **recommends bidirectional endoscopy over no endoscopy**. *(Strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.)*
- Bidirectional = esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) **and** colonoscopy, in the **same setting**.
- Does not apply to patients with GI symptoms (integrate symptoms into the evaluation); assumes no other unequivocal explanation for the IDA.
- Diagnostic yield (18 studies): lower GI malignancy 8.9%, upper GI malignancy 2.0%.

**3.** In asymptomatic premenopausal women with iron deficiency anemia, the AGA **suggests bidirectional endoscopy over iron replacement therapy only**. *(Conditional recommendation, moderate-quality evidence.)*
> *Comment:* Patients who place a high value on avoiding the small risk of endoscopy, particularly those who are young and might have other plausible reasons for iron deficiency anemia, and a low value on the very small risk of missing a gastrointestinal malignancy would reasonably select an initial course of iron replacement therapy and no initial bidirectional endoscopy.
- Yield in premenopausal women: lower GI malignancy 0.9%, upper 0.2%. Women with GI symptoms → evaluate as appropriate; bidirectional endoscopy in the same setting.

**4.** In patients with iron deficiency anemia without other identifiable etiology after bidirectional endoscopy, the AGA **suggests noninvasive testing for *[[helicobacter-pylori-infection|H pylori]]*, followed by treatment if positive, over no testing**. *(Conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence.)*

**5.** In patients with iron-deficiency anemia, the AGA **suggests against the use of routine gastric biopsies** to diagnose [[atrophic-gastritis|atrophic gastritis]]. *(Conditional recommendation, very-low-quality evidence.)*

**6.** In asymptomatic adult patients with iron deficiency anemia and plausible [[celiac-disease|celiac disease]], the AGA **suggests initial serologic testing, followed by small bowel biopsy only if positive, over routine small bowel biopsies**. *(Conditional recommendation, very-low-quality evidence.)*
> *Comment:* Celiac disease is a well-recognized cause of iron deficiency anemia, even in asymptomatic patients, and therefore it must be considered in the differential diagnosis of iron deficiency anemia.
- Serologic testing first is favored **unless the prevalence of celiac disease is >5%** in the population under consideration.
- Higher-risk features (family history, personal autoimmune disease such as type 1 diabetes, GI symptoms) → routine small bowel biopsies may be reasonable; shared decision-making.
- Duodenum abnormal at the initial upper endoscopy → biopsy.
- Serology negative but iron deficiency persists despite an adequate iron trial → reconsider celiac disease.

**7.** In uncomplicated asymptomatic patients with iron deficiency anemia and negative bidirectional endoscopy, the AGA **suggests a trial of initial iron supplementation over the routine use of [[capsule-endoscopy|video capsule endoscopy]]**. *(Conditional recommendation, very-low-quality evidence.)*
> *Comment:* Caution needs to be applied in patients with comorbid conditions where the identification of small bowel pathology will change medical management, such as the use of anticoagulation and/or antiplatelet therapy.
- Does not apply to (capsule may be indicated): symptoms suggesting small bowel disease; higher risk of small bowel pathology (e.g., propensity for angioectasias); anticoagulant or antiplatelet use; anemia refractory to adequate iron; hospitalized patients with acute or acute-on-chronic anemia.
- Capsule yield for small bowel malignancy 1.3% (16 studies; very serious risk of bias).

**Iron supplementation (no formal recommendation)**
- Oral iron first in most patients; no oral formulation shown more effective or better tolerated.
- Historically 150–200 mg elemental iron daily; lower or every-other-day dosing may improve tolerability and absorption.
- Hemoglobin response typically within 1 month; none → assess nonadherence, malabsorption, or ongoing blood loss exceeding intake.
- Intravenous iron may suit prior gastric surgery, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic kidney disease, or blood loss exceeding oral repletion.

## Clinical Relevance

Sets ferritin <45 ng/mL as the iron-deficiency threshold; bidirectional endoscopy for asymptomatic postmenopausal women and men (strong) and premenopausal women (conditional); after a negative bidirectional endoscopy, noninvasive *H. pylori* testing and celiac serology (biopsy only if positive), and a trial of iron before routine capsule. Feeds the [[small-bowel-bleeding]] and [[celiac-disease]] pathways.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- AGA notes the British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) guideline does not recommend bidirectional endoscopy for premenopausal women without GI symptoms, a strong family history of colorectal cancer, or age >50 y, and accepts colonoscopy or CT colonography for colonic evaluation. US guideline (this one) governs the wiki.
- Rec 1 Comment prints "soluble transferrin saturation"; the body text says "soluble transferrin receptor".
