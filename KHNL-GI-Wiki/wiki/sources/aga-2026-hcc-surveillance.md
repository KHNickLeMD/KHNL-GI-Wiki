## Summary

American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) Expert Review on hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) risk stratification and emerging surveillance. Fewer than 1 in 4 patients with cirrhosis receive consistent surveillance (~24% even among subspecialists), and only 30%–40% of HCC is diagnosed at an early stage. The preferred strategy remains **semiannual ultrasound (US) + α-fetoprotein (AFP)** (sensitivity ~63% combined vs ~51% US alone). The epidemiology is shifting from hepatitis C virus (HCV) toward nonviral disease ([[nafld-masld|metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)]], [[alcohol-associated-liver-disease|alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD)]]), which have lower annual HCC incidence — increasing the number needed to screen and challenging surveillance efficiency.

Emerging blood-based biomarkers (e.g., **GALAD**) and radiologic biomarkers are undergoing validation; some are commercially available but **evidence is insufficient** to replace guideline tests, and **multicancer detection panels should not be used**. Many cirrhosis risk-stratification scores exist but few are sufficiently validated; in non-cirrhotic chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV), **PAGE-B and REAL-B** can stratify future HCC risk.

## Best Practice Advice

**8 statements, numbered 1–8 by the document itself.** No systematic review was performed, so by the document's own methods statement the Best Practice Advice statements **do not carry formal ratings of the quality of evidence or of the strength of the advice** — there is no GRADE, level, or strength label anywhere in the paper. Statements are reproduced verbatim.

1. The best strategy for reducing hepatocellular carcinoma morbidity and mortality is to prevent cirrhosis. These strategies include vaccination and treatment of viral hepatitis (eg, hepatitis C virus and hepatitis B virus), recognizing and treating alcohol use disorder, managing metabolic syndrome, and addressing liver diseases at early stages.
2. The preferred current surveillance strategy for patients at risk of hepatocellular carcinoma is semiannual ultrasound and α-fetoprotein in combination. The benefits of surveillance include a higher likelihood of detecting early-stage hepatocellular carcinoma, access to curative therapy, and improved survival.
3. Among patients without cirrhosis, only a subset of those with chronic hepatitis B virus should undergo hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance; surveillance is not advised for those without cirrhosis from other etiologies due to the low annual incidence rate.
4. Although the benefits of hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance are well established, it is important for clinicians to consider the potential physical, psychological, and financial harms associated with the process.
5. Several novel blood-based biomarkers, such as GALAD, and radiologic biomarkers are undergoing clinical validation. Some are already commercially available, but evidence is insufficient to support their use in routine hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance. These assays should not replace guideline-recommended tests, although their accessibility and potential cost-effectiveness may support broader use once sufficiently validated.
6. Multicancer blood-based biomarker detection panels should not be used in the screening or surveillance of patients at risk for hepatocellular carcinoma.
7. Many hepatocellular carcinoma risk-stratification scores exist for patients with cirrhosis. However, few scores have undergone sufficient validation to support their use in clinical practice.
8. Among patients with chronic hepatitis B virus infection without cirrhosis, PAGE-B and REAL-B scores can stratify patients based on their future risk of hepatocellular carcinoma.

- Child-Turcotte-Pugh class C **not** a transplant candidate → little benefit (competing mortality risks). On the transplant waitlist, surveillance still matters: it identifies early-stage tumors (which may raise transplant priority) and larger tumors that may preclude transplant.
- Surveillance is **not advised when life expectancy is <1–2 years** from comorbidities that cannot be remedied by transplantation or HCC therapies.
- The Child-Turcotte-Pugh score itself is not reproduced here — see [[cirrhosis]] for the full table.

**Non-cirrhotic populations the update discusses but does not move into surveillance:**
- **Post-SVR HCV with cirrhosis** — HCC risk stays elevated for **up to 10 y** after SVR → surveillance continues **indefinitely**.
- **Post-SVR, never cirrhotic** — incidence is significantly lower than the cost-effectiveness threshold. Modeling shows surveillance is cost-effective at an annual HCC incidence of **approximately 1.0% per year**.
- **MASLD without cirrhosis** — accounts for **up to 20%** of MASLD-HCC, but overall incidence in that group is low (**~0.008 per 100 person-years**) → more accurate tools are needed to avoid unnecessary screening.
- **F3 fibrosis** — routine surveillance not advised, but noninvasive fibrosis staging can **misclassify**; individualized discussion and shared decision making are appropriate in select cases (uncertain fibrosis staging, additional risk factors such as family history of HCC).

## Risk Scores in Chronic HBV Without Cirrhosis — Table 3

*Table 3 — Externally Validated Risk Stratification Models for Patients With Chronic Hepatitis B Virus Without Cirrhosis.*

| Model | Components | Risk groups and cutoffs |
|---|---|---|
| **PAGE-B** | Age, sex, platelet count | Low **<10** · Intermediate **10–17** · High **≥18** |
| **REAL-B** | Age, male, alcohol use, cirrhosis, diabetes, platelet, AFP | Low **<4** · Intermediate **4–7** · High **8–13** |
| **AASL-HCC** | Age, male, albumin, cirrhosis | Low **≤5** · Intermediate **6–19** · High **≥20** |

- These components and cutoffs are the ones the update says "can be applied to risk stratify patients with HBV." The update gives the variables and the band cut-points but **not the point value assigned to each variable** — the original derivation papers hold the weights.
- Other HBV models named (components listed, no cutoffs given): **REACH-B** (age, sex, alanine aminotransferase, hepatitis B e-antigen, HBV DNA) · **mPAGE-B** (age, sex, platelet, albumin) · **CU-HCC** (age, cirrhosis, albumin, bilirubin, HBV DNA) · **HCC-RESCUE** (age, sex, cirrhosis) · **CAMD** (cirrhosis, age, sex, diabetes mellitus) · **APA-B** (age, platelet, AFP) · **RWS-HCC** (age, sex, cirrhosis, AFP).
- Comparative study of 10 models: area under the curve **≥0.75 for 7 of 10** (CAMD, APA-B, HCC-RESCUE, CU-HCC, AASL-HCC, RWS-HCC, REAL-B). The **three AFP-containing models — APA-B, RWS-HCC, REAL-B — had the highest values (all >0.80)**. No patient in a low-risk group defined by PAGE-B, mPAGE-B, AASL-HCC, or REAL-B developed HCC during the study; REAL-B and AASL-HCC discriminated better than PAGE-B in that cohort.
- Score alone does not decide: **endemic region, age, and family history of HCC** also factor into the surveillance decision.
- Emerging, not yet recommended: **PAGED-B** (PAGE-B plus HBV DNA viral load; performed well in patients undergoing phase change from chronic hepatitis B to hepatitis B e-antigen–positive HBV) and **SMART-HCC** (machine-learned; liver stiffness measurement plus 8 parameters — cirrhosis etiology, age, alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, creatinine, platelet count, sex, hypertension). Both need further external validation.

- Annual HCC incidence in cirrhosis ranges **1% to 8%** — the spread that motivates risk stratification.
- Most existing simple models discriminate only **~0.70 to 0.80**, "precluding widespread clinical use." Multidimensional (genetic, metabolic, radiomic) models may separate low from high risk better but add cost and mostly lack external validation.
- **All** current models produce false-positive and false-negative results; there is **no consensus on acceptable levels** of either, and no agreed clinically relevant thresholds with corresponding decisions — the stated reason uptake will stay limited.

## Supporting Detail

### Benefit and Underuse
- Strongest evidence: a randomized trial in chronic HBV — surveillance reduced HCC mortality (**hazard ratio 0.63; 95% CI 0.41–0.98**). No comparable randomized trial exists in cirrhosis; meta-analyses of observational studies show earlier detection, more curative treatment, and better survival, persisting after adjustment for lead-time and length-time bias.
- Only **30%–40%** of HCC is diagnosed at an early stage; **fewer than 1 in 4** patients with cirrhosis receive consistent surveillance (**~24%** even under subspecialist care).
- Uptake improves with **outreach** (eg, mailed invitations) and **in-reach** (eg, electronic medical record reminders, provider education).
- Lifetime HCC risk with HBV is estimated at **10%–25%**.

### Modality and Interval
- **Semiannual** — detects tumors at an earlier stage and improves survival vs annual surveillance.
- **US + AFP vs US alone: sensitivity 63% vs 51%**, with a small decrease in specificity; the diagnostic odds ratio for the combination is higher, and cost-effectiveness analyses support the combination. *(This update does not state an AFP cutoff value.)*
- US is limited by **operator dependence** and worse performance in **obesity, severe steatosis, or hepatic decompensation**.
- **Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) should not be used for routine surveillance** despite possibly higher sensitivity/specificity — radiation and contrast exposure, high cost, and low availability from limited radiology capacity. Decision models suggest MRI-based surveillance may be cost-effective in select populations, but the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio varies widely with HCC incidence, cirrhosis etiology, and test costs.
- **Abbreviated MRI** (fewer sequences, shorter time, lower cost) is promising: in a prospective study of 208 patients, annual noncontrast abbreviated MRI had higher sensitivity for early-stage HCC than semiannual US (**71% vs 45%**). The **PREMIUM** trial (Preventing Liver Cancer Mortality Through Imaging With Ultrasound vs MRI) is evaluating abbreviated MRI in a large United States–based study.

### Harms
- Modalities are noninvasive but not risk-free: false positives lead to unnecessary contrast-enhanced imaging or biopsy; liver biopsy carries bleeding, infection, and rarely tumor seeding.
- In 1 study of **614 patients** in HCC surveillance, **19.2% (n = 118)** had abnormal screening results but **only 26 developed HCC**. **Physical harms occurred in 10%**, most mild or moderate.
- **Psychological harms** are often overlooked — indeterminate lesions and incidental findings cause anxiety, stress, and diminished quality of life; even benign findings prompt months of further testing.
- **Financial harms:** in 1 trial of HCC screening outreach, **21.8% reported moderate to severe financial burden** from surveillance; missed work and travel costs add to it.

### Blood-Based and Radiologic Biomarkers
- **GALAD** components: **gender, age, AFP, lectin-reactive fraction of AFP, des-γ-carboxyprothrombin.**
  - Case-control studies: sensitivity **72%–82%** for early-stage HCC.
  - Phase 3 validation, 1558 patients with cirrhosis: at a **cutoff of −1.36**, assessed **6 months before** HCC diagnosis — sensitivity **65%**, specificity **82%**.
  - These cutoffs are typically benchmarked at ~**80% specificity**, ie, a **~20% false-positive rate**, to prioritize early-stage detection.
- **HES (Hepatocellular Carcinoma Early Detection Screening) algorithm, version 2.0** — AFP trends plus lectin-reactive fraction of AFP, des-γ-carboxyprothrombin, alanine aminotransferase, platelet count, age, and cirrhosis etiology. Better overall performance than GALAD and ASAP in phase 3, but **sensitivity for early-stage HCC only 47%**; optimal cutoffs need further validation.
- **Liquid biopsy** (cell-free DNA methylation, circulating tumor cells, extracellular vesicle profiling): some early-phase sensitivities and specificities **>80%**, but most data are phase 2 and several have Food and Drug Administration breakthrough device designation only — **not recommended for clinical use**.
- **Biomarker as an adjunct to imaging** (rather than replacement) **cannot be recommended at this time**: no prospective study has compared adjunct biomarker-plus-imaging with either modality alone, and the strategy increases cost without addressing patient-level barriers to imaging.
- The **TRACER** trial (National Liver Cancer Screening Trial) will provide more definitive data on effectiveness and harms of biomarker-based surveillance.

### Multicancer Detection Panels
- An early retrospective study detected **62%** of localized tumors (eg, colorectal, breast, lung, ovarian) with targeted error-correction sequencing of cell-free DNA; panels combining cell-free DNA mutations with protein biomarkers improved sensitivity for ovary, liver, stomach, pancreas, and esophagus.
- Prospective case-control, **2800 participants** across tumor types and stages: whole-genome DNA methylation achieved **98% specificity** but only **34%–39% sensitivity**.
- Reason for the "do not use" advice: these panels were tested in **general populations with few patients with cirrhosis or HCC**.

### Prevention
- **HBV:** universal vaccination; nucleos(t)ide analogs (eg, tenofovir, entecavir) suppress replication, reduce inflammation, and prevent progression to cirrhosis. Screen for **HCV, [[hepatitis-d|hepatitis D virus]], and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)** coinfection — each significantly increases HCC risk over HBV monoinfection.
- **HCV:** [[direct-acting-antivirals|direct-acting antivirals]] halt progression and partly reverse cirrhosis-related damage, lowering HCC risk; expanding testing and treatment access in high-risk populations is essential.
- **MASLD:** the most rapidly increasing etiology of HCC, closely linked to metabolic syndrome, [[obesity]], and type 2 diabetes (all established independent risk factors). Foundation is lifestyle — weight loss, balanced diet, regular exercise. **Resmetirom** (oral, liver-directed thyroid hormone receptor β-agonist) and **glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists** may reduce HCC risk by reducing fibrosis, but long-term follow-up studies are needed to confirm.
- **Alcohol-associated liver disease:** behavioral interventions, pharmacologic treatment of alcohol use disorder (eg, naltrexone), and public-health policy (alcohol taxes, awareness campaigns).

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- No major contradiction with [[aasld-2023-hcc]]; both endorse semiannual ultrasound plus α-fetoprotein. This 2026 Clinical Practice Update adds that novel biomarkers (GALAD) and risk scores are **not yet** ready to replace guideline tests, and that the use of biomarkers as an **adjunct** to imaging also cannot yet be recommended.
- **Incidence thresholds differ in form, not substance:** this update's Table 1 gives **>1.0%** per year for cirrhosis and **>0.2%** per year for non-cirrhotic chronic hepatitis B, where [[aasld-2023-hcc]] states ≥1.0% and ≥0.2%. The two documents name the same populations; the boundary case (exactly 1.0% or 0.2%) is the only difference.
- **Sensitivity figures differ slightly:** this update quotes ultrasound plus α-fetoprotein at **63% vs 51%** for ultrasound alone; [[aasld-2023-hcc]] quotes 63% vs 53% (and 61%/92% in its Table 2). The ranges overlap within the AASLD confidence intervals.
- Surveillance in post-SVR non-cirrhotic and non-cirrhotic MASLD populations remains open and evolving; better risk stratification is the stated prerequisite for changing the advice.
- The update gives PAGE-B, REAL-B, and AASL-HCC variables and risk bands but **not the point value for each variable** — the derivation papers hold the weights.
- No α-fetoprotein cutoff value is stated anywhere in this document.
- No ultrasound visualization-score categories are defined; the update notes only that ultrasound performs worse in obesity, severe steatosis, and hepatic decompensation.
