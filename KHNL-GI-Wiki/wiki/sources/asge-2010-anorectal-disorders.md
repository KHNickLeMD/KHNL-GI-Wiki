## Summary
American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) Standards of Practice statement on the endoscopic role across four benign anorectal disorders: chronic radiation proctopathy, [[anal-fissure|anal fissure]], internal [[hemorrhoids|hemorrhoids]], and [[fecal-incontinence|fecal incontinence]]. A revision of the 2001 ASGE statement on endoscopic therapy of anorectal disorders. Perianal disease in inflammatory bowel disease is explicitly out of scope.

For [[radiation-proctopathy|radiation proctopathy]], [[argon-plasma-coagulation|argon plasma coagulation (APC)]] and topical formalin are the practical first-line endoscopic approaches. For hemorrhoids, rubber band ligation (RBL) is the preferred office procedure for bleeding/prolapsing internal hemorrhoids failing conservative care. For fecal incontinence, all patients should have flexible sigmoidoscopy or [[colonoscopy]] as indicated by age and risk, with endoanal ultrasound (EAUS) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) complementing [[anorectal-manometry|anorectal manometry]] when results will change management.

Most of this 2010 guidance is now superseded or corroborated by newer sources — [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders|American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2021 benign anorectal disorders]] (hemorrhoids, fissure, fecal incontinence) and [[asge-2019-radiation-proctopathy|ASGE 2019 radiation proctopathy]] — which the disease pages use as primary references.

## Grading Scheme Used by This Document
- The document grades **quality of evidence only**, with the GRADE four-level scale (high / moderate / low / very low).
- **Strength is carried by the verb, not a separate label:** weaker recommendations read *"we suggest"*, stronger ones *"we recommend"*. The document assigns no separate strength grade.
- Recommendations are **numbered 1–9 in the document itself**; the numbering below is the document's.

## Recommendations
*Quality of evidence in parentheses; the verb carries the strength.*

1. Treatment of radiation proctopathy found **incidentally** on endoscopy is **not recommended** unless there are signs (eg, anemia) or symptoms affecting overall health status or quality of life — mild proctopathy resolves spontaneously in the majority. *(Very low)*
2. When endoscopic treatment of chronic radiation proctopathy is indicated, **suggest** APC as a first-line approach. *(Low)* Because of cost, hyperbaric oxygen should be **reserved as an alternative** for patients in whom conventional treatment fails. *(Low)* — graded as two separate statements.
3. **Suggest** topical formalin application as an alternative treatment for chronic radiation proctopathy. *(Low)*
4. **Suggest** topical nitrate or calcium channel blocker application, or botulinum toxin injection, as first-line **nonsurgical** treatment for chronic anal fissure. *(Low)*
5. **Suggest** referral for lateral internal sphincterotomy as the **definitive** treatment for chronic anal fissure, and for patients in whom botulinum toxin injection fails. *(Low)*
6. **Suggest** RBL **or sclerotherapy** as initial treatment of choice for **first-degree** internal hemorrhoids that bleed but do not prolapse, when conservative management has failed. *(Low)*
7. **Recommend** RBL as initial treatment of choice for chronic bleeding and/or prolapsing **second- and third-degree** internal hemorrhoids in whom conservative management fails. *(Moderate)*
8. **Suggest** that **all** patients with fecal incontinence undergo flexible sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy as indicated by age, family history, and previous endoscopic evaluation. *(Very low)*
9. **Suggest** EAUS or MRI as a **complement to** anorectal manometry for evaluating sphincter integrity in fecal incontinence, in patients for whom the results are expected to affect management. *(Low)*

## Classifications and Thresholds

### Goligher classification of internal hemorrhoids (4 degrees)
*Degree drives the treatment choice in recommendations 6–7.*

| Degree | Criteria |
|---|---|
| First | Bleed, do **not** prolapse |
| Second | Prolapse, **spontaneously** reduce |
| Third | Prolapse, require **manual** reduction |
| Fourth | Prolapse, **cannot** be reduced |

- Internal vs external = **above vs below the dentate line**.
- RBL = treatment of choice for mild rectal mucosal prolapse and bleeding in first-, second-, and **selected** third-degree hemorrhoids when medical treatment fails.
- Surgery (open/closed hemorrhoidectomy, stapled hemorrhoidopexy, Doppler-guided vascular pedicle ligation) = treatment of choice for **all fourth-degree**, **strangulated**, and hemorrhoids not successfully treated by other therapy; also for very large third-degree or failed RBL.

### Doses, settings, and technique parameters
- **APC for radiation proctopathy:** typically **1–3 sessions**; power **25–60 W**; argon flow **0.5–2.5 L/min**. Reduces rectal bleeding in 80%–90%.
- **Topical formalin:** dilute **2%–10%**, via rigid proctoscope or rectal instillation. In the largest series, 10% formalin on a cotton-tip applicator, averaging **3.5 applications at 2- to 4-week intervals**; bleeding ceased in 93%.
- **Botulinum toxin for chronic anal fissure:** total **20 U botulinum toxin A** via **27-gauge needle**, in divided doses on either side of the fissure. Reported healing 37%–92%.
- **Topical agents for chronic anal fissure:** nitroglycerin compounded as **0.2% ointment**; nifedipine **0.2%–0.3%**; diltiazem **2%**. Calcium channel blockers have fewer side effects than nitrates and no significant efficacy difference.
- **Rectal sucralfate for radiation proctopathy:** **2 g twice daily** enemas.
- **RBL:** usually **no more than 3 hemorrhoidal groups per session**; some experts place only 1 band at a time. Disease requiring **≥4 bands** was associated with higher failure and greater need for subsequent operative hemorrhoidectomy.

### Safety limits
- **No RBL in sedated patients** (expert caution): errant band placement at or below the dentate line causes significant pain.
- **Relative contraindications to endoscopic therapy of radiation proctopathy:** malignant recurrence, stenosis, fistulae.
- **Colonic explosion** reported after **enema-only** bowel preparation before APC — adequate full preparation required.
- All thermal methods may create symptomatic ulceration (pain, bleeding) taking months to resolve.
- APC in patients with pacemakers/defibrillators: same precautions as other endoscopic electrocautery.

## Key Findings / Claims
- **Chronic radiation proctopathy:** onset **9 months to 30 years** after pelvic radiation, typically within 2 years. Occurs in up to 20% of patients receiving prostate irradiation. ~**95% of mild** radiation proctopathy is temporary and self-limited; up to **5%** are refractory to conservative management.
- Histology shows **ischemic endarteritis of submucosal arterioles and submucosal fibrosis** — no inflammatory component, so "radiation proctitis" is a misnomer.
- Complications of radiation proctopathy: anemia, rectal strictures, rectovesical fistula, increased risk of [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]].
- **Anal fissure:** tear in anal mucosa **below the dentate line**. Usually posterior midline; anterior midline in **19%**. Also associated with [[crohns-disease|Crohn's disease]]. Acute = laceration; chronic = linear white-based lesion with horizontally oriented fibers, sentinel skin tag, hypertrophied anal papillae.
- Chronic anal fissure: initial **medical** therapy is typically recommended **except** for fissures associated with an abscess or fistula.
- **Lateral internal sphincterotomy:** mean healing/improvement **95%**, mean incontinence rate **9.3%**; quality of life still improves overall.
- Endoscopy for fissure: consider when the diagnosis is in doubt, if bleeding occurs, to rule out inflammatory bowel disease, and for patients due for screening colonoscopy — deferring colonoscopy until after initial fissure treatment avoids prep/procedure discomfort.
- **Hemorrhoids:** >75% of US adults report symptomatic hemorrhoids at some point; ~30% seek care. ~20% have a concomitant fissure. Diagnosis by perineal inspection, digital rectal examination, and office anoscopy; internal hemorrhoids best seen by anoscopy or on retroflexion.
- Sclerotherapy effective for first- and second-degree hemorrhoids; rare complications include mucosal necrosis, abscess, prostatitis, erectile dysfunction, portal pyemia.
- **Fecal incontinence:** prevalence **8.3%** in non-institutionalized US adults (men 7.7%, women 8.9%), rising with age. Endoscopy is needed because anorectal malignancy or proctitis can cause it; evaluation should also assess prolapsing hemorrhoids, rectal prolapse, and rectocele.
- EAUS was **superior to MRI** for diagnosing **internal** anal sphincter injury and **equivalent** for **external** sphincter injury; a second study found no significant sensitivity difference for external sphincter defects/atrophy (90% vs 81%).
- Other informative tests in fecal incontinence: pudendal nerve terminal motor latency, electromyography.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- Superseded as primary reference by [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]] and [[asge-2019-radiation-proctopathy]]; added only as corroborating/net-new endoscopic guidance, not overwriting newer content.
- The document states medical therapy for chronic anal fissure is "far less effective than surgery" (Cochrane) yet still suggests topical therapy/botulinum toxin first-line and sphincterotomy as definitive — an internal tension it does not resolve.
- No studies assessing anal fissure recurrence beyond 1 year; long-term benefits and complications of botulinum toxin are unknown.
- **APC settings differ from the newer ASGE 2019 proctopathy guideline** (this document: 25–60 W, flow 0.5–2.5 L/min, 1–3 sessions; ASGE 2019: 25–80 W, median 50 W, flow 0.6–2.5 L/min). [[radiation-proctopathy]] follows the newer document.
- **Lateral internal sphincterotomy incontinence rate:** this document gives a pooled mean of 9.3%; ACG 2021 gives no pooled rate and reports minor incontinence 4% and incontinence 16% separately. [[anal-fissure]] follows ACG 2021.
