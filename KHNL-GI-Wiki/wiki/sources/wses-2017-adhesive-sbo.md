## Summary

Society guideline from the World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) ASBO working group, updating the Bologna guidelines on adhesive small bowel obstruction (ASBO). Scope is **acute** bowel obstruction only; management of chronic adhesion-related abdominal pain is explicitly out of scope. Two sections are new in this update: **prevention of ASBO** and **special patient groups** (young, elderly, pregnant).

Core position: adhesions are the single most common cause of small bowel obstruction (60% of cases), and most episodes resolve without an operation. A **trial of non-operative management is the default** unless peritonitis, strangulation or bowel ischemia is present. Diagnostic work-up centres on **CT with oral water-soluble contrast**, with a **follow-up plain abdominal X-ray at 24 h** — contrast reaching the colon indicates resolution; failure of contrast to reach the colon is highly indicative that non-operative management will fail. Plain X-rays alone are not recommended.

The guideline also quantifies prevention: minimally invasive technique and adhesion barriers reduce adhesion formation, with hyaluronate carboxymethylcellulose the only barrier carrying a **strong recommendation** (Level IA) for reducing reoperations for ASBO. Icodextrin 4% is the barrier with trial evidence specifically for **secondary** prevention (applied at surgery for ASBO).

Grading scheme is the working group's own, combining evidence level with recommendation strength: conclusions are graded **A** (strong evidence) to **D** (expert opinion), and recommendations are **I** (strong recommendation) or **II** (weak recommendation / suggestion). Table 5 of the document therefore carries labels such as "Level IB" (strong recommendation, level B conclusion) and "Level IID" (weak recommendation, expert opinion). Rows labelled with a letter only (Level A, Level B, Level C) are conclusions, not recommendations.

| Recommendation level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| I | Strong recommendation |

Per-article evidence levels: **A1** systematic review/meta-analysis of ≥2 independent A2 studies · **A2** double-blind controlled randomized comparative clinical trial of good quality with adequate numbers (for diagnostic accuracy: test vs reference standard with pre-defined criteria, independent observers, consecutive patients, both tests in all patients) · **B** comparative study without all A2 features · **C** non-comparative studies · **D** expert opinion.

Recommendations were graded strong when evidence (level A or B) showed benefits of clinical importance clearly outweighing harm.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Definitions**
- *Peritoneal adhesions* — fibrous tissue connecting surfaces or organs within the peritoneal cavity that are normally separated; result of pathological peritoneal healing rather than "ad integrum" repair. Non-operative causes of peritoneal injury: radiotherapy, endometriosis, inflammation, local response to tumours.
- *Adhesiolysis* — releasing adhesions by blunt or sharp dissection. *Complicated adhesiolysis* = inadvertent injury during adhesiolysis, most often to bowel; classified as **seromuscular injury** (serosa + smooth muscle, lumen/contents not visible), **enterotomy** (full thickness, mucosa/lumen visible or leakage), **delayed diagnosed perforation** (injury unrecognised at closure, patient deteriorates post-operatively).
- Definitive confirmation of adhesive etiology is made at operation; non-invasive confirmation = prior episodes of adhesive obstruction, or exclusion of other causes on imaging (often CT).

**Epidemiology / burden**
- Adhesions cause 60% of small bowel obstructions; small bowel obstruction was the indication for 51% of all emergency laparotomies in the UK.
- Mean hospitalisation 8 days; in-hospital mortality 3% per episode. 20–30% of patients require operation. Stay 16 days after surgery vs 5 days after non-operative treatment; Dutch 2016 costs €16,305 surgical vs €2227 non-operative.
- Highest risk after colorectal, oncologic gynaecological or pediatric surgery. 1 in 10 patients has ≥1 episode within 3 years of colectomy. Reoperation for ASBO: 4.2–12.6% after pediatric surgery, 3.2% after colorectal surgery.
- Recurrence after non-operative treatment: 12% readmitted at 1 year, 20% at 5 years; after operative treatment 8% at 1 year, 16% at 5 years.
- Failure to diagnose or delayed diagnosis accounts for 70% of malpractice claims in ASBO.

**Adhesion classification / scores** (operative assessment only; none validated against long-term adhesion-related risk)
- **Zühlke** grades 0–4 by tenacity/morphology. Easy to use; does not measure extent, and tenacity varies between abdominal regions.
- **American Fertility Society (AFS) score** — designed for the small pelvis; extent + severity scored at right ovary, right tube, left ovary, left tube; right and left sums compared and the **lower** side's score taken (so AFS 0 does not mean no adhesions). Low inter-observer reproducibility; a modified AFS is more used in recent studies.
- **Peritoneal adhesion index (PAI)** (ASBO working group) — tenacity on a 1–3 scale at 10 predefined sites, integrating tenacity and extent; the only score validated as prognostic for convalescence after ASBO surgery and for risk of injury during adhesiolysis.

**Scores predicting need for surgery**
- **Zielinski** — three radiological/clinical signs: mesenteric edema, absence of the small-bowel feces sign, obstipation; validated in 100 ASBO cases, concordance index 0.77.
- **Baghdadi** — radiological findings + sepsis criteria + comorbidity index; area under the curve 0.80 in a validation study of 351 cases; somewhat complex to assess.

**Diagnosis**
- Classic presentation: intermittent colicky abdominal pain, distention, nausea ± vomiting, ± absence of stools. Pitfalls: watery diarrhea in incomplete obstruction (mistaken for gastroenteritis); stools still present in high obstruction presenting early; pain often less prominent in the elderly.
- Physical examination has **48% sensitivity** for strangulation, even in experienced hands.
- Minimum labs: blood count, lactate, electrolytes, C-reactive protein (CRP), blood urea nitrogen (BUN)/creatinine. Values that might indicate peritonitis: **CRP >75** and **white blood cell count >10,000/mm³** (sensitivity and specificity relatively low). Hypokalemia is frequent and needs correction; BUN/creatinine because dehydration can cause acute kidney injury.
- Plain X-ray: triad of multiple air-fluid levels, distended small bowel loops and absence of colonic gas is pathognomonic in high-grade obstruction, but overall sensitivity ~70%; cannot detect early peritonitis/strangulation and gives no anatomical information on cause. Large-volume pneumoperitoneum is detectable, preferably on erect chest X-ray.
- Water-soluble contrast: if contrast has **not** reached the colon on an abdominal X-ray taken **24 h** after administration, this is highly indicative of failure of non-operative management; accurately predicts need for surgery and reduces hospital stay, with some authors attributing an active therapeutic role to the contrast. The algorithm (Fig. 2) asks "contrast in colon after **24–36 hours**?"
- CT: ~90% accuracy for predicting strangulation and need for urgent surgery; differentiates causes by excluding alternatives even though adhesions are not directly visible; preferred technique whenever there is doubt about the diagnosis or about the need for urgent surgery. Can be enhanced with water-soluble contrast, with progress followed by X-ray at 24 h. Helps separate complete from incomplete obstruction and localise the obstruction (e.g. high jejunal vs deep pelvic). **Closed loop, bowel ischemia and free fluid suggest the need for surgery without delay.**
- Ultrasound and MRI: ultrasound is operator dependent but in experienced hands gives more than plain X-rays and is available in most low-income settings — detects free fluid and assesses degree of shock in dehydration; useful when radiation is undesirable (pregnancy), complemented by MRI for anatomical detail once obstruction is confirmed.
- If the diagnosis of ASBO is certain (other causes excluded on recent imaging) and no sign suggests immediate surgery, a **water-soluble contrast study alone is considered sufficient**.

**Management**
- Non-operative management is effective in approximately **70–90%** of patients. Cornerstone: nil per os and decompression with a naso-gastric tube or long intestinal tube, plus fluid resuscitation, correction of electrolyte disturbances, nutritional support, prevention of aspiration.
- Long tubes: an older trial found no difference in failure rates vs naso-gastric tubes; a newer randomized trial of 186 patients comparing a trilumen long tube with a naso-gastric tube found failure in 10.4% vs 53.3% — interpret with care, as the naso-gastric failure rate is far higher than other literature, and trilumen tubes need endoscopic placement.
- Duration: evidence for optimal duration is absent; most authors and the panel consider **72 h safe and appropriate**. Delays in surgery increase morbidity and mortality in retrospective series. Continuing beyond 72 h with persistent high tube output but no other deterioration remains debated.
- Algorithm (Fig. 2) criteria for **failure of non-operative treatment**: persistent obstruction >72 hours; drainage volume >500 ml on the 3rd day; peritonitis or ischemia — exacerbation of pain, CRP >75 mg/l, white blood cell count 10,000/mm³, free intraperitoneal fluid >500 ml.
- Common medical complications: dehydration with kidney injury, electrolyte disturbance, malnutrition, aspiration.
- Operative: open exploration is the historical standard. Laparoscopic adhesiolysis in a meta-analysis of 14 non-randomized studies reduced morbidity, in-hospital mortality and surgical infections, but with strong selection bias toward less severe cases. Bowel injury reported in **6.3–26.9%** of laparoscopic adhesiolysis for ASBO; in a population-based study bowel resection was more frequent laparoscopically (**53.5% vs 43.4%** open). Laparoscopy in an abdomen with very distended loops and multiple complex adhesions may increase enterotomy and delayed-perforation risk, and is more difficult after radiotherapy.
- Predictors of successful laparoscopic treatment (Farinella): **≤2 laparotomies in history**, appendectomy as the prior operation, no previous median laparotomy incision, and a single adhesive band. Fig. 2 adds **sufficient experience** of the surgeon to the selection criteria.
- In a surgeon questionnaire, 60% had performed laparoscopic adhesiolysis for ASBO, but half of those in <15% of cases.

**Prevention**
- Reoperation for ASBO in a systematic review of cohort studies: **1.4% (95% CI 1.0–1.8) after laparoscopic vs 3.8% (95% CI 3.1–4.4) after open** surgery. Meta-analysis after colorectal operations: OR 0.62 (95% CI 0.54–0.72) favouring laparoscopy, but no significant difference across the three included randomized trials (OR 0.50, 95% CI 0.20–1.2). Laparoscopy is therefore not a complete solution.
- Technique-related risk factors for aggravated adhesion formation: foreign body reaction (starch-powdered gloves, abdominal wall meshes); monopolar electrocautery causes more peritoneal injury than bipolar or ultrasonic devices. Animal data suggest systemic and intraperitoneal antibiotics, metronidazole in particular, reduce adhesion formation in septic conditions.
- Barriers act as a spacer separating injured peritoneal surfaces; they should be inert and slowly degradable.
- Hyaluronate carboxymethylcellulose in three trials of 1132 colorectal surgery patients reduced reoperations for ASBO (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.28–0.88).
- Secondary prevention: one randomized trial of liquid **4% icodextrin** applied at laparotomy for ASBO — recurrence **2.19% (2/91)** vs **11.11% (10/90)** without barrier at mean follow-up 41.4 months (p < 0.05). Icodextrin can also be given laparoscopically, is cheap with a good safety record, but other trials suggest it may not be the most potent barrier against adhesion *reformation*; hyaluronate carboxymethylcellulose may be more efficacious but is less practical laparoscopically.

**Special patient groups**
- *Young* — risk is lifelong; most obstructions occur within the first 2 years after surgery but new cases continue for many years. Pediatric surgery cohort: ASBO incidence **12.6%** at median follow-up 14.7 years. No barrier trials in pediatric surgery, but a cohort showed ASBO in **2.0% with vs 4.5% without** hyaluronate carboxymethylcellulose at 24 months.
- *Elderly* — quality-of-life considerations dominate; high frailty index predicts prolonged recovery and possible failure to return to baseline function. Marked paucity of research on stopping/withholding oral medications during nil per os. Diabetic patients operated **>24 h** after presentation had **7.5% acute kidney injury** and **4.8% myocardial infarction**, significantly higher than diabetics operated within 24 h and than non-diabetics with delayed operation.
- *Pregnancy* — very rare, significant risk of fetal loss. Review of 46 cases of bowel obstruction in pregnancy: about half attributed to adhesions, most from previous abdominal surgery. Imaging used in the case reports: ultrasound 10 cases (83%), abdominal X-ray 4 (33%), MRI 4 (33%), CT 3 (25%). Of 23 reported ASBO cases, 17 had an initial non-operative trial and **16 failed (94%)**. Fetal loss risk 17% (n = 8); maternal death 2% (n = 1).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No contradiction with existing wiki content identified.** No other ingested source covers adhesive small bowel obstruction, so none of this competes with a higher-priority or newer claim.
- **The guideline gives no contrast agent, dose or volume** for the water-soluble contrast study — only the timing of the follow-up film and what counts as a positive.
- **Follow-up film timing differs within the document:** the recommendation table and body text say the follow-up abdominal X-ray is made **after 24 h**, while the algorithm (Fig. 2) asks whether contrast is in the colon **after 24–36 hours**.
- Optimal duration of a non-operative trial is explicitly unevidenced; 72 h is the panel's and most authors' judgement. Continuing beyond 72 h in a patient with persistent high tube output but no deterioration is unresolved.
- The randomized trial of laparoscopic vs open surgery for ASBO had not reported at publication; the laparoscopy recommendation rests on non-randomized data with strong selection bias.
- Adhesion scores (Zühlke, AFS, PAI) require operative assessment and none is validated against the long-term risk of adhesion-related complications.
- Evidence in special groups is sparse: no barrier trials in pediatric surgery, no research on withholding oral medications in the elderly on nil per os, and the pregnancy data are case reports and series only.
