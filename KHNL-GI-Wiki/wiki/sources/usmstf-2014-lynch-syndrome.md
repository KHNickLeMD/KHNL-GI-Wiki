## Summary

Consensus statement from the US Multi-Society Task Force on Colorectal Cancer — reviewed and approved by the governing boards of the ACG, AGA, ASGE, and ASCRS — on genetic evaluation and clinical management of [[lynch-syndrome|Lynch syndrome]] (LS). It covers disease biology, clinical recognition (Amsterdam/Bethesda criteria), tumor testing (IHC and MSI) and universal screening, germline testing indications, gene-specific cancer risks, surveillance, risk-reducing surgery, aspirin chemoprevention, and genetic counseling.

LS is an autosomal dominant condition caused by germline inactivation of a DNA mismatch repair (MMR) gene — *MLH1*, *MSH2*, *MSH6*, *PMS2* — or by *EPCAM* deletion silencing *MSH2*. It accounts for about **3%** of newly diagnosed [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]]. *MLH1*/*MSH2* mutations account for up to 90% of LS families, *MSH6* about 10%, and *PMS2* was found in 6% of LS families in a recent study. Mean age at CRC diagnosis is 44–61 years vs 69 in sporadic CRC; 60–80% of LS CRCs are right-sided.

Testing of newly diagnosed CRC for MMR deficiency is recommended, by IHC and/or MSI, with *BRAF* or *MLH1* promoter hypermethylation testing when MLH1 is lost (Figure 2). Colonoscopy every 1–2 years beginning at age 20–25 is the cornerstone of surveillance; observational studies report 62% less CRC in screened at-risk persons, 65% fewer CRC deaths, and a 72% decrease in CRC mortality. Hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy after childbearing or at age 40 is recommended; in one retrospective series no uterine or ovarian cancer occurred in the surgical group vs 33% uterine and 5.5% ovarian cancer in the nonsurgical group. Aspirin 600 mg/day (CAPP2) is a consideration after discussion of patient-specific risks and benefits.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Epidemiology and penetrance

- LS is the most common form of inherited CRC, accounting for **about 3%** of newly diagnosed colorectal malignancy
- *MLH1* and *MSH2* mutations account for **up to 90%** of mutations in LS families; *MSH6* about **10%**; *PMS2* found in **6%** of all LS families in a recent study
- Mean age at CRC diagnosis in LS **44–61 y** vs **69 y** sporadic; **60–80%** of LS CRCs proximal to the splenic flexure (vs 30% sporadic)
- Metachronous CRC after segmental resection of the initial CRC: **16% at 10 y, 41% at 20 y**
- Adenoma→carcinoma dwell time in LS estimated at **35 months** vs 10–15 years sporadic

### Clinical criteria

- **Amsterdam II criteria (Table 5)** — ≥3 relatives with histologically verified HNPCC-associated cancer (colorectal, endometrium, small bowel, ureter, or renal pelvis), **1 of which is a first-degree relative of the other 2**; FAP excluded ([[familial-adenomatous-polyposis|FAP]]); cancer involving **at least 2 generations**; ≥1 cancer diagnosed **before age 50**
- **Revised Bethesda Guidelines (Table 6)** — (1) CRC <50 y; (2) synchronous or metachronous CRC or other LS-associated tumor, any age; (3) CRC with MSI-high pathologic features (Crohn-like lymphocytic reaction, mucinous/signet ring differentiation, medullary growth) diagnosed <60 y; (4) CRC plus CRC or LS-associated tumor in ≥1 first-degree relative <50 y; (5) CRC plus CRC or LS-associated tumor at any age in ≥2 first- or second-degree relatives
- **40%** of families meeting Amsterdam I criteria do not have LS

- **PREMM<sub>1,2,6</sub>:** a **5% cutoff** was used as the criterion for germline genetic testing in a cost-effective general-population analysis
- **3-question CRC risk assessment tool (Figure 1):** when all 3 questions were answered "yes," correctly identified **95%** of individuals with germline mutations causing LS; cumulative sensitivity **77%** for identifying patients who should undergo more extensive risk assessment

### Tumor testing

- MSI found in **>90%** of colon malignancies in LS and **12%** of sporadic CRC; MSI-high = ≥30% of markers unstable, MSI-low <30%, MSS = none unstable
- Aberrant *MLH1* promoter methylation is a somatic event responsible for MSI in approximately **12%** of sporadic CRCs; methylation must be biallelic to abrogate MMR activity
- Somatic *BRAF* mutations, largely at codon 600, occur in **15%** of sporadic CRCs; a *BRAF* mutation in an MSI-high CRC is usually, but not always, evidence against LS
- Specificity of MSI and IHC decreases with increasing age due to increasing prevalence of somatic *MLH1* hypermethylation; over age 70, *BRAF* testing when MLH1 loss is seen helps distinguish sporadic from germline
- Up to **28%** of LS patients would be missed with the most liberal of clinical criteria (revised Bethesda)
- Universal tumor IHC testing had greater sensitivity for identifying LS than Bethesda guidelines or a selective strategy (tumor testing of CRC ≤70 y or older patients meeting Bethesda); IHC of CRCs for MMR proteins followed by *BRAF* testing when MLH1 expression is absent emerged as the most cost-effective approach
- Table 8 maps each MSI/IHC pattern to its possible cause and the additional test indicated

### Surveillance outcomes

- Järvinen: **62%** less CRC in screened at-risk persons (*P*=0.03); **65%** fewer deaths from CRC (*P*=0.003)
- Dove-Edwin: **72%** decrease in CRC mortality in those undergoing screening
- Endometrial cancer: **75%** of LS patients with EC present with stage I disease and have an **88%** 5-year survival; transvaginal ultrasound has poor sensitivity and specificity for EC, while endometrial sampling identifies some asymptomatic EC and premalignant lesions
- Urine cytology sensitivity for urinary tract cancer **29%**; 0.1% of urine cytology examinations led to diagnosis of urothelial tumor, and 10 times more led to false-positive diagnosis
- Gastric cancer lifetime risk with MMR mutation **8.0% in males, 5.3% in females**; precursor lesions in MMR mutation carriers — *H. pylori* **26%**, atrophy 14%, intestinal metaplasia **14%** ([[helicobacter-pylori-infection|H. pylori]])
- Pancreatic cancer: standardized incidence ratio 10.7 (95% CI 2.7–47.7) with 10-y cumulative risk 0.95%; a second cohort reported an 8.6-fold increase (95% CI 4.7–15.7) with cumulative risk 3.7% by age 70

### Surgery and chemoprevention

- Metachronous CRC after **partial colectomy**: 10-y cumulative risk **16–19%**; Parry — 16%, 41%, 62% at 10, 20, 30 y; Win — 19%, 47%, 69% at 10, 20, 30 y
- Risk substantially reduced by subtotal (small bowel to sigmoid) or total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis: **0–3.4%**
- Life expectancy gained with total colectomy vs hemicolectomy by Markov modeling: **2.3, 1, and 0.3 years** at ages 27, 47, and 67
- Up to **20%** of LS CRCs occur in the rectum; after proctectomy for rectal cancer, risk of metachronous colon neoplasia was 51% (Kalady) and cumulative risk of cancer to 30 years 69% (Win)
- Prophylactic gynecologic surgery (Schmeler, 315 women): **no uterine or ovarian cancer** in the surgical group vs **33%** uterine and **5.5%** ovarian cancer in the nonsurgical group
- **CAPP2** (Burn 2008): 1,071 LS patients from 43 centers, 2 × 2 design — **727 randomized to resistant starch 30 g/d or placebo; 693 randomized to aspirin 600 mg/d or no aspirin**. No effect of starch or aspirin on colorectal adenoma/cancer at mean follow-up 29 months
- **CAPP2 long-term follow-up** (Burn 2011): 861 LS patients (427 aspirin 600 mg/d, 434 placebo); at mean follow-up 55.7 months, intention-to-treat analysis of **all LS cancers HR 0.65 (95% CI 0.42–1.00; *P*=0.05)**; time to first CRC HR 0.63 (95% CI 0.35–1.13; *P*=0.12), and per-protocol analysis in those completing 2 years of intervention (258 aspirin, 250 placebo) HR 0.41 (95% CI 0.19–0.86; *P*=0.02). Adverse events did not differ between groups
- The aspirin dose used in CAPP2 is significantly higher than the dose shown effective (75 mg/d) in sporadic CRC chemoprevention

## Guideline Statements

The document presents unnumbered shaded **"Guideline"** boxes in the order below, each with its own strength wording and evidence rating; a few carry no evidence level or GRADE rating at all.

| Guideline statement | Strength / evidence |
|---|---|
| **Tumor testing.** Testing for MMR deficiency of newly diagnosed CRC should be performed. This can be done for all CRCs, or CRC diagnosed at age 70 years or younger, and in individuals older than 70 years who have a family history concerning for LS. Analysis can be done by IHC testing for the MLH1/MSH2/MSH6/PMS2 proteins and/or testing for MSI. Tumors that demonstrate loss of MLH1 should undergo *BRAF* testing or analysis of *MLH1* promoter hypermethylation (Figure 2). To facilitate surgical planning, tumor testing on suspected CRC should be performed on preoperative biopsy specimens, if possible. | Strong recommendation · evidence level III · GRADE moderate-quality |
| **Genetic evaluation.** Individuals who have a personal history of a tumor showing evidence of MMR deficiency (without evidence of *MLH1* promoter methylation); uterine cancer diagnosed at younger than age 50 years; a known family MMR gene mutation; fulfill Amsterdam criteria or revised Bethesda guidelines; and/or have a personal history of ≥5% chance of LS based on prediction models should undergo genetic evaluation for LS (Figures 3–6). | Strong recommendation · evidence level III · GRADE moderate-quality |
| **CRC screening.** Screening for CRC by [[colonoscopy]] is recommended in persons at risk (first-degree relatives of those affected) or affected with LS every 1 to 2 years, beginning between ages 20–25 years or 2–5 years before the youngest age of diagnosis of CRC in the family if diagnosed before age 25 years. In surveillance of MMR gene mutation-positive patients, consideration should be given to annual colonoscopy. | Strong recommendation · evidence level III · GRADE moderate-quality |
| **Endometrial cancer screening.** Screening for EC should be offered to women at risk for or affected with LS by pelvic examination and endometrial sampling annually starting at age 30–35 years (Table 10). | Expert consensus · level V · GRADE low-quality |
| **Ovarian cancer screening.** Screening for ovarian cancer should be offered to women at risk for or affected with LS by transvaginal ultrasound annually starting at age 30–35 years (Table 10). | Expert consensus · level V · GRADE low-quality |
| **Risk-reducing gynecologic surgery.** Hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy should be recommended to women with LS who have finished childbearing or at age 40 years (Table 12). Patient considerations in this decision should include differences in uterine cancer risk depending on MMR gene mutation; morbidity of surgery; and the risk of menopausal symptoms, osteoporosis, and cardiac disease if hormone replacement therapy is not given. | Observational study · level IV · GRADE moderate-quality |
| **Gastric cancer screening.** Screening for gastric cancer should be considered in persons at risk for or affected with LS by [[upper-endoscopy\|EGD]] with gastric biopsy of the antrum at age 30–35 years with treatment of *H. pylori* infection when found. Subsequent surveillance every 2–3 years can be considered based on individual patient risk factors (Table 10). | Expert consensus · level V · GRADE low-quality |
| **Small bowel.** Routine screening of the small intestine is not recommended. | No evidence level or GRADE rating given |
| **Urinary tract screening.** Screening for cancer of the urinary tract should be considered for persons at risk for or affected with LS, with urinalysis annually starting at age 30–35 years (Table 10). | Expert consensus · level V · GRADE low-quality |
| **Pancreas.** Routine screening of the pancreas is not recommended. The benefit of screening for pancreatic cancer with this magnitude of risk is not established. | No evidence level or GRADE rating given |
| **Prostate and breast.** Routine screening of the prostate and breast cancer is not recommended beyond what is advised for the general population. | No evidence level or GRADE rating given |
| **Colectomy.** Colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis is the primary treatment of patients affected with LS with colon cancer or colon neoplasia not removable by endoscopy (Table 12). Consideration for less extensive surgery should be given in patients older than 60–65 years of age and those with underlying sphincter dysfunction. | Strong recommendation · level III evidence · GRADE moderate-quality |
| **Aspirin.** Growing but not conclusive evidence exists that use of aspirin is beneficial in preventing cancer in LS patients. Treatment of an individual patient with aspirin is a consideration after discussion of patient-specific risks, benefits, and uncertainties of treatment is conducted (Table 12). | Evidence from at least 1 randomized controlled trial · level I · GRADE moderate-quality |

### Genetic counseling

Genetic counseling is covered in narrative text, not in a graded Guideline box. The document states that expert groups advocate pre- and post-test genetic counseling by trained health care professionals, and that counseling sessions should include collection of personal and family medical history; education about the disorder; exploration of psychosocial dimensions; informed consent, including cost and risk of genetic discrimination; disclosure of test results; and follow-up, including the ability of the patient to recontact the counselor for future discoveries. Genetic testing should be offered to all at-risk relatives in families with known MMR/*EPCAM* gene mutations, where testing can be specific for the known family mutation.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Aspirin evidence rests on CAPP2 at 600 mg/day — a dose significantly higher than the 75 mg/d shown effective in sporadic CRC; optimum dose and duration unknown, with CAPP3 underway at the time of publication
- Urinary tract surveillance efficacy is uncertain — urine cytology sensitivity 29%, with 10-fold more false-positive than true-positive diagnoses; urinalysis is recommended on expert consensus alone
- Transvaginal ultrasound has poor sensitivity and specificity for endometrial cancer and adds limited yield over endometrial sampling
- Two large studies of extracolonic cancer in MMR mutation carriers came to opposite conclusions on small bowel cancer (lifetime risks 0.6% vs 12%)
- The relationship between LS and breast cancer is unclear — data are inconsistent across registry, British, German/Dutch, and prospective cohorts
- Sarcoma and laryngeal/hematologic malignancy associations with LS are described but not established
