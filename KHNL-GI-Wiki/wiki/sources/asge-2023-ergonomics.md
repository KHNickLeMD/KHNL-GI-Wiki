## Summary
This ASGE Standards of Practice Committee guideline provides an evidence-based, GRADE-graded approach to preventing endoscopy-related injury (ERI) in GI endoscopists. ERIs are musculoskeletal injuries caused by repetitive microtrauma to connective tissues. The guideline addresses three broad domains: (1) the rates and most common sites of ERI, (2) predictors of ERI, and (3) interventions that reduce ERI risk — dedicated ergonomics education, targeted stretching microbreaks, adjustable monitors for neutral posture, adjustable beds/stretchers for neutral posture, and antifatigue mats.

By pooled meta-analysis of 17 survey studies (5227 respondents), the overall rate of ERI was 57.7% (95% CI, 48.8-66.1). The most common sites were hands/fingers (35.8%), back (35.3%), upper back/neck (32.6%), thumb alone (29.2%), and neck alone (26.1%). Predictors of ERI include female gender (OR 1.79 for female vs male endoscopists), greater procedure volume, and more time performing endoscopy; data on hand size were inconsistent. Sixty-one percent of gastroenterologists report spending >40% of their time performing endoscopic procedures.

"Neutral posture" is defined as the body position where muscles are at resting length and joints are naturally aligned — achieved when endoscopists work with their joints at about the middle of their range of motion, allowing maximum control and strength while minimizing stress on joints and spine. The guideline issues two strong and three conditional recommendations, all on very low quality of evidence (most evidence is extrapolated from the laparoscopic surgical literature). Strong recommendations were made despite very low evidence because the panel placed high emphasis on preventing harm and on the low cost and ease of the interventions.

Beyond the five graded recommendations, the panel provides expert-opinion concept statements on hand size and ERI, load-reduction devices, lead apron–related ERI, and the need for robust endoscope maintenance programs.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Findings (epidemiology)
- **Finding 1:** Endoscopists report high rates of ERI. Pooled overall rate 57.7% (95% CI, 48.8-66.1; I²=93%). Most common sites: hands/fingers 35.8% (95% CI, 18.1-58.6), back 35.3% (95% CI, 24.3-48), upper back/neck 32.6% (95% CI, 21.3-46.3), thumb alone 29.2% (95% CI, 16.3-46.7), neck alone 26.1% (95% CI, 16.9-37.9).
- **Finding 2:** Female endoscopists are at higher risk of ERI compared with male endoscopists. ERI in female endoscopists 62.4% vs 45.5% in males; meta-analysis OR 1.79 (95% CI, 1.35-2.38; P<.01; I²=64%).
- **Finding 3:** Greater exposure to endoscopy procedures (time spent performing endoscopy and procedure volume) is associated with higher rates of ERI. Independent predictors include hours/week performing endoscopy, years in practice (>15 years), procedure volume >20 endoscopies/week, and >16 endoscopy hours/week.

### Recommendations (GRADE)
- **Recommendation 1 (Question 1 — should ergonomics education be implemented to reduce ERI risk?):** The ASGE recommends ergonomics education to reduce the risk of ERI. **(Strong recommendation, very low quality of evidence.)**
- **Recommendation 2 (Question 2 — do breaks decrease the risk of ERI?):** The ASGE suggests that GI endoscopists take microbreaks and scheduled macrobreaks to reduce the risk of ERI. **(Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.)**
- **Recommendation 3 (Question 3 — should a neutral monitor position be used to reduce ERI risk?):** The ASGE recommends a neutral monitor position during endoscopies to reduce the risk of ERI. **(Strong recommendation, very low quality of evidence.)**
- **Recommendation 4 (Question 4 — should a neutral bed height be used to reduce ERI?):** The ASGE recommends the use of a neutral bed height to reduce the risk of ERI. **(Strong recommendation, very low quality of evidence.)**
- **Recommendation 5 (Question 5 — should antifatigue floor mats be used to prevent ERI?):** The ASGE suggests the use of antifatigue mats to reduce the risk of ERI. **(Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.)**

### Supporting evidence and quantitative specifics
- **Ergonomics education:** Randomized trial (Khan et al, n=15) — ergonomics education improved Rapid Entire Body Assessment scores vs no training (P<.001). Ahmed et al (58 fellows) — teaching video improved ergonomics knowledge on pre/post tests. Markwell et al — individualized training with physical therapist assessment; 63% of endoscopists reported reduction or resolution of pain. No studies assessed cost-effectiveness; posters and video-based didactic training are low-cost, but physical therapy assessments and individualized plans may add cost.
- **Breaks (definitions):** *Microbreaks* = short biologically meaningful movement breaks from 30 seconds to 2 minutes. *Targeted stretching microbreaks (TSMBs)* = 1.5-minute stretching breaks at 20- to 40-minute intervals throughout each procedure targeting neck, shoulders, back, wrists, hands, knees, and ankles. *Macrobreaks* = scheduled breaks lasting 15 to 45 minutes built into a day's endoscopy schedule.
- **Breaks (evidence):** In a survey of 1698 endoscopists, microbreaks throughout the day were associated with lower odds of reporting ERIs (OR 0.69; 95% CI, 0.54-0.87); macrobreaks of 15-45 minutes also associated with lower odds (OR 0.72; 95% CI, 0.92-0.92 [as printed]); macrobreak duration not significantly associated with ERI (P=.50). Two surgical studies: TSMBs improved postprocedure pain, physical performance, and mental focus without negatively affecting operative duration. Mayo Clinic OR-Stretch intraoperative microbreak web application available.
- **Neutral monitor position:** Monitors should be placed directly in front of the endoscopist just below eye level, optimal viewing angle 15 to 25 degrees below the horizon, viewing distance 52 to 182 cm; monitor adjustable from 93 to 162 cm above the floor (to accommodate 5th percentile female to 95th percentile male eye height). Surgical data: neck strain lowest with monitor at eye level; task performance best with monitor directly in front; optimal monitor-surgeon distance 90-182 cm; max distance to still see finest detail 139-303 cm. Evidence down-rated for indirectness (surgical extrapolation) and imprecision.
- **Neutral bed/table height:** Optimal procedure table height adjusted to allow holding the endoscope between elbow height and 10 cm below elbow height; examination table height adjustable from 85 to 120 cm (5th percentile female to 95th percentile male elbow height). Berguer et al: optimal table height between elbow height and 10 cm below elbow, improved discomfort ratings. Van Veelen et al: neutral bed height kept surgeon's joints in neutral positions.
- **Antifatigue mats:** Prolonged standing implicated in lower-extremity tiredness/swelling, venous blood restriction, low-back pain, whole-body tiredness. Haramis et al (RCT, gel mat, 18 providers, laparoscopic renal surgery): less pain in feet (P=.003), knees (P=.001), back (P=.001), lower overall discomfort (P=.001), higher energy (P=.049), benefits persisting 24 h. Graversen et al: improved postoperative discomfort in 11 urologists performing cystoscopy. Risks: contamination/biohazard, tripping hazard; mats should be cleanable with beveled edges.

### Other considerations (expert opinion, no systematic review)
- **Hand size and ERI:** Data inconsistent and could not be pooled; large national study found no significant difference in ERI by glove size. However, small-handed endoscopists had longer colonoscope insertion times (9.4 vs 8.2 min, P=.04) and more injury sites (P=.03), leading to fewer colonoscopies performed vs large-handed providers. 97.4% of fellows with small hand size (glove ≤6.5) were women. Reusable dial adaptors for smaller hands include the MAJ-1072 auxiliary angle knob cover (Olympus) and the OE-B1 right/left assistant knob (Pentax); a pilot study (hand span <19 cm) showed a trend toward decreased procedure time without significant difference. Olympus discontinued its device. Panelists noted stigma concerns and called for more funding.
- **Load-reduction devices:** Endoscope support stand and antigravity arm are being evaluated but are not currently available in the United States.
- **Lead apron–related ERI:** Lead aprons used during fluoroscopic/luminal stent procedures (eg, ERCP, EUS-guided biliary interventions) increase load on trunk muscles and intervertebral discs. 22% (222/1277) surveyed reported lead apron–related GI ERI; men reported more than women (26.5% vs 14.3%, P<.001). Lead aprons not systematically studied in ERCP; 2-piece lead aprons produced less discomfort by redistributing weight to the hips.
- **Endoscope maintenance programs:** All endoscopy units should have a robust endoscope maintenance program to identify suboptimally performing scopes; wear and tear makes scopes less responsive, requiring greater forces and increasing ERI risk.

### Future directions / update
- Open questions on risk factors and mechanisms, gender differences, pregnancy and ERI, hand size, TSMBs, optimal schedules, room design optimization, and quality-improvement impact. Call for manufacturers to improve endoscope design and implement maintenance programs. Guidelines reviewed for updates approximately every 5 years.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- Most recommendations rest on very low quality evidence extrapolated from the laparoscopic surgical literature; two are strong despite this because of high value placed on harm prevention and low intervention cost.
- Internal discrepancy in the document on the evidence grade for Recommendation 1 (graphical abstract "low" vs Table 1/text "very low").
- Hand-size data are inconsistent and could not be pooled; relationship to ERI remains uncertain.
