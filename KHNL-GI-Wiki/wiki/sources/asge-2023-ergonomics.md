## Summary
This American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) Standards of Practice Committee guideline gives an evidence-based, Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)-graded approach to preventing endoscopy-related injury (ERI) in gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopists. ERIs are musculoskeletal injuries caused by repetitive microtrauma to the connective tissues of the body. The guideline covers three broad categories: (1) rates and most common sites of ERI, (2) predictors of ERI, and (3) interventions that reduce ERI risk — dedicated ergonomics education, targeted stretching microbreaks, adjustable monitors for neutral posture, adjustable patient beds/stretchers for neutral posture, and antifatigue mats.

Scope of the problem: 61% of gastroenterologists report spending >40% of their time performing endoscopic procedures; survey studies report ERI prevalence of 39%-89% in practicing gastroenterologists and 20%-47% in gastroenterology trainees. Biomechanical forces implicated in ERI are high-force loads applied in non-neutral postures — torque steering (right wrist extensors), grasping and stabilizing the endoscope controller (left forearm extensors), manipulating the endoscope dial (left thumb abductors), and high-risk pinching. Long-term consequences range from pain and physical restrictions during procedures to disability, with downstream provider dissatisfaction and loss of a highly skilled workforce.

"Neutral posture" is defined in the ergonomics literature as the position of the body where muscles are at resting length and joints are naturally aligned — achieved when endoscopists work with their joints at about the middle point of their range of motion, allowing maximum control and strength while minimizing stress on joints and spine. It is the principle behind the monitor-position and bed-height recommendations.

The guideline issues 5 numbered recommendations (3 strong, 2 conditional), all on very low quality of evidence; for the monitor, bed-height, and antifatigue-mat questions the search identified no gastroenterology studies at all and the panel extrapolated from the laparoscopic surgical literature, down-rating for indirectness and imprecision. Strong recommendations were made despite very low evidence because the panel placed high emphasis on preventing harm to endoscopists and on the low cost and ease of the interventions. Beyond the 5 graded recommendations, the panel offers ungraded expert-opinion concept statements on hand size and ERI, load-reduction devices, lead apron-related ERI, and endoscope maintenance programs; no systematic reviews were conducted for these.

**The document's own GRADE vocabulary:** recommendations are categorized as **strong** or **conditional** — *"recommend"* is used for strong recommendations and *"suggest"* for conditional recommendations. The panel included 2 content experts, a GRADE methodologist, Standards of Practice Committee members, and the Standards of Practice Chair; because the subject was ERI there were no patient representatives, and all panel members (practicing endoscopists) served as "patient representatives."

### Supporting evidence and quantitative specifics
- **Ergonomics education (Rec 1):** systematic review identified 6 studies; outcomes pain and formalized ergonomic assessments.
  - Randomized trial (Khan et al, 15 fellows) — improved Rapid Entire Body Assessment scores vs no training (P<.001); the training comprised didactic sessions, a video on ergonomics, ergonomic-specific feedback from supervisors, and an ergonomic checklist to review.
  - Prospective nonrandomized study (Ahmed et al, 58 gastroenterology fellows) — teaching video improved ergonomics knowledge on pre-/post-training tests.
  - Markwell et al — individualized training with physical therapist assessment; **63%** of endoscopists reported reduction or resolution of pain.
  - **Operative qualifier:** the panel recommended that **all endoscopists pursue some form of ergonomics education, at minimum in the form of a didactic session.**
  - Delivery options named: online courses, in-person teaching, physical therapist consultation, short written guides/posters hanging in the endoscopy unit, short videos — ASGE training curriculum, ASGE video "Ergonomic Essentials for Your Practice" (GI LEAP website), the VideoGIE series on endoscopy ergonomics, YouTube videos.
  - No studies assessed cost-effectiveness; posters and video-based didactic training are low-cost, but physical therapy assessments and individualized plans may incur additional cost. The panel noted no standardized approach to ergonomics education yet exists, so responsibility for being educated remains with the endoscopist.
- **Breaks (definitions):** *Microbreaks* = short biologically meaningful movement breaks from 30 seconds to 2 minutes. *Targeted stretching microbreaks (TSMBs)* = 1.5-minute stretching breaks at 20- to 40-minute intervals throughout each procedure targeting neck, shoulders, back, wrists, hands, knees, and ankles. *Macrobreaks* = scheduled breaks lasting 15 to 45 minutes built into a day's endoscopy schedule.
- **Breaks (evidence, Rec 2):** 3 studies included; outcomes reduced rates of ERI and improvement in postprocedure pain scores.
  - Survey of 1698 endoscopists — microbreaks throughout the day associated with lower odds of reporting ERIs (OR 0.69; 95% CI, 0.54-0.87); longer breaks (macrobreaks) ranging from 15 to 45 minutes also associated with lower odds (OR 0.72; 95% CI, 0.92-0.92 [as printed]); **duration of macrobreaks was not significantly associated with ERI (P=.50)**.
  - 2 surgical studies — TSMBs improved postprocedure pain, physical performance, and mental focus **without negatively affecting operative duration**.
  - Certainty rated down for imprecision (small number of studies and patients) and the panel voiced concern about extrapolating from surgical laparoscopic literature, since surgical procedures generally have longer procedure times; it agreed the surgical literature could guide breaks until GI work-and-rest-schedule data exist.
  - Practical qualifiers: microbreaks pose **minimal to no risk** and **no significant impact on procedure duration**, and can be incorporated into individual schedules by endoscopists; **macrobreaks may require administrative support**. ASGE pre-/postprocedural exercise materials (GI LEAP website) and the Mayo Clinic human factors engineering OR-Stretch intraoperative microbreak web application are available.
- **Neutral monitor position (Rec 3):** no gastroenterology studies identified; 3 laparoscopic surgical studies on optimal monitor position (outcomes: task performance, neck muscle strain, electromyographic activity of the main neck muscles).
  - Target setup: monitor **directly in front** of the endoscopist, **just below eye level**; optimal viewing angle **15 to 25 degrees below the horizon** from the eyes; viewing distance **52 to 182 cm**; monitor adjustable **93 to 162 cm above the floor** (accommodates 5th percentile female to 95th percentile male eye height).
  - Ergonomic stance: neutral neck and back without hyperextension or flexion, even weight distribution between both legs, avoidance of knee hyperextension. Monitor booms and mobile stands facilitate flexible positioning.
  - Surgical data: neck strain lowest with the monitor in front at the surgeon's eye level; task performance best with the monitor directly in front (not to the right or left); optimal monitor-surgeon distance **90 to 182 cm**; maximum distance at which the finest image detail could still be seen **139 to 303 cm**.
  - Evidence down-rated for indirectness (surgical extrapolation) and imprecision (very small sample size in each study) → overall quality very low. Units should make **all** monitors adjustable; endoscopists are strongly encouraged to adjust the monitor **before starting a procedure**.
- **Neutral bed/table height (Rec 4):** no gastroenterology studies identified; 2 observational laparoscopic surgical studies on optimal procedure table position.
  - Target setup: table height adjusted to allow holding the endoscope **between elbow height and 10 cm below elbow height**; examination table adjustable **85 to 120 cm** (5th percentile female to 95th percentile male elbow height).
  - Berguer et al — optimal table height between elbow height and 10 cm below elbow height, associated with significant improvement in discomfort rating. Van Veelen et al — neutral bed height kept the surgeon's joints in neutral positions.
  - Down-rated for indirectness and imprecision → very low quality; made strong because adjustable stretchers and beds are already widespread in endoscopy units and the change costs nothing in most settings.
- **Antifatigue mats (Rec 5):** no gastroenterology studies identified; 2 surgical studies. Prolonged standing directly implicated in lower-extremity tiredness and discomfort, lower-extremity swelling, venous blood restriction, low-back pain, and whole-body tiredness.
  - Haramis et al (randomized study of a gel mat vs no mat during laparoscopic renal surgeries, 50 per arm, 18 providers): less pain in the feet (P=.003), knees (P=.001), and back (P=.001); lower overall discomfort (P=.001); higher levels of overall energy (P=.049); **benefits still present 24 hours postoperatively**.
  - Graversen et al: significant improvement in postoperative discomfort in 11 urologists performing cystoscopy. Both studies were urologic → results may not be entirely applicable to GI endoscopy; evidence ranged from very low to low, rated down for indirectness and imprecision.
  - Risks and specifications: contamination/potential biohazard from routine use during GI procedures → mats must be **easily and regularly cleanable**; also a potential tripping hazard → **beveled edges** to minimize the risk of falls. The panel judged benefits to outweigh these risks.

### Other considerations — general concept statements
*Expert opinion of the multidisciplinary panel, offered "for endoscopists, in special circumstances, to reduce the risk of ERI." **No systematic reviews were conducted** for any statement below, and none carries a strength or quality grade or a Table 1 number.*

- **Ancillary devices:** the structured abstract states *"We suggest the use of ancillary devices in those with risk factors predisposing them to ERI."* This appears only in the abstract; it is **not** one of the 5 numbered, graded recommendations, and the body treats devices as ungraded concept statements.
- **Hand size and ERI:** data are inconsistent and could not be pooled; 4 studies examined ERI rate and hand size.
  - Large national study — **no** significant difference in ERI rate: small glove size 78.13% (288 respondents) vs large glove size 75.95% (682 respondents), P=.12; extra-small/small/medium 74.74% (855 respondents) vs large/extra-large 77.05% (828 respondents), P=.27. 2 international studies showed similar results.
  - A recent anthropometric study — small-handed endoscopists (small and medium glove size) had **longer colonoscope insertion times (9.4 vs 8.2 minutes, P=.04)** and an increased number of injury sites (P=.03), leading to a decreased number of colonoscopies performed vs large-handed providers.
  - Survey of 227 gastroenterology fellows (Cohen et al) — a significant number of trainees believed small hand size negatively impacted overall training; **97.4%** of those with a small hand size (**glove size ≤6.5**) were women.
  - **2 reusable dial adaptors** are current options for smaller hands: **MAJ-1072 auxiliary angle knob cover** (Olympus) and **OE-B1 right/left assistant knob** (Pentax). These rubber extensions clip onto the standard right/left angulation control knob in the control body section, so the knob can be more easily reached and manipulated by smaller hands.
  - Pilot study of an angulation dial adaptor for **hand spans <19 cm** (hand span = thumb to fifth digit): trend toward decreased procedure time, but **no significant difference in procedure duration or ease of procedure**; the **retroflexion maneuver was rated significantly easier** by all endoscopists with the adapter. The devices are inexpensive, easy to use, safe, non-disposable, and must be processed per manufacturer recommendations. Olympus recently discontinued its device without notice.
  - The panel acknowledged the importance of keeping such a device available in the endoscopy unit, noted the need to dispel stigma around using assist devices, and advocated for more funding for device development and endoscope redesign.
- **Load-reduction devices:** the endoscope support stand and the antigravity arm are being evaluated for reducing ERI but are **not currently available in the United States** for use.
- **Lead apron-related ERI:** lead aprons used during procedures requiring fluoroscopy (eg, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography [ERCP], endoscopic ultrasound [EUS]-guided biliary interventions, luminal stent placement) place additional loads on the trunk muscles and intervertebral discs. **222 of 1277 (22%)** of those surveyed reported ERI specifically related to lead apron use; **men reported more than women — 26.5% vs 14.3% (P<.001)** of those affected. Lead aprons have **not been systematically studied in ERCP**; **2-piece lead aprons** produce less discomfort by redistributing a portion of the weight across the hips from the upper body.
- **Endoscope maintenance programs:** all endoscopy units should have a robust endoscope maintenance program to identify suboptimally performing endoscopes. This protects against the usual wear and tear, which makes the endoscope less responsive to maneuvers over time and requires the endoscopist to expend greater forces for the same task, thereby increasing ERI risk.

### Future directions / update
- Open questions the document names: risk factors and mechanisms for ERIs; gender differences in type of ERI, overall rate, and impact in female gastroenterologists; pregnancy and risk of ERI; impact of hand size; TSMBs; optimal endoscopy schedules including micro- and macrobreaks; endoscopy room design optimization (monitor height, bed height); and the impact of quality-improvement initiatives on ergonomic practices.
- Calls on endoscope manufacturers to improve current endoscope design to mitigate biomechanical exposures and to implement optimal endoscopy management programs; calls for more funding to study effective ergonomic interventions.
- **Guideline update:** ASGE guidelines are reviewed for updates approximately every 5 years, or if new data may influence a recommendation; updates follow the same ASGE guideline development process.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- All 5 recommendations rest on **very low** quality of evidence. For the monitor, bed-height, and antifatigue-mat questions no gastroenterology studies exist at all and the evidence is extrapolated from the laparoscopic surgical literature; 3 of the 5 are nonetheless **strong** because the panel placed high value on preventing harm to endoscopists and the interventions are low-cost and easy to implement.
- **Internal discrepancy in the document on evidence grades.** The graphical abstract grades both the ergonomics-education recommendation and the neutral-monitor-position recommendation as *"Strong recommendation, low quality of evidence,"* while Table 1 and the SUMMARY OF RECOMMENDATIONS text grade both *"very low quality of evidence."* The Table 1 and recommendation-text grading is what this page carries.
- **Numbering differs between the graphical abstract and Table 1.** The graphical abstract orders the recommendations education (1), neutral monitor (2), neutral bed height (3), antifatigue mats (4), micro- and macrobreaks (5); Table 1 and the recommendation text order them education (1), breaks (2), monitor (3), bed height (4), mats (5). This page follows Table 1.
- The abstract's suggestion to use **ancillary devices in those with risk factors predisposing them to ERI** has no counterpart among the 5 numbered recommendations and no strength or quality grade; in the body, assist devices appear only as ungraded expert-opinion concept statements.
- **Hand-size data are inconsistent and could not be pooled**; the relationship between hand size and ERI remains uncertain, even though small-handed endoscopists showed longer insertion times and more injury sites in one anthropometric study.
- Lead aprons have not been systematically studied in ERCP, and load-reduction devices (endoscope support stand, antigravity arm) remain under evaluation and unavailable in the United States.
