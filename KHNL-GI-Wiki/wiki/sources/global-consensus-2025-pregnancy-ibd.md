## Summary

The Helmsley PIANO (Pregnancy in Inflammatory Bowel Disease and Neonatal Outcomes) global consensus is a multi-society, multi-continent statement covering the whole reproductive arc in [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]]: maternal factors, fertility, preconception optimization, management of active disease in pregnancy, management of the pregnancy itself, medications in pregnancy and in lactation, pregnancy adverse events, fetal/neonatal adverse events, and infant vaccines. Its organizing principle, stated explicitly, is that **maternal health best supports infant health** — stringent disease control, not drug avoidance, is what protects the pregnancy.

Methods were deliberately mixed. Where comparative observational data existed, the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) process was used, producing **34 GRADE recommendations** (Table 1). Where only case series or animal data existed — mostly the newer small molecules — a **RAND/UCLA appropriateness panel** produced **35 Consensus statements** (Table 2), rated 1–9 by 39 panelists with a disagreement index, re-rated after a 2-day in-person meeting. The literature search ran from inception to December 2023. There are no randomized controlled trials in pregnant women with IBD, so **every GRADE statement rests on low or very low quality evidence**; several were upgraded to *Strong* only because of potential catastrophic harm.

Practically, the document is organized around six tables that carry most of the decision content: preconception care (Table 5), contraception (Table 6), assessing disease activity in pregnancy (Table 7), trimester-by-trimester management (Table 8), and the drug-by-drug preconception→pregnancy→lactation grid (Table 9). The drug grid splits IBD therapy three ways: **appropriate throughout** (aminosalicylates, thiopurines, corticosteroids, anti-tumor necrosis factor [TNF], anti-integrin, anti-interleukin [IL]-12/23 and anti-IL-23), **avoid** (methotrexate), and **avoid if possible, use only if no other viable option for maternal health** (Janus kinase inhibitors [JAKis], sphingosine-1-phosphate [S1P] receptor modulators).

The consensus also moves two long-standing practices. Live **rotavirus** vaccine *may* now be given to infants with in utero biologic exposure (previously withheld), while live **Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG)** must still be avoided for the first 6 months after in utero anti-TNF exposure because of fatal disseminated tuberculosis. And low-dose **aspirin 150–162 mg starting at 12–16 weeks** is suggested for all pregnant women with IBD to prevent preterm preeclampsia.

### Consensus Statements (Table 2, RAND/UCLA; numbered by the document; ungraded)

**Maternal factors impacting pregnancy**
1. Children born to a parent with CD may have a higher risk of developing IBD than children born to a parent with UC.

**Fertility**
2. Women with IBD may have reduced fertility vs women without IBD, due to reduced ovarian reserve.
3. Women with IBD may undergo **oocyte retrieval without increased risk of flare**.

**Preconceptional counseling and optimization**
4. Women with IBD desiring contraception should use **long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) over estrogen-containing contraceptives**.
5. Women with IBD should be in **documented remission and medically optimized prior to elective conception**.

**Management of disease activity during pregnancy**
6. **Endoscopy in pregnancy is low risk** but should only be performed if it may change management.
7. If cross-sectional imaging is needed in pregnancy, **intestinal ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without gadolinium are preferred to computed tomography (CT)**.
8. **Fecal calprotectin (FC) is useful** for monitoring disease activity in pregnant women with IBD.

**Management of pregnancy**
9. Pregnancies in women with IBD should be considered **high risk** for complications.
10. Women with **current or past rectovaginal fistula** should deliver by Cesarean.
11. Women with IBD should be assessed **early in pregnancy or preconception** for nutritional status, weight gain, and micronutrient deficiency.

**Medications during pregnancy**
12. Pregnant women with active disease should **start or optimize the same appropriate therapies as nonpregnant patients, except thiopurines, methotrexate, JAKis, and S1P receptor modulators**.
13. If thiopurines are continued in pregnancy, take precautions for **intrahepatic cholestasis**: measure liver enzymes and metabolite levels, and consider **split dosing**.
14. Pregnant women with infections, fistula, or pouchitis requiring antibiotics **may take an appropriate course of a low-risk antibiotic**.
15. **Calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus)** may be initiated or continued in pregnancy with careful monitoring **if there are no viable alternate treatment options**.
16. Pregnant or attempting conception — **continue biosimilars** to existing biologics.
17. Pregnant or attempting conception — **continue anti-IL-23 therapy throughout pregnancy** (mirikizumab, risankizumab, guselkumab).
18. **Discontinue ozanimod at least 3 months prior to conception** unless there is no effective alternative therapy to maintain maternal health.
19. **Discontinue etrasimod at least 1–2 weeks prior to conception** unless there is no effective alternative.
20. **Discontinue tofacitinib at least 4 weeks prior to conception** unless there is no effective alternative.
21. **Discontinue upadacitinib at least 4 weeks prior to conception** unless there is no effective alternative.
22. **Discontinue filgotinib at least 4 weeks prior to conception** unless there is no effective alternative.

**Medications during lactation** *(statements 23–31: may breastfeed / should not breastfeed)*
23. On 5-ASA / sulfasalazine — **may breastfeed**.
24. On thiopurines — **may breastfeed**.
25. On corticosteroids — **may breastfeed**.
26. On anti-TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab, certolizumab) — **may breastfeed**.
27. On anti-integrins (vedolizumab, natalizumab) — **may breastfeed**.
28. On anti-IL-12/23 and anti-IL-23 agents (ustekinumab, risankizumab, mirikizumab, guselkumab) — **may breastfeed**.
29. On biosimilars — **may breastfeed**.
30. On S1P receptor modulators (etrasimod, ozanimod) — **should not breastfeed**.
31. On JAKis (tofacitinib, upadacitinib, filgotinib) — **should not breastfeed**.

**Pregnancy adverse events**
32. **Controlling disease activity during pregnancy is critical** to reduce adverse outcomes.

**Vaccines**
33. **Inactive vaccines should be given on schedule** to infants of women with IBD **regardless of in utero IBD medication exposure**.
34. Children exposed in utero to **JAKis or S1P receptor modulators may receive live vaccines after 1 month of age**.
35. **Live vaccines can be given to infants of mothers breastfeeding while on biologics.**

### Preconceptional Care (Table 5)

- Cessation of smoking and other substances (alcohol, recreational drugs, opioids, cannabis).
- **Stop potentially teratogenic medications and establish remission on the new therapy** — methotrexate: cease **at least 1 month prior to conception**; JAKis and S1P receptor modulators per the medication statements.
- **Disease remission 3–6 months prior to conception** on optimal therapy for pregnancy:
  - Clinical and biochemical remission — **normal C-reactive protein (CRP), FC < 150**.
  - Optimize serum drug concentrations (thiopurines, anti-TNF agents).
  - Endoscopic and/or radiographic remission — intestinal ultrasound, or magnetic resonance enterography or CT.
- Nutritional assessment.
- **Prenatal vitamin containing folic acid at least 1 month prior to conception**
  - **2 mg folic acid daily for 3 months prior to conception if on sulfasalazine**.
  - **5 mg folic acid daily** if extensive small bowel disease, malabsorption, or family history of neural tube defects.
- Achieve ideal weight range; record baseline weight to monitor adequate gestational weight gain (GWG).
- **Vaccinations and cervical cancer screening up to date** — annual influenza; hepatitis B; measles-mumps-rubella (MMR); varicella. MMR and varicella are **live attenuated → avoided on high-dose steroid, thiopurine and/or biologic therapy**; **live vaccines must be given 28 days prior to pregnancy**.
- Preconceptional maternal-fetal medicine and/or colorectal surgeon review where relevant; communicate the IBD treatment plan to the rest of the health care team.
- **Fertility specialist review if not pregnant after 6 months of timed intercourse.**
- Encourage regular physical activity.

### Contraception (Table 6)

| Method | Disadvantages | Benefits |
|---|---|---|
| Barrier | Least effective — emphasize correct use | No side effects; protection against sexually transmitted infection |
| Oral contraceptive pill — combined (COCP) or progesterone-only (POP) | Increased VTE risk with COCP and higher unintended pregnancies. Consider an alternative if higher VTE risk (past VTE, current smoking, active disease, on steroids or JAKis, recent surgery). POP **must be taken at the same time each day (within 3 hours)** | COCP may improve cyclical IBD symptoms; **no increased VTE risk with POP** |
| LARC — intrauterine device (IUD) or contraceptive implant | Longer-term (**IUD 5–10 years, implant 3 years**); consider if not planning pregnancy within 1 year; cost and insertion pain | **Most effective (failure rate <1%)**; no estrogen, no absorption or VTE concerns; levonorgestrel IUD may improve cyclical IBD symptoms |

### Assessing Disease Activity in Pregnancy (Table 7)

| Modality | Test | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Laboratory | Serum inflammatory markers — CRP, sedimentation rate | **Can be elevated from pregnancy** |
| Laboratory | FC | **Effective in pregnancy** |
| Laboratory | Serum drug concentrations — thiopurines, anti-TNF | **May vary in pregnancy** |
| Cross-sectional imaging | Intestinal ultrasound | Low risk; accurate in trimesters 1 and 2, **technically challenging in trimester 3** |
| Cross-sectional imaging | CT | Relatively safe — cumulative radiation of a single CT (**~50 mGy**) is below the level of concern |
| Cross-sectional imaging | MRI | Low risk. **Avoid gadolinium (potential teratogen) during the first trimester** |
| Procedures | Endoscopy | Low risk; perform **if indicated and if it will change management** |
| Procedures | Surgery | **Perform if indicated regardless of trimester**; at expert centers. Indications: acute refractory colitis, perforation, abscess, refractory hemorrhage, bowel obstruction |

- **FC cut-off > 100 mcg/g** had the best sensitivity and negative predictive value for presence/absence of disease activity (vs Physician's Global Assessment, Harvey-Bradshaw Index in CD, partial Mayo in UC) in a prospective cohort of 157 pregnancies.
- FC is **unaffected by pregnancy physiology** in healthy women without IBD — median 29.5 mcg/g at 16 weeks, 25.6 at 34 weeks, 23.4 at 4 weeks postpartum, 29.4 at 12 weeks postpartum (no significant change).
- Intestinal ultrasound in pregnancy: **sensitivity 74%, specificity 83%, negative predictive value 90%** vs FC in one study; **sensitivity 84%, specificity 98%** in a second. Sigmoid and terminal ileum visualization **decreases in the third trimester**.
- Most IBD endoscopy in pregnancy is **flexible sigmoidoscopy** — low risk, needs neither preparation nor sedation. Full colonoscopy or an advanced procedure → **fetal monitoring recommended**. **Capsule endoscopy should be avoided** in pregnancy (obstruction risk in CD).

### Supporting Commentary — Pregnancy

- **Placental transfer.** Monoclonal antibodies are too large to passively cross during organogenesis (**weeks 3–8**) but actively cross via Fc receptors **after week 14**, with **80% of transfer in the third trimester**. **Certolizumab is a Fab' fragment without an Fc portion → does not actively cross.** Natalizumab and mirikizumab have an immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4) backbone (transferred less efficiently than IgG1); all other IBD biologics are IgG1. Small molecules passively traverse the placenta throughout, including during organogenesis.
- **Dosing.** Infliximab concentrations rise in later trimesters despite unchanged dosing → **use stable pre-pregnancy dosing regardless of pregnancy weight gain**. Maternal vedolizumab concentrations fall with rising weight; ustekinumab concentrations stay stable.
- **Aspirin.** Benefit only when started **≤16 weeks gestation at a daily dose ≥100 mg**; for IBD, with obstetric guidance, **150–162 mg starting week 12–16**. Many practice guidelines suggest **stopping after 36–37 weeks** (delivery/postpartum bleeding). Low-dose aspirin did **not** increase IBD flares in two cohorts (16.9% vs 14.4%; and odds ratio 1.27, 95% confidence interval 0.55–2.94).
- **Nutrition.** Evaluate folate, iron, vitamin B12 and vitamin D preconception and in the first trimester. **Folic acid 0.4 mg/d for at least 4 weeks before conception** (general guidance); higher doses may be needed in IBD. **Elemental iron 30–60 mg/d** for all pregnant women (World Health Organization), **up to 100 mg/d** in IBD. **Intravenous iron only in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters — avoid in the first trimester (possible teratogenicity).** Calcium if dietary intake is low; **routine calcium and vitamin D for women on corticosteroids** to prevent steroid-associated osteopenia. Restrictive diets should be dietitian-supervised; record weight and body mass index at every antenatal visit.
- **Mode of delivery.** Number of children desired matters — **>3 Cesareans can cause complications such as uterine rupture**. Active perianal CD carries an **11-fold higher risk of 4th-degree perineal tear**; independent predictors of postpartum perianal flare were prior perianal disease (odds ratio 22) and active perianal CD (odds ratio 96). For **inactive** perianal disease the case for Cesarean is less clear, but caution with prior complex disease and avoid prolonged labor. **Rectovaginal fistula (active or prior) → planned Cesarean.** With IPAA, Cesarean should ideally be done at a center with colorectal surgical support; anorectal manometry after IPAA showed higher mean squeeze pressure after Cesarean than vaginal delivery (**150 vs 120 mm Hg**), with more anterior sphincter defects after vaginal delivery.
- **VTE.** Relative risk ~**1.72 during pregnancy and 2.10 postpartum** (Danish population data). Prophylaxis should be used in hospital and **particularly after Cesarean delivery**.
- **Thiopurines.** Metabolism shifts in late pregnancy (**6-methylmercaptopurine [6-MMP] : 6-thioguanine nucleotide [6-TGN] ratio increases**) → measure metabolites in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters to avoid high 6-MMP and hepatotoxicity. Thiopurines are associated with **intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy** (background incidence 1.1%), the basis of a United States Food and Drug Administration warning. If shunting occurs, trial **split dosing**. **Allopurinol data are insufficient to recommend routine continuation in pregnancy.** Combination thiopurine + anti-TNF was the only exposure linked to higher serious infection in the first 5 years (adjusted hazard ratio 1.36).
- **Corticosteroids.** Associated with gestational diabetes, preterm birth, LBW, SGA, intrauterine growth restriction and NICU admission in registry data; **late (2nd/3rd trimester) use was associated with serious infections at 9 and 12 months**. Budesonide multimatrix **9 mg once daily** may be considered for mild-moderate UC not responding to oral + topical 5-ASA (only 4 reported pregnancy cases).
- **Methotrexate.** Cessation **1–3 months prior to conception** allows clearance of methotrexate conjugates and transition to another therapy. **Exposure should not necessitate termination**; continue or start folic acid, which reduces the relative risk of cardiac defects with dihydrofolate reductase inhibitors.
- **Antibiotics.** Metronidazole, amoxicillin ± clavulanic acid and ciprofloxacin are well-supported as low risk; **no published human data on rifaximin in pregnancy**. Group B streptococcus testing around **week 36** with intrapartum intravenous penicillin-based antibiotics if positive.
- **Small molecules.** JAKi and S1P modulator teratogenicity signals come from animal studies. Human data: 2 serious congenital malformations in 55 live births exposed to tofacitinib, 1 in 21 with filgotinib, 0 in 30 with upadacitinib. **Both classes are relatively contraindicated in pregnancy**, but women with no immediate conception plans should not be denied them.
- **Surgery.** The "operate only in the second trimester" adage is **not supported by modern data**; elective surgery is deferred until after pregnancy, urgent/emergent surgery is done when required.

### Supporting Commentary — Lactation

- **Relative infant dose (RID) ≤10%** of the weight-adjusted maternal dose is considered low risk.
- **5-ASA**: RID <1%; **sulfasalazine** itself RID <1% but the sulfapyridine metabolite reaches higher levels — **non-sulfonamide mesalamine derivatives are preferred over sulfasalazine**.
- **Thiopurines**: RID <1%; no difference in growth, development or infection in breastfed vs non-breastfed infants.
- **Allopurinol + thiopurine**: **breastfeeding should be avoided** — little supportive data and a report of possible harm.
- **Methotrexate**: RID 0.11%, but the active metabolite 7-hydroxymethotrexate is detectable in milk → **breastfeeding should be avoided on methotrexate**. If low-dose weekly methotrexate (**≤25 mg/wk**) is used, no alternative exists, and the mother strongly wishes to breastfeed, it may be considered with close infant monitoring, **not initiated within 24 hours of the dose**.
- **Prednisone/prednisolone**: "worst-case" milk amounts <100 mcg/d even at maternal doses up to 120 mg/d; **the old advice to wait 4 hours after a dose is unnecessary**. **Budesonide**: systemic RID ~0.27%.
- **Monoclonal antibodies**: maximum milk concentration <1% of maternal serum; ~50% of ingested IgG is digested in the infant gut and ~0.01% absorbed → **an older breastfed infant receives an RID <0.005%**. Breastfeeding does not affect neonatal clearance of infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab or ustekinumab.
- **Tofacitinib**: detected in all 55 milk samples from 2 lactating women; worst-case **RID 3.4%** — under the 10% cutoff, but with a 3.2-hour half-life the label's advice to withhold breastfeeding **18 hours (immediate release) or 36 hours (sustained release)** after a dose makes regular breastfeeding infeasible. Upadacitinib and filgotinib milk excretion unknown; both labels advise avoidance.
- **Sedation for endoscopy**: midazolam — ASGE 2012 advises withholding nursing **4 hours** after a dose (**6–8 hours** with a newborn or preterm infant); **fentanyl and propofol need no waiting period or milk discard** — resume once the mother has recovered enough to nurse.
- **Contrast**: iodinated contrast RID <0.01%; gadolinium absorbed <0.0004%. **Breastfeeding need not be interrupted**; if the mother is concerned, 24 hours of abstinence is more than adequate.
- **Postpartum pain after Cesarean**: multimodal, first-line scheduled acetaminophen and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs); opiates reserved for breakthrough. Intravenous ketorolac did **not** increase clinical disease activity to 6 weeks postpartum in 246 treated patients with IBD, and reduced pain scores and opiate need.
- **Stopping IBD medications postpartum to breastfeed is itself a risk** — women who stopped before breastfeeding had more postpartum disease activity.

### Supporting Commentary — Infant Vaccines

- Infant adaptive immunity (T- and B-cell subsets) develops **unaltered** after in utero anti-TNF monotherapy or combination therapy; serologic responses to tetanus, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, hepatitis B, pneumococcus and diphtheria are adequate on the standard schedule.
- **Rotavirus** (live, given **between 2 and 6 months** of age; later administration risks intussusception): prospective data in 168 infants exposed to biologics (anti-TNF, vedolizumab, ustekinumab) showed **no serious adverse events**; a further 65-infant Spanish registry and a 46-case systematic review likewise. Infant drug clearance: vedolizumab median **11 weeks** (range 5–19), ustekinumab **14 weeks** (range 9–36).
- **BCG**: in 215 infants vaccinated in the first year after in utero biologic exposure (>80% at ≤6 months), **one died of disseminated BCG infection at 4.5 months** after third-trimester infliximab exposure and vaccination at 3 months; 4 further BCG-related deaths after in utero anti-TNF exposure were reported to the United Kingdom regulator. **All reported adverse events occurred in infants vaccinated at <6 months.** In high-risk countries where BCG must be given early, **measure infant serum drug concentrations before vaccinating**, and consider **holding the maternal biologic in mid-pregnancy** to reduce transfer.
- **JAKis / S1P modulators**: short half-lives mean they should be undetectable within the first days after birth → inactive vaccines on schedule and **live vaccines after 1 month of age**; inform the child's physician to monitor.
- **Live vaccines while breastfeeding on a biologic**: a 2022 European Medicines Agency communication advised against live vaccines in infants breastfed by mothers on infliximab unless infant levels are undetectable; the consensus **does not adopt that restriction** — based on negligible IgG transfer across breast milk and registry safety data, **live vaccines should not be withheld** from infants breastfeeding from mothers on biologic therapy.

- **[[thiopurines]]** — continue in pregnancy; metabolite/liver-enzyme monitoring, split dosing, intrahepatic cholestasis risk; breastfeeding compatible.
- **[[anti-tnf-agents]]**, **[[vedolizumab]]**, **[[il-23-and-il-12-23-inhibitors]]** — continue throughout pregnancy; lactation compatible; infant clearance times and live-vaccine implications.
- **[[mesalamine-5-asa]]** — continue; sulfasalazine folic acid **2 mg/d for 3 months preconception**; mesalamine preferred over sulfasalazine during lactation.
- **[[corticosteroids-ibd]]** — use when clinically necessary with monitoring; steroid-sparing emphasis; calcium/vitamin D.
- **[[calcineurin-inhibitors]]** — may be initiated/continued in pregnancy with careful monitoring only if no viable alternative.
- **[[ibd-preventive-care]]** — preconception vaccination checklist and the 28-day live-vaccine window before pregnancy.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Methotrexate preconception interval.** [[aga-2024-pregnancy-gi-liver]] advises stopping methotrexate **≥6 months before conception**; this consensus says **1–3 months** (Table 9) and **at least 1 month** (Table 5). Both are tier-1; **the newer publication wins** — 1–3 months, with the older interval noted.
- **Ozanimod preconception interval.** [[aga-2024-pregnancy-gi-liver]] advises **≥6 months**; this consensus says **at least 3 months** (Consensus statement 18). **Newer source wins.**
- **Live vaccines in the biologic-exposed infant.** Existing wiki text (from [[aga-2024-pregnancy-gi-liver]]) states **no live vaccines to the infant for 6 months** after in utero infliximab/adalimumab. This consensus splits the rule: **live rotavirus may be given** (GRADE 33), while **BCG is avoided for the first 6 months** (GRADE 34). **Newer source wins**; the blanket 6-month ban no longer holds for rotavirus.
- **Live vaccines while breastfeeding on infliximab.** The 2022 European Medicines Agency Direct Healthcare Professional Communication advises against them unless infant infliximab levels are undetectable; **the consensus explicitly declines to follow it** (Consensus statement 35).
- **Internal discrepancy on methotrexate grade.** Table 1 grades GRADE statement 15 *Strong*; the body text prints the same statement as *Conditional*.
- **Internal discrepancy on filgotinib.** Consensus statement 22 says discontinue filgotinib **at least 4 weeks** prior to conception; the body text says the manufacturer recommends **at least 1 week** for filgotinib (4 weeks for tofacitinib and upadacitinib). The statement is what the panel voted.
- **No randomized data exist.** Every GRADE statement rests on low or very low quality evidence; several *Strong* ratings were upgraded solely for potential catastrophic harm.
- **Figures.** The article is **not open access** ("All rights are reserved, including those for text and data mining, AI training, and similar technologies") — no Creative Commons licence line. Figures 1–3 (placental transfer of monoclonal antibodies and small molecules; passage into breast milk; relative infant dose) are therefore **not reproduced** in the wiki.
