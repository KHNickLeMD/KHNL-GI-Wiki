## Summary

Society guideline from the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) Standards of Practice Committee, replacing the 2014 ASGE guideline on the role of endoscopy in variceal hemorrhage. It covers the whole arc of variceal care in cirrhosis: who needs screening endoscopy at all, what to prescribe once clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH) is present, and how to manage the patient who arrives bleeding.

Five questions were answered with the full Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework — endoscopy-with-band-ligation versus nonselective beta-blocker (NSBB) in compensated advanced chronic liver disease (cACLD) with CSPH, antibiotic prophylaxis in cirrhosis with upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, short versus long vasoactive therapy, emergent versus urgent endoscopy, and prophylactic endotracheal intubation (PEI). Four further questions — Baveno VI screening, refractory esophageal variceal bleeding, the preferred endoscopic therapy for high-risk gastric varices, and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) versus retrograde transvenous obliteration (RTO) — were answered without full GRADE certainty assessment and are issued as recommendations or Best Practice Advice.

The direction of travel is consistently toward **less**: fewer screening endoscopies (noninvasive Baveno VI criteria), fewer days of vasoactive drug, a later endoscopy window (24 hours rather than 12), and no routine prophylactic intubation. The one area where the guideline asks for **more** technical investment is gastric varices, where EUS-guided injection with coils displaces direct endoscopic injection and pre-emptive radiologic therapy enters the secondary-prophylaxis conversation. Table 9 of the document tabulates all seven changes from the 2014 guideline; the comparison is reproduced below, because readers carry the old version.

Every statement in the document is conditional, and certainty of evidence is **low or very low throughout** — the only moderate-certainty finding anywhere in the document is a data row (variceal bleeding, all NSBB vs variceal band ligation), not a recommendation.

## Key Findings / Claims

### ⚠ Numbering and grading — read before citing

- **The document numbers none of its statements.** The numbering `R1`–`R7` and `BPA1`–`BPA3` used below and on the entity pages is **the wiki's own**, assigned in document order for cross-reference only. Do not cite these numbers as the guideline's.
- **The document grades every recommendation it issues as *Conditional*.** Certainty/quality is *Low* for four statements and *Very low* for six. No strong recommendation appears anywhere in the document.
- The guideline uses two labels — **Recommendation** (R1–R7) and **Best Practice Advice** (BPA1–BPA3). The three Best Practice Advice items carry a Conditional/Very-low pairing of their own; they are not ungraded.

### Definitions the guideline sets for itself

| Term | Definition as given |
|---|---|
| cACLD | Significant hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis **without current or prior clinical decompensation** |
| Decompensated cirrhosis | Development of clinically overt manifestations of portal hypertension — overt ascites, variceal hemorrhage, overt hepatic encephalopathy |
| CSPH | Hepatic venous pressure gradient **≥10 mm Hg**. Surrogate markers: presence of varices or portosystemic collaterals. **All decompensated cirrhosis has CSPH by definition** |
| High-risk **esophageal** varices | Moderate/large size, **or any size with high-risk bleeding stigmata** (e.g., red wale signs, cherry-red spots) |
| High-risk **gastric** varices | Actively bleeding, **or** previously bled, **or** high-risk bleeding stigmata, **or** **>10 mm** in size |
| Emergent upper endoscopy | Within **12 hours** of presentation to hospital |
| Urgent upper endoscopy | Within **24 hours** of presentation to hospital |
| PEI | Endotracheal intubation performed **only for the indication of protecting the airway before upper endoscopy** |

Background epidemiology given: ~40% of patients with cACLD develop varices annually; of patients with cACLD who develop varices, **5%–15% will decompensate and experience variceal bleeding**.

### R1 — Baveno VI criteria to screen for high-risk esophageal varices in cACLD
*Conditional recommendation / Low certainty of evidence.*

- Suggests **using the Baveno VI criteria** to screen for high-risk varices in cACLD.
- The suggestion **presumes** two things: access to transient elastography, and that the patient is an appropriate candidate for it.
- **Appropriate candidate = none of:** history of decompensated cirrhosis, variceal bleeding, treatment with NSBBs, hepatocellular carcinoma, splenectomy, liver transplant, or body mass index (BMI) **≥40 kg/m²**.
- **Operating characteristics** (meta-analysis of 30 studies, mostly retrospective): sensitivity **0.97** (95% CI, 0.95–0.98) — *Low* certainty, judged **Suggest Baveno VI**; specificity **0.32** (95% CI, 0.26–0.39) — *Low* certainty, judged **No Baveno VI**. High sensitivity is what carries the recommendation.
- **The operative rule:** liver stiffness measurement by transient elastography **<20 kPa AND platelets >150,000/µL** → screening endoscopy may be avoided. **Reassess annually; perform endoscopy when liver stiffness is ≥20 kPa or platelets are ≤150,000/µL.**
- **Do not apply to decompensated cirrhosis** — especially significant ascites or prior variceal bleeding — nor to patients with hepatocellular carcinoma or prior splenectomy. Not yet validated in pediatric or adolescent populations; those patients should undergo endoscopic surveillance as indicated.
- **BMI qualifier.** Transient elastography has limited diagnostic accuracy at high BMI; content experts suggested it performs well at **BMI <40 kg/m²**. At **BMI >40 kg/m²**, magnetic resonance (MR) elastography can assess liver stiffness — but **the Baveno VI criteria were not validated using MR elastography**, so the ASGE makes no recommendation on using it as a screening modality. Consult the multidisciplinary team for the higher-BMI patient.
- **Why not Baveno VII.** Baveno VII adds a splenic stiffness measurement (SSM) **≤40 kPa** by transient elastography to rule out high-risk varices in patients who are not NSBB candidates and who would otherwise need endoscopy under Baveno VI. Studies show Baveno VII results in fewer endoscopies, but one prospective study found the **"miss rate" of higher-risk varices was lower with Baveno VI than Baveno VII**, and the comparative studies had notable exclusion criteria. Given the lack of robust data and the limitations regarding SSM (splenectomy, small spleen volume/length, high BMI, portal vein thrombosis), **the panel limited its conditional recommendation to Baveno VI**.

### R2 — cACLD with CSPH: carvedilol, with surveillance endoscopy at least every 12 months
*Conditional recommendation / Low quality of evidence.*

- For cACLD **and** CSPH, suggests **prescribing carvedilol**, with **surveillance endoscopies performed at least every 12 months**.
- Where carvedilol or another NSBB cannot be given or tolerated: **upper endoscopy at least every 12 months, with variceal band ligation (VBL) as needed**.
- **Carvedilol dosing — the detail the pathway turns on.** Unlike other beta-blockers, which are titrated to a resting heart rate of **55–60 beats/min**, carvedilol is **typically started at 3.125 mg twice daily or 6.25 mg daily** and titrated upward as allowed by blood pressure, **keeping systolic blood pressure (SBP) >90 mm Hg and mean arterial pressure >65 mm Hg**. Dosing may be continued **daily or twice daily** as tolerated. **Maximum dose for this indication is 12.5 mg per day** — this dose can be increased further only if indicated for a nonhepatic reason.
- **NSBB contraindications:** hypotension (SBP <90 mm Hg), severe asthma, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, acute kidney injury, bradycardia (resting heart rate <55 beats/min). Patients may also be unable to tolerate dizziness and fatigue.
- **Evidence** (2 meta-analyses, summarized in Table 2):

| Outcome | Data | Effect measure | 95% CI | Certainty | Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All-cause mortality (all NSBB vs VBL) | 11 RCT | OR = 1.35 | 0.98–1.86 | Low | Neither |
| Variceal bleeding (all NSBB vs VBL) | 12 RCT | OR = 0.52 | 0.35–0.78 | **Moderate** | Favors VBL |
| Variceal bleeding (carvedilol only vs VBL) | 2 RCT | OR = 1.50 | 0.49–4.60 | Very low | Neither |
| Decompensation (carvedilol only vs VBL) | 4 RCT | SHR = 0.51 | 0.29–0.89 | Low | **Carvedilol** |
| Mortality (carvedilol only vs VBL) | 4 RCT | SHR = 0.42 | 0.20–0.90 | Low | **Carvedilol** |

  *OR, odds ratio; RCT, randomized controlled trial; SHR, subdistribution hazard ratio; VBL, variceal band ligation.*
- **The benefit is driven by the 2 newer RCTs**, which enrolled patients with CSPH who did **not** have high-risk varices (the 2 older RCTs enrolled patients who did). Critically, in both newer trials patients also underwent **surveillance endoscopy at 6- to 12-month intervals with VBL performed if high-risk varices were seen** — which is why the recommendation pairs carvedilol *with* continued surveillance rather than replacing it. **16%–20% of patients receiving carvedilol developed high-risk varices and required band ligation.**

### R3 — Antibiotic prophylaxis in cirrhosis presenting with upper GI bleeding
*Conditional recommendation / Very low certainty of evidence.*

- Suggests **prescribing antibiotics to prevent bacterial infection** for patients with cirrhosis presenting with an upper GI bleed.
- **Evidence** (meta-analysis published 2025; 15 RCTs, 1322 patients, 1995–2023; Table 3):

| Outcome | Data | Effect measure | 95% CI | Certainty | Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All-cause mortality | 12 RCT | RD = 2.1 | −1.9 to 6.6 | Low | Neither prophylaxis nor prophylaxis |
| Bacterial infection | 12 RCT | RD = 18.8 | 8.6–29.4 | Very low | **Prophylaxis** |
| Early recurrent bleeding | 9 RCT | RD = 4.0 | −4.2 to 12.3 | Very low | Neither prophylaxis nor prophylaxis |

  *RD, risk difference.*
- Mortality showed an **89.8% probability of noninferiority** between the two cohorts. A subanalysis split by era: RCTs performed **after 2004 → 98.9% probability of noninferiority**; **before 2004 → 72.4%** — i.e., the mortality benefit appears to be **decreasing over time**, and the most recent RCT (2023) demonstrated **no mortality benefit** for antibiotic prophylaxis.
- The recommendation rests on the **infection** reduction, not mortality: the panel judged the current evidence insufficient to materially alter the current standard of practice.
- ⚠ **Counterweight the panel asks clinicians to carry:** patients increasingly receive **third-generation cephalosporins** for prophylaxis, and these are associated with increasing rates of ***Clostridioides difficile*** infection. Weigh the possible decreased benefit against the risk of antibiotic-associated infection.

### R4 — Short-course (2–3 days) vasoactive therapy
*Conditional recommendation / Low certainty of evidence.*

- For cirrhosis presenting with **bleeding varices (or other bleeding related to portal hypertension)**, suggests that **short-term administration, defined as 2–3 days**, of vasoactive medications is sufficient to reduce the risk of variceal recurrent bleeding.
- **Start vasoactive medication as soon as variceal bleeding is suspected**; it may be stopped once the appropriate duration is completed. Agents studied: octreotide, terlipressin, somatostatin.
- **Evidence** (meta-analysis of 14 RCTs comparing short course, total duration <3 days after presentation, with long course, total 3–5 days; Table 4):

| Outcome | Data | Effect measure | 95% CI | Certainty | Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Recurrent bleeding at 5 d | 12 RCT | RR = 0.79 | 0.37–1.65 | Low | Favors neither |
| Recurrent bleeding at 30–42 d | 10 RCT | RR = 0.77 | 0.46–1.30 | **Moderate** | Favors neither |
| Mortality due to recurrent bleeding | 7 RCT | RR = 0.75 | 0.26–2.13 | Low | Favors neither |
| Overall mortality | 7 RCT | RR = 1.03 | 0.62–1.71 | Low | Favors neither |
| Length of stay | 4 RCT | MD = −1.12 | −1.42 to −0.30 | Low | **Favors short-term medication** |

  *MD, mean difference; RR, risk ratio.*
- ⚠ **Sclerotherapy exception.** In a subgroup analysis of patients treated with sclerotherapy, a **shorter** duration of vasoactive therapy was associated with **more recurrent bleeding and greater mortality**, attributed to an acute rise in portal pressure lasting up to 5 days after sclerotherapy. The authors of that analysis encouraged a **longer, 5-day** vasoactive duration in patients undergoing sclerotherapy.

### R5 — Endoscopy within 24 hours for suspected variceal hemorrhage
*Conditional recommendation / Very low certainty of evidence.*

- Suggests **endoscopy be performed within 24 hours** for patients with cirrhosis presenting with suspected variceal bleeding.
- **Additional decision-making regarding timing should be based on the patient's clinical presentation** — resuscitation status, hemodynamic status, and clinical suspicion for ongoing active bleeding.
- **Evidence** (meta-analysis of 9 observational studies; the 6 that specifically compared endoscopy within 12 hours vs not; Table 5):

| Outcome | Data | Effect measure | 95% CI | Certainty | Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overall mortality | 6 studies | RR = 0.72 | 0.38–1.38 | Very low | Neither |
| Recurrent bleeding | 4 studies | RR = 1.04 | 0.55–1.95 | Very low | Neither |
| Length of hospital stay (d) | 3 studies | MD = −0.28 | −1.26 to 0.7 | Very low | Neither |
| Hemostasis | 2 studies | RR = 0.56 | 0.27–1.15 | Very low | Neither |

- A single study comparing endoscopy within 24 hours vs after 24 hours found **fewer deaths** in the within-24-hour group (OR, 0.16; 95% CI, 0.02–1.36) — **not statistically significant**. In the absence of robust data arguing against it, the panel kept the 24-hour window.
- **Emergent endoscopy is still indicated** when the clinical picture demands it: changes in hemodynamic status, response to resuscitation, or persistence of active bleeding. The panel emphasized **not under-resuscitating a patient in order to perform an emergent endoscopy**, and **not delaying endoscopy in patients with hemorrhagic shock or ongoing active bleeding after initial resuscitation**.
- **Preprocedure resuscitation targets given by the document:**
  - **At least 2 large-bore intravenous catheters.**
  - Colloid or crystalloid fluids with a **restrictive transfusion target hemoglobin of 7–8** g/dL, in the absence of ischemic comorbidities, ongoing bleeding and/or hemodynamic instability — **overtransfusion may worsen portal hypertension–related bleeding**.
  - **Platelet counts >50 × 10⁹/L and fibrinogen >120 mg/dL were previously targeted but are no longer routinely recommended**; platelet and cryoprecipitate transfusion may be considered for correction on a case-by-case basis.
  - **Elevated prothrombin time and international normalized ratio do not reflect a coagulable state** in cirrhosis in patients not on antithrombotic agents; thromboelastography provides a more accurate assessment. Consider carefully whether plasma transfusion or vitamin K is truly required.

### R6 — Against prophylactic endotracheal intubation
*Conditional recommendation / Very low certainty of evidence.*

- For cirrhosis presenting with suspected variceal bleeding, **suggests against PEI**.
- **Additional decision-making regarding endotracheal intubation should be based on the patient's clinical presentation** — resuscitation status, mental status, hemodynamic status, and presence or absence of active bleeding.
- **Evidence** (Table 6):

| Outcome | Data | Effect measure | 95% CI | Certainty | Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overall mortality (varices only) | 2 studies | OR = 3.47 | 1.24–9.74 | Very low | **Favors no PEI** |
| Overall mortality (all upper GI bleeding) | 7 studies | OR = 1.92 | 0.71–5.23 | Very low | Neither |
| Aspiration (varices only) | 2 studies | OR = 4.90 | 0.53–39.91 | Very low | **Favors no PEI** |
| Aspiration (all upper GI bleeding) | 7 studies | OR = 3.85 | 1.46–10.15 | Very low | **Favors no PEI** |
| Length of hospital stay (varices only) | 2 studies | Standard MD = 0.22 | −.08 to 0.53 | Very low | Neither |
| Length of hospital stay (all upper GI bleeding) | 7 studies | Standard MD = 0.86 | 0.13–1.59 | Very low | **Favors no PEI** |

- ⚠ **Scope limit — this is about PEI only.** The recommendation does **not** touch other indications for endotracheal intubation: acute respiratory failure, inability to protect the airway, significantly impaired mental status, massive ongoing hematemesis. **Patients who cannot protect their airway because of ongoing emesis or mental status changes require intubation.**
- Endoscopists should discuss the potential need for intubation **case by case with anesthesia and critical care** before endoscopy.
- The studies evaluated primarily adult populations; the suggestion **may not apply to pediatric and adolescent patients**.

### R7 — Refractory esophageal variceal bleeding: esophageal stent **or** balloon tamponade as a bridge
*Conditional recommendation / Very low certainty of evidence.*

- Suggests that **either fully covered esophageal stents or balloon tamponade be used as a bridge** — the choice **based on institutional expertise** — until definitive therapy (e.g., transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt [TIPS]) can be performed.
- **20% of patients with bleeding esophageal varices are refractory to initial attempts at hemostasis.**
- **Evidence** — a single RCT across 9 hospitals in Spain, 28 patients (15 balloon tamponade, 13 esophageal stent), bridging to TIPS or endoscopic intervention, **median indwelling stent time 5 days**:

| Outcome | Esophageal stent | Balloon tamponade | *P* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Immediate hemostasis | 66% | 20% | .025 |
| Recurrent bleeding at 15 days | 15% | 53% | .037 |
| Device-related serious adverse events | 8% | 40% | .049 |
| 6-week mortality | 46% | 60% | .46 |

  - **Refractory bleeding** was defined in that trial as failure to control bleeding after pharmacologic and endoscopic therapy; **massive variceal bleeding** as persistent hemodynamic instability despite attempted volume resuscitation.
- ⚠ **The trial's stent is not available in the United States.** It was a specialized self-expandable nitinol SX-ELLA stent (**25-mm body diameter, 30-mm flare diameter, 135-mm length**), with a gastric balloon built into the delivery system allowing placement over a guidewire **without fluoroscopic or endoscopic placement**, approved for placement **up to 7 days**. This limits generalizability — which, with the noncomparative stent-vs-TIPS data, is why certainty was rated very low and why the panel suggested *either* technique rather than favoring stents.
- **Esophageal stent selection rules, when a stent is chosen:**
  - **Only fully covered self-expandable metal stents.**
  - **Largest available stent diameter**, to facilitate mechanical varix compression.
  - Place so that **the majority (at least two-thirds) of the stent is within the esophagus**.
  - **Do not place a stent in the setting of known or suspected gastric variceal bleeding.**
  - **Bridge only** — place as a bridge to more definitive therapy, then remove endoscopically.
  - **Ongoing bleeding despite stent placement → balloon tamponade placement and/or emergent TIPS.**
- Other concerns with stents: **inability to tamponade gastric varices**, stent migration, and esophageal ulceration.
- ⚠ **Institutional expertise is a real constraint**: esophageal stent placement is usually performed by endoscopists with advanced training, while variceal bleeding is frequently managed by physicians without it. Availability of fluoroscopy for deployment may also dictate which stents an endoscopist can use.

### BPA1 — High-risk gastric varices: EUS-guided injection over direct endoscopic injection
*Conditional recommendation / Very low certainty of evidence.*

- For high-risk gastric varices (**gastric varices >10 mm, or gastric varices that have previously bled**), suggests **EUS-guided injection (EUS-I) be performed over direct endoscopic injection (DEI)**.
- When EUS-guided interventions are performed, suggests **EUS-guided cyanoacrylate injection (EUS-CYA) plus coils be performed over EUS-CYA alone, when feasible**.
- **If EUS-guided variceal interventions are not available, DEI can be performed.**
- **Anatomy decides the technique, not preference.** Gastric varices are characterized by the **Sarin classification**. For **GOV1** varices (on the lesser curvature of the stomach extending from the gastroesophageal junction), **band ligation is an effective and accepted management strategy**. For **cardiofundal** varices — **IGV1** (isolated fundic) and **GOV2** (gastric fundal varices in continuity with esophageal varices) — **band ligation is not effective, because of vascular depth and anatomy**; management centers on injecting a thrombotic agent into the varix or its feeder vessel.
- **EUS-I vs DEI** (systematic review of 23 studies; >800 EUS, >3400 DEI patients; Table 7):

| Outcome | EUS-I (n = 851) | DEI (n = 3467) | Judgment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technical success | 0.94 (95% CI, 0.90–0.96) | 0.91 (95% CI, 0.83–0.96) | Neither |
| Obliteration of gastric varices | 0.84 (95% CI, 0.75–0.91) | 0.63 (95% CI, 0.43–0.79) | **Favors EUS** |
| Recurrence of gastric varices | 0.09 (95% CI, 0.05–0.16) | 0.18 (95% CI, 0.11–0.27) | Neither, leans EUS |
| Early recurrent bleeding | 0.07 (95% CI, 0.05–0.11) | 0.05 (95% CI, 0.03–0.07) | Neither |
| Late recurrent bleeding | 0.12 (95% CI, 0.09–0.15) | 0.17 (95% CI, 0.12–0.23) | Neither |

  Recurrence trended lower with EUS (9% vs 18%; *P* = .06) and obliteration was significantly higher (84% vs 63%; *P* = .02).
- **EUS-CYA + coils vs DEI** (subgroup analysis; Table 8): obliteration **0.88 vs 0.63** (*P* = .02, favors coil+glue); recurrence **0.05 vs 0.18** (*P* = .01, favors coil+glue); technical success 0.947 vs 0.91 (neither).
- **EUS-CYA + coils vs EUS-CYA alone** — RCT in Sarin **IGV1 and GOV2** varices, patients reassessed at **12 weeks** and retreated if necessary. Similar obliteration and adverse-event rates, but coils gave **significantly lower reintervention (20.8% vs 53.8%; *P* = .03)**, **longer time to reintervention (44 vs 25 weeks; *P* = .01)**, **longer survival (48 vs 42 weeks; *P* = .04)** and **significantly lower glue volume (1.5 vs 3.5 cm³; *P* < .001)**. Patients received **1 to 4 coils**.
- **Pre-intervention workup in the stable patient:** a **3-phase CT of the abdomen and pelvis** before intervention to assess for a **large splenorenal shunt** (which may represent a higher risk of systemic embolization), and a **transthoracic echocardiogram with bubble agitation study** to assess for a **patent foramen ovale**. Findings should be discussed with the multidisciplinary team — interventional radiology, hepatology, transplant surgery.
- **Coil sizing — the operative numbers.** Deploy coils **approximately 20% to 30% greater in diameter than the diameter of the varix**. Needle compatibility: a **22-gauge needle requires a 0.018-inch coil**; a **19-gauge needle is compatible with both 0.018-inch and 0.035-inch coils**.
- **Cyanoacrylate formulation changes the injection technique.** **n-butyl-2-CYA** polymerizes faster → inject **more rapidly** to decrease the risk of premature needle occlusion. **2-octyl CYA** polymerizes more slowly → lower risk of premature needle occlusion but **increased concern for embolization**; inject **at a slower rate**, and consider **placement after coils**. **Rapid needle flush with sterile water or 5% dextrose in water after glue injection is crucial** to deliver the glue in time and clear the needle. **Ethiodized oil (e.g., lipiodol)**, previously added to CYA to slow polymerization and allow fluoroscopic visualization of the injectate, **is now generally avoided because of embolization concerns**. Gelfoam and thrombin are emerging alternatives with insufficient comparative evidence.
- ⚠ **Embolization risk is real and under-recognized.** Although reported rates are low, endoscopists should be aware of **pulmonary embolization and systemic embolization** after EUS-CYA with or without coils — manifesting as pulmonary embolism, coronary embolism, or neurovascular thromboembolism. **Asymptomatic ectopic pulmonary embolism has also been reported.**
- ⚠ **Risk to the EUS scope and needle.** CYA can polymerize **within the working channel** of the EUS scope, and can **occlude the fine-needle aspiration needle channel or cause the needle to stick within the varix**. Review techniques to avoid scope damage and premature needle occlusion before beginning the procedure.
- **Cost:** true cost-effectiveness studies have not been performed, but one study suggests **EUS-CYA + coils may cost less than EUS-CYA alone**, attributed to fewer reinterventions, hospitalizations, and lower glue volumes.
- **Training is not standardized.** Physicians should ensure adequate training or proctoring before performing endoscopic therapies for gastric varices. **A lack of local expertise in EUS-I or DEI should not delay bleeding management** — unstable patients with ongoing gastric variceal bleeding may require nonendoscopic intervention (radiologic intervention or balloon tamponade) for initial hemostasis if endoscopic intervention is not available locally.

### BPA2 — EUS-guided injection over retrograde transvenous obliteration for initial management
*Conditional recommendation / Very low certainty of evidence.*

- For the **initial** management of high-risk gastric varices, suggests **EUS-I be considered over RTO**.
- **Mechanistic trade-off the guideline names:** EUS-I directly obliterates gastric varices, but varices **can re-form because of persistent portal hypertension**. **RTO, in contrast, may worsen ascites or esophageal varices** resulting from the increased portal pressure and flow it causes.
- **Evidence** — multicenter propensity-matched analysis of EUS-I (EUS-CYA and EUS-CYA + coils) vs RTO, covering acute bleeding, primary prophylaxis, and secondary prophylaxis:

| Outcome | EUS-I | RTO | *P* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Periprocedural adverse events | 5.1% | 22.0% | .007 |
| Worsening of ascites | 0% | 15.3% | .003 |
| Reintervention for gastric varices | 28.8% | 5.1% | .001 |
| Unplanned intervention for worsening esophageal varices | 1.7% | 16.9% | .008 |

  EUS-CYA + coils compared with plug-assisted RTO: less likely to develop ascites, **more** likely to require reintervention for gastric varices, and **less** likely to require reintervention for esophageal varices.
- The adverse-event advantage is what drives the consensus. A panel expert noted that performing EUS-I initially to **stabilize the patient** when feasible facilitates the multidisciplinary discussion of best long-term management.
- ⚠ **Availability caveat the panel raised against itself:** **RTO procedures may be more commonly available than EUS interventions for gastric varices.** Both are technically complex. Know what local expertise exists in each before offering either.

### BPA3 — Pre-emptive TIPS or other radiologic intervention after successful endoscopic treatment
*Conditional recommendation / Very low certainty of evidence.*

- For patients with cirrhosis who have had **successful initial endoscopic treatment of bleeding gastric varices**, suggests **multidisciplinary discussion to consider a pre-emptive TIPS or other radiologic intervention**.
- **Pre-emptive TIPS RCT** — patients with cirrhosis and gastric varices previously treated with DEI for active bleeding, randomized to continued standard endoscopic glue therapy with NSBBs vs pre-emptive TIPS. **Free from death or recurrent bleeding at 1 year: 77% (TIPS) vs 37% (standard glue therapy); *P* < .0001.** TIPS patients had **similar** rates of jaundice, ascites, infection, encephalopathy, and further decompensation.
- **DEI vs radiologic secondary prophylaxis RCT** — balloon-assisted retrograde transvenous obliteration (BRTO) where an amenable shunt was present, TIPS where it was not; intractable ascites and portal vein thrombosis excluded. **BRTO was favored over TIPS in patients with amenable shunts**, because a large spontaneous shunt would divert blood away from the TIPS (decreasing its effectiveness) and because gastric varices may bleed at lower pressures, making esophageal-variceal-and-TIPS-alone strategies inadequate. In all arms, patients underwent primary hemostasis by direct endoscopic injection followed by **endoscopic surveillance at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months**, with repeat glue injection if any soft component was felt during endoscopic probing. Results:

| Outcome | Radiologic | Endoscopy alone | *P* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gastric variceal recurrent bleeding | 2.2% | 24.4% | .04 |
| Variceal bleeding–related mortality | 2.2% | 17.8% | .03 |
| Hepatic encephalopathy (TIPS group) | 30% | 6.7% | .020 |
| Ascites (BRTO group) | 36% | 4.4% | .001 |

  The excess hepatic encephalopathy in the TIPS group **was able to be managed medically**; the excess ascites in the BRTO group **was diuretic responsive**. **26.7% of patients in the CYA-only arm required 2 sessions of glue treatment**, whereas only a single patient in the radiologic arm required an additional CYA treatment.
- **How to choose, per the panel:** patient-specific factors — presence of shunts and thromboses, Model for End-Stage Liver Disease with Sodium score, and history of hepatic encephalopathy or intractable ascites. **Shunt-related hepatic encephalopathy may benefit from RTO with shunt closure; poorly controlled ascites or large high-risk esophageal varices may benefit from TIPS placement with or without RTO.** One expert panelist noted that **RTO will ultimately cause the formation of collateral vessels elsewhere** in portal hypertension.
- ⚠ **Interpretation limit the panel flags on its own evidence:** in both trials, patients randomized to endoscopic therapy **did not necessarily receive EUS-guided therapy with coils and CYA**. Future studies including more optimal EUS therapies may show less disparate performance between radiologic and endoscopic arms.

### What changed from the 2014 ASGE guideline (Table 9)

| Question | ASGE 2014 | **ASGE 2026** |
|---|---|---|
| Screening for varices in cACLD | Endoscopy | **Baveno VI criteria** |
| Management of cACLD and CSPH | Endoscopic variceal ligation **or** NSBB | **Carvedilol and endoscopy every 6–12 mo** |
| Length of vasoactive therapy in variceal bleeding | 3–5 d | **2–3 d** |
| Timing of endoscopy for variceal hemorrhage | Within 12 h | **Within 24 h** |
| Aspiration risk management in suspected variceal bleeding | Periprocedural endotracheal intubation for **all** patients | **Periprocedural endotracheal intubation is not needed for all patients** |
| Refractory variceal bleeding | Balloon tamponade, then TIPS | **Balloon tamponade *or* esophageal stent, then TIPS** |
| Initial endoscopic management of gastric varices | Endoscopic injection of cyanoacrylate | **EUS-guided cyanoacrylate and coils** |

### Future directions the guideline names as unresolved

- Whether antibiotic prophylaxis is helpful for cirrhosis presenting with upper GI bleeding.
- The optimal timing of endoscopy in suspected variceal hemorrhage.
- The optimal EUS therapy for gastric varices.
- The optimal management of **primary** prophylaxis of gastric varices, and further refinement of risk stratification in that setting.
- Sustainability is cited as an explicit rationale: avoiding unnecessary VBL, prolonged antibiotic or vasoactive therapy, and routine prophylactic intubation reduces equipment waste, medication exposure, disposable waste, and associated CO₂ emissions.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **ASGE 2026 vs ASGE 2014 (same society, newer wins).** Seven direct reversals, tabulated above. The 12-hour endoscopy window, the 3–5-day vasoactive course, universal periprocedural intubation, and cyanoacrylate by direct injection are all superseded.
- **Baveno VI vs Baveno VII.** The guideline **declines to adopt Baveno VII** despite it being newer, citing a prospective study in which the miss rate for higher-risk varices was **lower with Baveno VI**, and the SSM limitations (splenectomy, small spleen volume/length, high BMI, portal vein thrombosis). Any wiki page carrying a Baveno VII splenic-stiffness rule should note that ASGE 2026 did not endorse it.
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis is weakening, not strengthening.** The era subanalysis (98.9% noninferiority after 2004 vs 72.4% before) and the 2023 RCT showing no mortality benefit sit uncomfortably beside the long-standing strong recommendations for prophylaxis in other guidelines. ASGE 2026 keeps the practice but on infection grounds only, and explicitly raises *C difficile* as a counterweight. This is a genuine softening against existing wiki content built on older sources.
- **R2 internal tension.** Table 2 judges **variceal bleeding** as *favoring VBL* at moderate certainty (OR 0.52) across all NSBBs, yet the recommendation is for carvedilol. The panel resolves this on decompensation and mortality (SHR 0.51 and 0.42, carvedilol-only RCTs) — but the carvedilol-only variceal-bleeding comparison is non-significant and very low certainty (OR 1.50; 95% CI, 0.49–4.60). The pairing with ongoing endoscopic surveillance is doing real work here and should not be dropped when the recommendation is quoted.
- **BPA1 vs BPA3 sequencing is not fully specified.** The document says EUS-I is preferred initially and that pre-emptive TIPS/radiologic intervention should be *considered* after successful endoscopic treatment, but does not give a threshold — number of sessions, varix size, or MELD-Na cut-point — at which the multidisciplinary conversation must happen. The guideline leaves this to patient-specific factors.
- **Stent availability is a US-specific gap.** The only RCT supporting esophageal stents used a device unavailable in the United States, and the guideline substitutes general rules (fully covered, largest diameter, two-thirds within the esophagus) for device-specific evidence.
- **No figure was captured for this page.** The document's Figure 1 (esophageal varices management strategy) and Figure 2 (high-risk gastric varices flowchart) are reproduced as Mermaid flowcharts on [[variceal-upper-gi-bleeding]] rather than as screenshots.
