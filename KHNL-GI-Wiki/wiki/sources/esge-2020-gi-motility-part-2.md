## Summary
Second of two ESGE guidelines on endoscopic therapy for gastrointestinal (GI) motility disorders; part 1 (separate publication) covers achalasia and gastroparesis. Part 2 covers **Zenker's diverticulum**, **gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)**, **intractable constipation** (percutaneous endoscopic cecostomy) and **Ogilvie's syndrome** (acute colonic pseudo-obstruction).

Method: 18-member committee, PICO (population, intervention, comparator, outcome) questions, systematic search (Medline, Embase, Cochrane). Strength and evidence quality by Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE). Consensus by modified Delphi on a five-point Likert scale; a statement passed if >75 % of members agreed (Likert 4–5); three voting rounds (§2 Methodology). Each recommendation prints its **level of agreement**.

Main messages: flexible endoscopic septotomy is first-line for symptomatic Zenker's of any size, with complete cricopharyngeal myotomy and routine carbon dioxide (CO₂); Zenker's peroral endoscopic myotomy (Z-POEM) is research-only. Endoscopic antireflux therapies are largely discouraged (transoral incisionless fundoplication (TIF), Medigus ultrasonic surgical endostapler (MUSE) and antireflux mucosectomy (ARMS) against; radiofrequency energy to the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) (Stretta) only in selected patients). Endoscopic cecostomy is a high-risk last resort after failed conservative care, with three-point cecal fixation and antibiotic cover. In Ogilvie's syndrome not improving with conservative care, endoscopic decompression is a valid option, urgent when the cecum is >12 cm and distension has lasted >4–6 days, followed by a decompression tube and oral polyethylene glycol (PEG).

The document does not number its recommendations; the numbers in the table below are the wiki's, in document order, with the section number as printed. It contains no tables or algorithm figures.

## Key Findings / Claims

27 recommendations (wiki numbering R1–R27). Grade line = the document's own wording, then level of agreement.

### Zenker's Diverticulum (§3)
- Background (§3): pulsion diverticulum through the Killian triangle (between thyropharyngeus and cricopharyngeal fibers of the inferior constrictor); prevalence 0.01 %–0.11 % (American population); most often men in their 7th–8th decades; dysphagia, regurgitation.

**R1 — Diagnosis (§3.1):** ESGE recommends barium swallow radiography with video fluoroscopy in the evaluation of patients with (suspected) Zenker's diverticulum.
- *Strong recommendation, very low quality of evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- Fluoroscopy gives pouch size plus "dynamic" information on regurgitation and aspiration — helps decide whether the diverticulum is the real cause of symptoms.

**R2 — Manometry (§3.1):** ESGE suggests against manometry as standard in the diagnostic work-up of Zenker's diverticulum.
- *Weak recommendation, very low quality of evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- In individual cases (e.g. small Zenker's), esophageal manometry can be used to rule out other causes of similar symptoms.

**R3 — First-line treatment (§3.2.1):** ESGE suggests flexible endoscopic treatment over open surgical treatment as first-line therapy for patients with a symptomatic Zenker's diverticulum of any size.
- *Weak recommendation, low quality of evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- Treatment is indicated for symptomatic Zenker's. Three options: open surgery (transcervical diverticulectomy, diverticulopexy with cricopharyngeal myotomy, or diverticular inversion); rigid endoscopy (stapling or CO₂ laser); flexible endoscopy. Common step = division of the cricopharyngeus muscle forming the septum.
- Single-incision technique: midline incision of the cricopharyngeus, option to clip the base. Double-incision: two incisions 1 cm apart, septum between resected with a snare, then base clipped.
- No prospective comparison with surgery. Retrospective (36 septotomy vs 31 diverticulectomy + myotomy): dysphagia recurrence 39 % vs 0 % (P = 0.001) at 2 months; complications 13 % vs 31 % (P = 0.08).
- Two meta-analyses (3079 and 596 patients): clinical success 82 %–87 % endoscopic vs 94 %–96 % open surgery; complications 7 %–9 % vs 11 %–15 %. Most "endoscopic" data here = rigid-endoscope laser/stapler by ear, nose and throat (ENT) surgeons; rigid endoscopy technical failure 6 %–7 % (not always possible in the elderly).
- Flexible septotomy meta-analysis (813 patients): pooled success 91 %, adverse events 11.3 %, recurrence 11 %.
- Rationale: surgery/rigid endoscopy need general anesthesia and are often abandoned for restricted neck mobility in the elderly; surgery has seemingly higher complications with comparable success.

**R4 — Recurrent Zenker's (§3.2.2):** ESGE suggests treatment by flexible endoscopy for recurrent Zenker's diverticulum.
- *Weak recommendation, low quality of evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- Predictors of symptom relapse within 48 months of endoscopic therapy: pretreatment size ≥50 mm, post-treatment size ≥10 mm, septotomy length ≤25 mm.
- Flexible septotomy for recurrence (25 recurrent vs 34 naïve): success 84 % vs 82 %, recurrence 24 % vs 15 %, complications 8 % vs 8.8 % (naïve vs recurrent). Needle-knife + diverticuloscope series (n = 134): recurrence 23.1 %; 78.3 % of the 23 retreated achieved remission after redo myotomy.
- Rigid stapler for recurrence (two series, 20 and 18 patients): short-term remission 81 %–90 %, complications 20 %–28 %, recurrence 5 %.

**R5 — Diverticuloscope (§3.3.1):** ESGE suggests it is left to the endoscopist's discretion whether or not to use a diverticuloscope when performing flexible endoscopic septotomy.
- *Weak recommendation, low quality of evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- One retrospective series: diverticuloscope-assisted success higher than cap-assisted; meta-analysis: no impact on success or complications.

**R6 — Minimum size (§3.3.2):** ESGE suggests symptomatic Zenker's diverticula of any size are amenable to flexible endoscopic treatment, although the usefulness of a diverticuloscope remains uncertain for Zenker's diverticula <2 cm.
- *Weak recommendation, very low quality of evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- Published series: mean size 20–50 mm; possible optimal efficacy for 30–50 mm. No minimum size for symptomatic diverticula.

**R7 — Emerging techniques (§3.3.3):** ESGE recommends that emerging treatments, such as Zenker's peroral endoscopic myotomy (Z-POEM) and tunneling, be considered experimental; offer them in a research setting only.
- *Strong recommendation, low quality of evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- Z-POEM, 75 patients, 10 centers: technical success 97 %, complications 6.7 % (1 bleed, 4 perforations, all conservative), clinical success 92 %, one recurrence at median 10 months. Evidence mostly case reports/series, no long-term follow-up, publication bias.

**R8 — CO₂ insufflation (§3.3.4):** ESGE recommends routine use of CO₂ in the endoscopic treatment of Zenker's diverticulum.
- *Strong recommendation, very low quality evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- Extrapolated from peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) for achalasia (CO₂ reduces subcutaneous emphysema). After cricopharyngeal division, only the buccopharyngeal fascia separates the field from the superior mediastinum; CO₂ reabsorbs faster than room air (less pneumomediastinum, subcutaneous emphysema).

**R9 — Prophylactic antibiotics (§3.3.5):** ESGE does not recommend routine administration of prophylactic antibiotics before or after endoscopic septotomy for Zenker's diverticulum.
- *Strong recommendation, low quality of evidence; agreement 92.9 %.*
- Meta-analysis: 7/20 studies used antibiotics; meta-regression — no reduction in complications.

**R10 — Extent of myotomy (§3.3.6):** ESGE recommends a complete myotomy of the cricopharyngeus muscle when performing endoscopic septotomy.
- *Strong recommendation, low quality of evidence; agreement 93.8 %.*
- Full transection advocated by all experts to prevent recurrence; direct evidence lacking; value of cutting deeper controversial.
- Costamagna et al.: failure at 6 months if septotomy <2.5 cm or pretreatment pouch >5 cm; at 48 months if septotomy <2.5 cm or post-procedure size >10 cm (as printed in §3.3.6; §3.2.2 gives the post-treatment predictor as ≥10 mm). Their septotomy-length measurement is debated (pouch size assumed equal to septum length).

**R11 — Clips at septotomy base (§3.3.7):** ESGE suggests the decision to deploy clips at the base of the septotomy should be dictated by endoscopist practice or clinical need (bleeding or suspected perforation).
- *Weak recommendation, low quality of evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- Meta-regression (six studies with clips): no impact on adverse events such as perforation.

**Ungraded technique notes (§3.3.8–3.3.9):**
- Needle-knife: cheap, available; downward cutting linked with complications.
- HookKnife (bent tip; hook 1.3 mm, arm 4.5 mm): hooks fibers upward before cutting, theoretically lowers perforation; complication 6.3 %, success 90.6 % (Repici); success 91.7 %, complications 8.4 %, recurrence 12.5 % (Rouquette).
- Stag Beetle knife Junior (SB knife Jr.): insulated scissor-type, cuts apex to base pulling fibers toward the scope, 360° rotation; median procedure 14 minutes, 83.9 % remission at median 7 months (n = 31); recurrence 9.6 % over 6 months, no perforation or mediastinitis (n = 52).
- Electrosurgical settings: obtain from the generator manufacturer.
- Post-procedure: monitor for complications; nil per os for 24 hours routinely; liquids the next day if uneventful. No evidence that a contrast study is needed before oral intake unless perforation is suspected.

### Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (§4)
- Background: GERD affects ~8.8 %–25.9 % of European adults; proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and fundoplication both proven effective; many earlier endoscopic therapies withdrawn for lack of efficacy or major side effects.

**R12 — TIF (§4.1):** ESGE recommends against the widespread clinical use of TIF as an alternative to PPI therapy or antireflux surgery in GERD — lack of long-term outcome data, inferiority to fundoplication, and modest efficacy in only highly selected patients. TIF may have a role for patients with mild GERD who are not willing to take PPIs or undergo antireflux surgery.
- *Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence; agreement 92.8 %.*
- TIF = endoscopic suturing device with T-fasteners creating a gastroplication. Five randomized controlled trials (RCTs), mostly moderate GERD; excluded hiatal hernia >2 cm, Los Angeles grade C or D esophagitis, Barrett's esophagus; 6-month follow-up vs sham, sham + PPI, or unblinded PPI.
- Meta-analysis: clinical response (≥50 % improvement in GERD health-related quality of life (GERD-HRQL) score, or remission of heartburn and regurgitation) 66 % TIF vs 30 % controls. Limited fall in esophageal acid exposure, similar to PPI.
- No long-term RCT data; uncontrolled studies: PPI cessation 70 % at 6 months → 34 % at 5 years. Severe adverse events (perforation, bleeding) 2.4 %. Prospective open comparison: Nissen fundoplication superior on objective and symptomatic measures.
- May offer symptomatic relief for PPI-intolerant or PPI-unwilling patients, or persistent regurgitation on PPIs with reluctance to have surgery.

**R13 — MUSE (§4.2):** ESGE recommends against MUSE in clinical practice because of insufficient data on effectiveness and safety in GERD; MUSE should be used in clinical trials only.
- *Strong recommendation, low quality evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- Device: flexible endoscope + ultrasonic range finder + surgical stapler (five 4.8-mm titanium staples).
- Prospective multicenter trial (66 patients): at 6 months GERD-HRQL improved >50 % off PPI in 73 % (95 % confidence interval 60 %–83 %); 42 (64.6 %) off daily PPI; 2 severe complications (empyema, hemorrhage). 4-year data in 37/66: modest decline but still better than baseline.
- Non-randomized MUSE (11) vs laparoscopic fundoplication (16; hiatal hernia >3 cm excluded for MUSE only): fundoplication more effective at 6 months; one esophageal perforation with MUSE. No sham-controlled or randomized MUSE-vs-fundoplication trials.

**R14 — Stretta (§4.3):** ESGE suggests Stretta can be considered in selective patients only, for symptom relief, and in the absence of erosive esophagitis and a hiatal hernia.
- *Weak recommendation, moderate level of evidence; agreement 92.9 %.*
- Radiofrequency via radially arranged needles at the esophagogastric junction (EGJ); thought to cause submucosal fibrosis, raising LES pressure and/or lowering compliance. Not available in some countries.
- Evidence: four RCTs (three vs sham, one vs PPI), 23 cohorts, two systematic reviews. RCT quality low; no convincing normalization of acid exposure or LES pressure; short-term symptom and quality-of-life improvement. RCT-only review: no difference vs sham or PPI in % time pH <4 over 24 h, LES pressure, PPI cessation, or quality of life. A meta-analysis including cohorts found benefit on objective and subjective end points except basal LES pressure.

**R15 — ARMS (§4.4):** ESGE recommends against ARMS in routine clinical practice for GERD because of lack of data and potential complications.
- *Strong recommendation, low quality evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- ARMS = endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) at the cardia over 180–270° of the circumference; scarring narrows the EGJ and changes the angle of His.
- Three case series, 39 PPI-refractory patients (no sliding hernia or hernia ≤2 cm): clinical response 69 %–80 %, dysphagia 13 %; no controlled data.

### Intractable Constipation — Percutaneous Endoscopic Cecostomy (§5)
- Background: cecostomy technique comparable to percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy, but the tube goes directly into the colon; higher complication risk and a significant mortality; data rare and retrospective. Main indications: relief of colonic obstruction or antegrade irrigation for colonic motility disorders.

**R16 — Indication (§5.1):** ESGE recommends endoscopic cecostomy only after conservative management with medical therapies or retrograde lavage has failed.
- *Strong recommendation, low quality evidence; agreement 93.3 %.*
- High-risk procedure with significant morbidity and even mortality → intractable cases only.

**Periprocedural management (§5.2, ungraded):** CO₂ insufflation; left lateral or supine; aseptic puncture. Rigorous bowel preparation mandatory (fecal remnants raise septic risk); some expert centers use 7 days of fiber-free diet + 3 days of polyethylene glycol (PEG) solution.

**R17 — Antibiotic prophylaxis (§5.2.2):** ESGE recommends antibiotic prophylaxis starting before and continuing for 3 days after the procedure.
- *Strong recommendation, very low quality evidence; agreement 93.3 %.*
- Follow local protocols; could be amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (1 g) or ofloxacin + metronidazole (500 mg) 1 hour before, mostly maintained until 72 hours after cecostomy.

**R18 — Cecal fixation (§5.3):** ESGE recommends fixing the cecum to the abdominal wall at three points (T-anchors, a double-needle suturing device, or laparoscopic fixation) to prevent leaks and infectious adverse events, whatever percutaneous endoscopic cecostomy method is used.
- *Strong recommendation, very low quality evidence; agreement 86.7 %.*
- Three techniques (no evidence favoring one): pull-through, introducer, laparoscopically assisted percutaneous endoscopic cecostomy (LAPEC). Steps: good cleansing, sedation, skin disinfection, transillumination, cecal fixation. Metal anchors removed within 3–4 weeks.
- Pull-through (§5.3.1): colonoscopy, maximal transillumination point infiltrated with local anesthetic, cecum fixed at three points; 18 G Seldinger needle (children 12 G) through the center of the sutured triangle; guidewire snared and withdrawn from the anus; tube 14–20 Fr (adults; 12 Fr children) pulled through; internal bolster checked endoscopically, external bolster applied.
- Introducer (§5.3.2): Seldinger introducer into the cecum, then definitive catheter placed and fixed; Chait Trapdoor ("multiple pigtails") or balloon catheters (11–15 Fr). Chait catheters: no balloon rupture/leak, no buried bumper, less granulation, easily exchanged.
- LAPEC (§5.3.3): colonoscopy identifies cecum; fixation and tube placement laparoscopically or under laparoscopic control; one camera port, often two extra ports.

**R19 — Choice of technique (§5.4; one box, two statements):** ESGE suggests the endoscopic route in critically ill patients in whom cecostomy is considered. ESGE suggests LAPEC as the preferred technique for patients whose clinical condition is good.
- *Weak recommendation, very low quality evidence; agreement 86.7 %.*
- Purely endoscopic cecostomy: technical success >80 %, complications 30 %–40 % (mostly minor; deaths from fecal peritonitis reported); acceptance reduced in ~25 %, mostly from pain. LAPEC success 95 %. No direct comparison.

**R20 — Other colonic sites (§5.5):** ESGE recommends performing endoscopic colostomy at no locations other than the cecum unless this is technically not feasible.
- *Strong recommendation, very low quality evidence; agreement 93.3 %.*
- Left-sided (descending/sigmoid) colostomy feasible but no advantage; very high serious adverse event rates in some studies. One series of 31 left-sided colostomies: 13 of 14 constipation patients needed tube removal; two deaths from fecal peritonitis.

### Ogilvie's Syndrome — Acute Colonic Pseudo-obstruction (§6)
- Definition: pathologic colonic dilatation without mechanical obstruction; mainly in patients with serious comorbidities.
- Initial evaluation (ungraded): exclude mechanical large-bowel obstruction with abdominal computed tomography (CT) or water-soluble contrast enema; complete blood count, electrolytes, renal and thyroid function to find correctable factors (electrolyte imbalance, renal insufficiency, infection, hypothyroidism).
- Conservative therapy first: stop narcotics, anticholinergics and calcium-channel antagonists; correct electrolytes; nil per os; nasogastric and/or rectal tube decompression; frequent position changes (individual effects unstudied).

**R21 — Endoscopic decompression vs neostigmine (§6.1.1):** ESGE recommends considering endoscopic decompression of the colon in patients with Ogilvie's syndrome that is not improving with conservative treatment.
- *Strong recommendation, low quality evidence; agreement 93.8 %.*
- Endoscopic decompression and intravenous neostigmine are both valid options; no recommendation on superiority of either (no prospective head-to-head trials). Choice depends on local expertise and access to urgent colonoscopy.
- Endoscopic decompression success 36 %–88 % (retrospective). Vs neostigmine: total response 82 % vs 49 % (P <0.001); no further therapy after one or two interventions 75.0 % vs 35.5 % (P <0.001) and 84.6 % vs 55.6 % (P = 0.003). Neostigmine efficacy in earlier studies 61 %–100 %.
- Perforation with endoscopic decompression 0–5 %.

**R22 — Prompt decompression criteria (§6.1.2):** ESGE recommends prompt endoscopic decompression if the cecal diameter is >12 cm **and** the Ogilvie's syndrome has lasted longer than 4–6 days.
- *Strong recommendation, low quality evidence; agreement 87.5 %.*
- Colonic ischemia and/or perforation → no longer eligible for endoscopic management; refer for surgery.
- Risk data (§6.1.2, one retrospective study):

| Cecal diameter | Perforation and/or ischemia |
|---|---|
| <12 cm (n = 44) | 0 % |
| 12–14 cm (n = 29) | 7 % |
| >14 cm (n = 69) | 23 % |

- Same study: cecal diameter >14 cm → two-fold mortality; mortality by delay to decompression <4 days 15 %, 4–7 days 27 %, >7 days 73 %. Another study: perforation risk related more to duration of distension (>6 days) than absolute cecal size.

**R23 — Recurrence (§6.1.3):** ESGE recommends considering repeated endoscopic decompression for recurrence of Ogilvie's syndrome.
- *Strong recommendation, low quality evidence; agreement 92.9 %.*
- Recurrence after initial success: 0–38 % after neostigmine, 0–50 % after endoscopic decompression. Sustained success single vs multiple decompressions 95 % vs 56 % (P <0.05); repeat colonoscopic decompression 87 % success but recurrence 40 % vs 22 % after initial colonoscopy. Choice vs neostigmine again depends on local expertise and urgent colonoscopy access.

**R24 — Decompression tube (§6.2.1):** ESGE recommends placing a decompression tube in the right or transverse colon after endoscopic decompression, as this seems associated with lower recurrence rates.
- *Strong recommendation, low quality evidence; agreement 92.9 %.*
- Sustained decompression 80 % with tube vs 25 % without (n = 50); right vs transverse 90 % vs 83 % (P >0.05); lower with tube tip at hepatic flexure (63 %), splenic flexure (75 %), descending colon (0 %). Old, small retrospective studies.
- Practice (no literature on duration): tube kept 1–3 days; low (intermittent) suction; flush every 2–4 hours with 20–30 mL normal saline.

**R25 — Bowel preparation (§6.2.2):** ESGE recommends against oral bowel preparation solutions before colonic decompression, as these may worsen colonic dilatation.
- *Strong recommendation, very low quality evidence; agreement 93.8 %.*
- Usefulness of enemas before decompression never investigated.

**R26 — Post-procedure oral PEG (§6.2.3):** ESGE recommends oral PEG solution in Ogilvie's syndrome after initial resolution of colonic dilatation, as it decreases the risk of recurrence.
- *Strong recommendation, low level of evidence; agreement 100 %.*
- Randomized placebo-controlled trial (30 patients responding to neostigmine or colonoscopic decompression): relapse 0 % vs 33 % (P = 0.04). Other laxatives acting on colonic motility seem reasonable; no supporting studies. Dose not stated.

**R27 — Percutaneous endoscopic colostomy (§6.3):** ESGE recommends considering percutaneous endoscopic colostomy/cecostomy for Ogilvie's syndrome refractory to pharmacologic and endoscopic treatment, especially in patients not amenable to surgery because of increased perioperative risk.
- *Strong recommendation, very low quality evidence; agreement 80.0 %.*
- Small series: symptom resolution 74 % (33 patients, 4 with recurrent Ogilvie's — 3 of 4 resolved; 3 fecal peritonitis, 1 death); 7 of 8 cecostomies successful; 81 % improvement in 31 patients (1 death from fecal peritonitis among 5 with acute-on-chronic pseudo-obstruction; 3 tubes removed for infection).
- Advantages over surgery: avoids general anesthesia; reversible after improvement.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **TIF — ESGE more restrictive than US guidelines.** ESGE 2020 recommends *against* widespread use (strong, moderate), allowing a role only in mild GERD with patients unwilling to take PPIs or have surgery. [[acg-2021-gerd]]: "We suggest consideration of TIF for patients with troublesome regurgitation or heartburn who do not wish to undergo antireflux surgery and who do not have severe reflux esophagitis (LA [Los Angeles] grade C or D) or hiatal hernias >2 cm" (Conditional, Low). American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) [[asge-2024-gerd]]: "Suggest TIF evaluation for confirmed GERD with hiatal hernia ≤2 cm and Hill grade I or II…" (Conditional, low) and combined TIF + hiatal hernia repair (cTIF) for hernia >2 cm and Hill grade III or IV. US guidelines are newer (2021, 2025) and win within tier 1.
- **Stretta — ESGE slightly more permissive.** ESGE: may be considered in selected patients without erosive esophagitis or hiatal hernia (R14 above). [[acg-2021-gerd]]: "we cannot recommend its use as an alternative to medical or surgical antireflux therapies" (Conditional, Low). [[asge-2024-gerd]]: best practice advice only — "Stretta can be considered for confirmed GERD with hiatal hernia <2 cm and Hill grade I or II when TIF and surgical fundoplication are not available or feasible." Note ESGE requires *no* hiatal hernia; ASGE allows <2 cm.
- **MUSE and ARMS:** ESGE recommends against both (R13, R15). [[asge-2024-gerd]] makes **no recommendation in favor of or against** ARMS and the other novel endoscopic antireflux therapies (insufficient controlled data); for MUSE it states use over TIF 2.0 is not recommended.
- **Ogilvie's — first-line after failed conservative care.** ESGE: endoscopic decompression and neostigmine both valid, no superiority recommendation. ASGE [[asge-2020-acpo-volvulus]]: neostigmine 2 mg over 3–5 minutes recommended (Rec 6), with decompression + tube suggested "as an alternative" (Rec 9); conservative care up to 72 h. **Cecum >12 cm — endoscopy vs surgery:** ESGE recommends **prompt endoscopic decompression** when the cecum is >12 cm *and* the syndrome has lasted >4–6 days (R22); ASGE refers patients with **cecal diameter >12 cm** (or peritonitis, ischemia, perforation, clinical deterioration) **for surgery**, and links ischemia/perforation risk to cecal diameter >10–12 cm and distension >6 days. Same publication year (2020); both shown. Both support decompression-tube placement and PEG to reduce recurrence (ASGE: daily PEG via nasogastric tube).
- **Cecostomy:** [[asge-2014-constipation]] recommends colonoscopy to allow creation of percutaneous cecostomy "when clinically appropriate and feasible" with largely pediatric evidence; ESGE 2020 gives adult selection (after failed medical therapy/retrograde lavage), three-point fixation and antibiotic rules.
- **Internal inconsistency (Zenker's recurrence predictor):** §3.2.2 states post-treatment size ≥10 mm; §3.3.6 states post-procedure size >10 cm for the same study. The 10 mm figure in §3.2.2 is the one listed as a relapse predictor.
- Part 1 of this guideline (achalasia, gastroparesis) is a separate publication and not covered here.
