## Summary

The Multisociety Sedation Curriculum for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (MSCGE) is a **training curriculum, not a clinical practice guideline**. It was built as an outgrowth of the Gastroenterology Core Curriculum to give fellowship programs a competency-based framework for teaching procedural sedation, and is explicitly described by its authors as a "living document" representing the sponsoring societies' vision of best practice based on published data and expert consensus.

Its structure is uniform and is the key to reading it: an overview of training plus **11 sections**, each organized as *Importance* → *Goals of Training* → *Training Process* → *Assessment of Competence*. The 11 sections are sedation pharmacology, informed consent, periprocedure assessment, levels of sedation, training in administration of specific agents for moderate sedation, airway/rescue techniques and management of complications, anesthesiologist assistance, intraprocedure monitoring, postprocedure assessment, endoscopy in pregnant and lactating women, and assessment of competency. An appendix provides a primer in sedation pharmacology.

Because it is a curriculum, its clinical value to this wiki sits almost entirely in its **13 numbered tables and one figure**, which are the operative reference content: the ASA physical status scale, two sedation-depth scales, a drug table with onset/peak/duration/initial dose/antagonist for 14 agents, the indications for anesthesiology assistance, two recovery/discharge scoring systems, the pregnancy and lactation tables, and the modified Mallampati classification.

The document also records the regulatory framing that was current in 2012 and that still shapes staffing: CMS treats **moderate sedation as outside the scope of anesthesia services**, and therefore exempt from the facility requirements that apply when anesthesia is provided, while **deep sedation is viewed by CMS as a form of anesthesia** (monitored anesthesia care) and is subject to the statutory requirements applying to anesthesia services generally — including administration only by individuals qualified under statute §482.52(a), and documented preanesthesia and postanesthesia evaluations.

## Key Findings / Claims

### No graded recommendations — numbering below is the wiki's where noted
This document **issues no numbered recommendations and assigns no evidence grades or strength-of-recommendation labels anywhere**. It has no GRADE table, no strong/conditional vocabulary, and no Class/Level scheme. Its numbered lists are *training objectives*, and its tables are numbered by the document itself (Tables 1–13, Figure 1). Where this page cites a table or figure number it is the document's own; nothing on this page is a graded recommendation, because the source contains none.

- The curriculum teaches moderate sedation in **ASA classes 1 through 3**.
- **ASA class 4 or higher is associated with an increased risk of cardiopulmonary complications** and appears in Table 4 as an indication for anesthesiology assistance.

### Table 4 — Indications for anesthesiology assistance during GI endoscopy (document's own table)
- Prolonged or therapeutic endoscopic procedures requiring deep sedation and/or general anesthesia
- Anticipated intolerance, paradoxical reaction, or allergy to standard sedation regimens
- Increased risk of complications because of severe comorbidity — **ASA class 4 and higher**
- Increased risk of airway obstruction
- History of stridor · history of severe sleep apnea
- Dysmorphic facial features — trisomy 21, Pierre-Robin syndrome
- **Oral abnormalities** — oral opening **<3 cm in adults**, protruding incisors, macroglossia, high arched palate, tonsillar hypertrophy, **Mallampati score of 4**
- **Neck abnormalities** — decreased hyoid-mental distance (**<3 cm in adults**), short thick neck, limited neck extension, cervical spine disease (eg, advanced rheumatoid arthritis) or trauma, severe tracheal deviation
- **Jaw abnormalities** — retrognathia, micrognathia, trismus, severe malocclusion

### Fasting intervals before sedation
- **2 hours after clear liquid intake** and **6 hours after a light meal** before administration of moderate sedation or anesthesiologist-directed sedation.
- **These intervals should be lengthened in the setting of gastric-emptying abnormalities.**

### Levels of sedation — the continuum
- The Joint Commission's **4 levels**: minimal sedation (anxiolysis), moderate sedation, deep sedation, general anesthesia. They stretch along a continuum **without clear boundaries**, are defined by response to verbal/light tactile/painful stimuli, and have **no physiologic data supporting the definitions**.
- Minimal = retains ability to respond voluntarily to vocal commands and maintains a patent airway with protective reflexes. Moderate = tolerates unpleasant procedures while maintaining adequate cardiorespiratory function, protective airway reflexes, and ability to react to verbal or tactile stimulation. Deep = medically controlled depressed consciousness, not easily aroused, can respond purposefully to painful stimulation. General anesthesia = unarousable even to painful stimuli.
- Operative teaching point: **all providers should be prepared to rescue patients from a level of sedation deeper than the one targeted**, because targeting a minimal level by definition creates the potential for patients to become deeply sedated.
- **Transient deep sedation at some time during endoscopy is a frequent outcome of conventional benzodiazepine–opioid sedation even when moderate sedation is specifically targeted and titrated.** Unintended deep sedation is distinguished from *planned* targeting of deep sedation, which triggers the CMS anesthesia requirements above.

### Propofol — what the curriculum actually states
- Propofol used in combination with other agents is a valuable option for moderate sedation, but **deep sedation generally results when it is administered as a single agent** for endoscopic sedation.
- Titrating propofol to moderate sedation after low presedation doses of an opioid, a benzodiazepine, or both is **balanced propofol sedation**, a form of nonanesthesiologist-administered propofol sedation.
- Endoscopists who do not personally administer or direct propofol **must still be prepared to decide when propofol-mediated sedation by an anesthesia provider is appropriate**, and must be skilled in recognizing **delayed propofol-related adverse events arising after recovery — fever, chills, or myalgia within 48 hours of administration**.
- In many states a certified registered nurse anesthetist must be supervised by the physician endoscopist if not otherwise supervised by an anesthesiologist.
- SEDASYS (computer-assisted personalized sedation) is described as **experimental at the time of writing**, with "approvable" status from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA).

### Monitoring
- Minimal monitoring for moderate sedation and analgesia: **periodic assessment of blood pressure** and **continuous assessment of cardiac rhythm and rate, ventilation, oxygenation, level of consciousness, and pain**.
- The curriculum quotes the ASA House of Delegates: during moderate or deep sedation the adequacy of ventilation shall be evaluated by continual observation of qualitative clinical signs **and monitoring for the presence of exhaled carbon dioxide** unless precluded or invalidated by the nature of the patient, procedure, or equipment.
- **Capnography — the document's own scope limit:** the only evidence suggesting benefit is in **adults undergoing prolonged procedures such as endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)**, and in the **pediatric** population undergoing upper endoscopy and colonoscopy. **There are no data showing a benefit in adults undergoing upper endoscopy or colonoscopy**, and whether it becomes a standard requirement is stated to be undetermined.
- Pulse oximetry is a standard of care but **may not adequately reflect hypoventilation, apnea, impending hemodynamic instability, or vasoconstrictive shock** — patients may be well saturated on oxygen while retaining significant carbon dioxide.
- **Bispectral index (BIS) monitoring: no data support its role** in procedural sedation for GI endoscopy.
- Monitoring and assessment must be documented at **regular intervals throughout** the procedure, and the sedation scale used must be identified and documented.

- Postprocedure training must cover the indications, contraindications, dosing and side effects of flumazenil and naloxone, and **the risk of resedation**.

### Airway, rescue and complications
- The most common serious and life-threatening sedation-related complications are **respiratory**; the most serious is **aspiration**, whose consequences may be impossible to correct once substantial aspiration has occurred.
- Most common respiratory events relate to **hypoventilation induced by sedation agents**; most cardiopulmonary events during GI endoscopy stem from hypoventilation cascading into hypoxia and cardiac decompensation.
- Conditions associated with increased aspiration risk, named by the document: **active upper GI hemorrhage, achalasia, bowel obstruction with gastric distention, and delayed gastric emptying**.
- Trainees are to complete **Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) certification** (or an equivalent with hands-on airway training) and hold a valid certificate, and to practice head-tilt, chin-lift and jaw-thrust maneuvers, nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal airway placement, bag-mask ventilation and laryngeal mask airway on models.
- Pharmacologic agents to be trained in beyond ACLS: naloxone, flumazenil, **atropine or glycopyrrolate or vagolytic agents for bradycardia**, position change and fluid bolus for hypotension, **ephedrine** for severe hypotension, and **β-blockers** for severe hypertension.
- A **time out per the Joint Commission's Universal Protocol** is required before administration of anesthesia, including at minimum the procedure team's agreement as to patient identity and type of procedure.

### Informed consent
- Most state laws specify that obtaining informed consent is a **nondelegatable duty** — it must be performed by the physician and cannot be relegated to staff or the endoscopy nurse.
- Consent explicitly **covers the sedation as well as the procedure**, and the alternatives discussed run from **no sedation to anesthesiologist-provided general anesthesia**.
- Consent may be **withdrawn**; an ineffectively sedated patient has the right to demand that the procedure stop even though partially sedated. Risk factors for ineffective sedation that may prompt withdrawal are named: chronic narcotic and/or anxiolytic use, and medical conditions that may preclude effective sedation such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, morbid obesity, advanced cardiomyopathy, and severe obstructive sleep apnea.
- Caution the document attaches: a request to stop must be carefully evaluated (eg, whether the patient is speaking in full coherent sentences or mumbling incomprehensibly), because many such requests are an artifact of sedation causing misperception rather than true withdrawal of consent.

### Figure 1 and Table 13
- **Figure 1** is the **modified Mallampati classification**, illustrated as four line drawings (Grade I–IV). The document's legend: class 1, full visibility of tonsils, uvula and soft palate; class 2, visibility of hard and soft palate, upper portion of tonsils, and uvula; class 3, soft and hard palate and base of the uvula are visible; class 4, only hard palate is visible.
- The document notes that in patients receiving **anesthesia-assisted** sedation an increased Mallampati score is a risk factor for the need for anesthesia-directed airway manipulation, and that **there are no similar data for endoscopist-directed sedation targeting moderate sedation**.
- **Table 13** maps competencies (medical knowledge, practical competencies, interpersonal and communication skills, patient care, professionalism, practice-based learning, systems-based practice) to assessment tools, following the American Board of Internal Medicine competency framework.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Superseded in large part by [[asge-2018-sedation]]** (ASGE, *Anesthesia and Sedation in Endoscopy*), which is the newer tier-1 source and wins on the wiki wherever the two differ. This 2012 document should be read as the multisociety **training** framework and as the home of the scales and tables above, not as current sedation practice.
- **The FDA pregnancy letter categories in Tables 10 and 11 no longer exist as a labeling system.** The categories are reproduced here because the document prints them; they should not be used as current labeling. Where this wiki needs a current pregnancy position, it comes from [[aga-2024-pregnancy-gi-liver]].
- **SEDASYS is described as experimental** and is of historical interest only.
- **Internal inconsistency in Table 3:** midazolam's peak effect is printed as "3–3" minutes, which is not a range; the document gives no correction.
- **No evidence grading anywhere**, so none of this content carries a strength or quality rating, and no claim here can be cited as a graded recommendation. Its clinical statements rest on the sponsoring societies' expert consensus.
- The document states plainly that the four sedation levels have **no physiologic data to support the definitions** and that there is **no boundary** between them — a limitation it does not resolve.
- The modified Mallampati classification is a grading made by looking, and the document's four-panel drawing is its only illustration; the class definitions above are the document's own legend text.
