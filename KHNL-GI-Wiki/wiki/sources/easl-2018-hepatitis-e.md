## Summary

European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) clinical practice guideline on hepatitis E virus (HEV) infection. Its premise is a paradigm shift: HEV was once thought to be an imported infection of travellers returning from Asia or Africa, but is now known to be **endemic in most high-income countries and largely zoonotic**, and locally acquired HEV is the commonest cause of acute viral hepatitis in many European countries. The guideline is therefore deliberately focused on **HEV genotype (gt) 3 (and 4)**; management of gt 1/2 outbreaks in resource-limited settings is deferred to the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines.

The document separates the two clinical pictures. **Acute HEV** (gt 3) is clinically silent in the vast majority; probably fewer than 5% develop symptomatic hepatitis, disproportionately older males, and progression to acute liver failure is rare; acute hepatitis E is a concern in pre-existing chronic liver disease, where some cases of acute-on-chronic liver failure are caused by HEV. **Chronic HEV** occurs only in the immunosuppressed (solid organ transplant [SOT] recipients, haematological disease, human immunodeficiency virus [HIV] with low CD4 count, heavy immunosuppression for rheumatic disease), is defined by persistent HEV RNA, and can progress rapidly to cirrhosis, decompensation and death.

Diagnosis is reframed around two rules: **test everyone with hepatitis, irrespective of travel history**, as part of first-line virological investigation; and **in the immunosuppressed, serology is unreliable — use nucleic acid amplification testing (NAT)**, because anti-HEV immunoglobulin (Ig) M and IgG are often undetectable in chronically infected patients. Serology plus NAT in combination is the recommended approach in the immunocompetent. Extrahepatic disease — above all **neurological injury** (neuralgic amyotrophy, Guillain-Barré syndrome, encephalitis/myelitis) and **renal injury** (membranoproliferative and membranous glomerulonephritis, with or without cryoglobulinaemia) — generates its own testing recommendations, including testing irrespective of liver test results.

Treatment is pragmatic. Acute HEV usually needs no antiviral therapy; ribavirin may be considered for severe acute hepatitis E or acute-on-chronic liver failure. For chronic HEV in SOT recipients the first manoeuvre is **reduction of immunosuppression**, which clears HEV in about 30%; those still replicating get **ribavirin monotherapy for 12 weeks**, with HEV RNA assessed in **serum and stool** at the end of therapy and extension to six months if either remains positive. PEGylated-interferon-α is reserved for liver transplant non-responders (contraindicated in kidney, pancreas and heart/lung grafts because of rejection risk). Prevention rests on food hygiene for the vulnerable and on blood-donor NAT screening informed by local risk assessment; the licensed vaccine (China, 2011) is not available elsewhere.

## Recommendations and Statements

**Grading (the guideline's own system, Table 1 — Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation [GRADE]):** level of evidence **A** (meta-analyses/systematic reviews or multiple high-quality randomised controlled trials), **B** (single randomised controlled trial or multiple non-randomised studies), **C** (small studies, retrospective observational studies, registries). Grade of recommendation **1 = strong** ("must", "should", "EASL recommends"), **2 = weak** ("can", "may", "EASL suggests"). Statements carry a level of evidence only.

**The guideline does not number its recommendations — the numbering below is the wiki's**, following the order of the document.

**Epidemiology**
1. Travellers with hepatitis returning from areas endemic for HEV gt 1 or 2 should be tested for HEV. **(A1)**
2. Pregnant women with HEV gt 1 or 2 should be cared for in a high-dependency setting, and transferred to a liver transplant unit if liver failure occurs. **(A1)**

**Acute infection**
3. All patients with symptoms consistent with acute hepatitis should be tested for hepatitis E. **(A1)**
4. EASL suggests testing for hepatitis E in patients with unexplained flares of chronic liver disease. **(C2)**

**Chronic infection**
5. EASL recommends HEV testing in all immunosuppressed patients with unexplained abnormal liver function tests. **(A1)**

**Extrahepatic manifestations**
6. EASL recommends HEV testing, irrespective of liver function test results, in patients presenting with neuralgic amyotrophy **(B1)** and Guillain-Barré syndrome **(B1)**, and suggests HEV testing for patients with encephalitis/myelitis **(C2)**.
7. EASL suggests testing patients with HEV infection for proteinuria. **(C2)**
8. Patients with acute or chronic HEV infection who develop new onset proteinuria may be considered for a renal biopsy. **(C2)**
9. EASL suggests antiviral treatment for patients with chronic HEV infection and associated glomerular disease. **(C2)**

**Laboratory diagnosis**
10. EASL recommends using a combination of serology and nucleic acid amplification testing (NAT) to diagnose HEV infection. **(A1)**
11. EASL recommends NAT testing to diagnose chronic HEV infection. **(A1)**

**Differential diagnosis**
12. All patients with hepatitis should be tested for HEV, as part of the first-line virological investigation, irrespective of travel history. **(A1)**
13. Patients presenting with suspected drug-induced liver injury should be tested for HEV. **(A1)**

**HEV and the blood supply**
14. Patients with abnormal liver function tests after receiving blood products should be tested for HEV. **(A1)**
15. EASL recommends that blood donor services screen blood donors for HEV by NAT, informed by local risk-assessment and cost-effectiveness studies, both of which may vary considerably by geographical location. **(A1)**

**Treatment of acute hepatitis E**
16. *Statement:* Acute HEV infection does not usually require antiviral therapy. **(A)**
17. Ribavirin treatment may be considered in cases of severe acute hepatitis E or acute-on-chronic liver failure. **(C2)**

**Treatment of chronic HEV infection in solid organ transplant recipients**
18. EASL recommends decreasing immunosuppression at diagnosis of chronic HEV infection in solid organ transplant recipients, if possible. **(B1)**
19. In patients with persisting HEV replication three months after detection of HEV RNA, EASL recommends ribavirin monotherapy for a duration of 12 weeks. **(B1)**
20. At the end of the scheduled period of therapy, HEV RNA should be assessed in the serum and in the stool **(B1)**. If HEV RNA is undetectable in both, EASL suggests stopping ribavirin **(C2)**.
21. *Statement:* The optimal treatment duration in patients who test HEV RNA positive after four or eight weeks of therapy and who are HEV RNA negative after 12 weeks of therapy is unknown. **(C)**
22. In patients in whom HEV RNA is still detectable in the serum and/or in the stool after 12 weeks, ribavirin monotherapy may be continued for an additional three months (six months therapy overall). **(C2)**
23. *Statement:* The optimal therapeutic approach is unknown in patients who show no response to ribavirin and/or who relapse after retreatment. **(C)**
24. Liver transplant recipients who show no response to ribavirin can be considered for treatment with PEGylated-interferon-α. **(C2)**

**Prevention**
25. Immunocompromised individuals and those with chronic liver diseases should avoid consumption of undercooked meat (pork, wild boar and venison) and shellfish. **(B1)**
26. EASL suggests that immunocompromised patients consume meat only if it has been thoroughly cooked to temperatures of at least 70 °C. **(B2)**

### Unanswered questions flagged by the guideline
- Dose and duration of ribavirin in acute liver failure are not defined; whether ribavirin reduces morbidity in acute hepatitis E is unknown.
- Whether corticosteroids should be used in HEV-associated acute liver failure (probably safe, more data needed).
- Optimal ribavirin dose and duration; best approach for ribavirin non-responders and post-retreatment relapsers; mechanism of ribavirin action.
- Definition of chronic HEV infection and when therapy should be initiated in the immunosuppressed.
- Incidence, pathogenesis and treatment of HEV-associated extrahepatic injury; whether other extrahepatic manifestations remain to be discovered.
- Whether organ donors should be screened; whether HEV can be effectively removed from blood products.
- Efficacy of the HEV vaccine against gt 3; safety/efficacy in the immunocompromised, end-stage liver disease and chronic liver disease; duration of natural and vaccine immunity.
- Risk of patient-to-patient transmission by contaminated body fluids/close personal contact.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Virology, genotypes and epidemiology
- HEV: 7.2-kb positive-strand RNA virus, family *Hepeviridae*, genus *Orthohepevirus*, species A; **eight genotypes**, four of which infect humans.
- **gt 1 and 2** — obligate human pathogens, **faecal-oral via contaminated water**; gt 1 Asia/Africa, gt 2 Africa and Mexico; sporadic cases punctuated by outbreaks of thousands to tens of thousands.
- **gt 3 and 4** — **zoonotic**, endemic in pigs, wild boar and deer; human infection from undercooked pork products (e.g. figatellu sausage), direct animal contact, contaminated shellfish, irrigated produce; infectious HEV found in the food chain in Europe, Japan and the USA. gt 3 is the dominant circulating genotype in Europe and almost universally the cause in South America; gt 4 found in a small number of European pigs, and in cattle and their milk in China (surviving pasteurisation) — not yet confirmed in Europe.
- gt 5 and 6 so far only in wild boar; **gt 7 and 8 in camels** (one chronic human infection in a transplant recipient who consumed camel meat and milk).
- Pigs are the primary host; R₀ of HEV in pigs is up to 8 — once one animal in a herd is infected, nearly all become infected.
- At least **2 million locally acquired HEV infections in Europe each year** (seroprevalence and blood-donor data); the 2005 global estimate of 20 million infections / 3 million symptomatic cases / 70,000 deaths is based on gt 1 and 2 only and on insensitive assays, and requires urgent updating.
- Epidemiology is shifting: in eastern China gt 1 has become uncommon and **gt 4 is now the commonest human genotype**; as in high-income countries with zoonotic HEV, hepatitis E in China is now most common in **middle-aged men**.
- 'Hot-spots' of HEV in Europe (south-west and south-east France; parts of the Netherlands, Germany, Scotland, Czech Republic, Italy, Poland); Scottish blood-donor viraemia rose from 1:14,500 to 1:2,481 with a 100% increase in seroprevalence in Edinburgh, mainly in individuals under 35 years.

### Acute infection (gt 3)
- Clinically silent in the vast majority; **probably <5% develop symptoms** (elevated liver enzymes, jaundice, fatigue, itching, nausea).
- Male predominance — M:F 3:1 in an English series, **median age 63 years**; likely host factors (subclinical hepatic steatosis/fibrosis, alcohol, diabetes) rather than differential exposure.
- Progression to **acute liver failure (ALF) is rare** in gt 3 infection; in a German single-centre study of 80 patients with ALF, **HEV RNA was found in 10%** and HEV was considered the probable cause.
- Monitoring of liver enzymes is sufficient in patients without chronic disease: **alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), bilirubin and international normalised ratio (INR)**.
- Immunity after clearance is **not sterilising** — re-infection is possible, though symptomatic hepatitis is less likely.
- Acute hepatitis E on chronic liver disease can cause **acute-on-chronic liver failure**; in elderly patients the course may be more severe. Only **11/343** patients with decompensated chronic liver disease followed in France or the UK had acute hepatitis E and three of those died, and HEV did not drive mortality in that study.

### gt 1 and 2 — pregnancy and chronic liver disease
- gt 1/2 cause brief, self-limiting hepatitis in young adults; **clinical attack rate on exposure approximately 1 in 5**.
- **Mortality in pregnant women is approximately 25%** — fulminant hepatic failure plus obstetric complications (eclampsia, haemorrhage), with high perinatal infant mortality. The cause of the excess maternal mortality is unknown; **there are no data to support ribavirin in pregnancy** (and it is potentially teratogenic).
- **Chronic infection with gt 1 and 2 has not been reported.**
- gt 1 superinfection of chronic liver disease: reported mortality 0–67% across Asian studies; an Indian study put the **12-month mortality at approximately 70%**.
- Only a few case reports of gt 3/4 in pregnancy; **excess maternal mortality has not been observed** with gt 3/4.

### Chronic infection
- Occurs **only with gt 3 and gt 4**, and only in the immunosuppressed: SOT recipients, haematological disorders, HIV, heavy immunosuppression for rheumatic disease.
- Original definition: HEV replication persisting **six months**. However, an observational SOT study found **no spontaneous clearance between three and six months** — all spontaneous clearance occurred within the first three months — so patients **viraemic beyond three months can be regarded as chronically infected and considered for treatment**. (A small number of spontaneous clearances between three and six months have been reported.)
- In SOT recipients, **one-third develop resolving hepatitis and the remainder become chronic**; other smaller studies put progression to chronicity at under 50%.
- In a series of 85 SOT recipients **only one-third were symptomatic**, with fatigue the main symptom.
- Liver enzymes in chronic infection can be normal or only mildly raised; in one study of chronically infected transplant recipients median values at diagnosis were **ALT 260 ± 38 IU/L, AST 155 ± 25 IU/L, gamma-glutamyl transferase 308 ± 56 IU/L**.
- Some patients with persistent HEV replication **remain anti-HEV IgG and IgM negative** — hence NAT on serum or plasma, and if possible stool, is mandatory.
- Rapid progression of fibrosis to **cirrhosis, decompensation and death** is described; **fibrosis can regress after HEV clearance**.
- HEV RNA concentration does not differ between patients with and without progressive fibrosis.
- Risk factors for chronicity after exposure in SOT recipients: **low lymphocyte count at diagnosis** and **use of tacrolimus rather than cyclosporine A**. In HIV, chronic HEV has mostly been described at a **CD4⁺ T-cell count <200/mm³**. No predictive factors identified in other immunosuppressed groups.
- *In vitro*, mTOR inhibitors upregulate HEV replication and mycophenolate suppresses it; in one heart-transplant study mycophenolate was associated with lower risk of chronic hepatitis E, but mycophenolate-treated patients can still develop it.

### Extrahepatic manifestations (Table 2)
- **Neurological** (strongest evidence of causality, and the most important): neuralgic amyotrophy (NA), Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), meningoencephalitis/encephalitis-myelitis, mononeuritis multiplex, myositis, Bell's palsy, vestibular neuritis, peripheral neuropathy. Best documented: **NA, GBS and encephalitis/myelitis**.
  - Approximately **150 cases** of neurological injury with gt 3 described, mainly European; **>90% in the immunocompetent**; neurological injury also occurs with chronic gt 3 infection.
  - NA: **5/47 (10.6%)** of an Anglo/Dutch cohort had evidence of HEV infection at illness onset; HEV-associated NA has a distinct phenotype — **bilateral brachial plexus involvement, more extensive damage, neurological damage outside the brachial plexus, particularly phrenic nerve involvement**. In one centre's experience, the **triad of bilateral shoulder pain in a middle-aged male with abnormal liver tests is highly predictive of HEV infection**.
  - In 450 consecutive patients with acute non-traumatic neurological injury, **2.4% had evidence of HEV infection**, three of whom had NA with bilateral involvement.
  - GBS: HEV infection at onset of neurological illness in **5–11%** across case-control studies (Netherlands, Bangladesh, Japan); a Belgian cohort found **6/73 (8%)**.
  - Patients with HEV-associated neurological injury generally have only **modest abnormalities of liver function and are mostly anicteric**; some have normal liver tests.
- **Renal** (good evidence for causality): **membranoproliferative and membranous glomerulonephritis** (with and without cryoglobulinaemia) and **IgA nephropathy**; reported in both immunocompetent and immunosuppressed. **Renal function improves and proteinuria decreases after HEV clearance**, spontaneous or treatment-induced.
- **Cryoglobulinaemia** in chronic HEV; disappears after antiviral therapy.
- **Haematological:** thrombocytopenia (mild common, occasionally severe); **monoclonal immunoglobulin reported in 25% of acute HEV cases in a UK study** (significance uncertain); cryoglobulinaemia (mainly with renal disease); aplastic anaemia and haemolytic anaemia (case reports only).
- **Other:** acute pancreatitis — **55 cases worldwide, gt 1 only, usually mild; no cases documented with gt 3 or 4**; arthritis, myocarditis, autoimmune thyroiditis (case reports only).
- Causality is established for neurological and renal disease; for the other associations it remains to be established.

### Diagnosis
- **Incubation period approximately 15 to 60 days.** HEV RNA detectable in blood and stool from **around three weeks post-infection**; **viraemia lasts approximately three to six weeks** and **stool shedding approximately four to six weeks**. IgM appears first, then IgG; **IgM is short-lived (usually no longer than three to four months, may persist up to a year)**; the IgG response is long lasting with increasing avidity.
- NAT: assays target the conserved ORF2/ORF3 overlap region and detect all four human genotypes; **analytical sensitivity can be lower than 10 IU/ml**; a WHO International Standard and International Reference Panel (gt 1–4) exist for HEV RNA. Sequence analysis determines genotype.
- **A negative polymerase chain reaction does not exclude acute infection**, and **serology is sometimes negative in the immunosuppressed with chronic infection** — hence serology and NAT are best used in combination (Figure 1).
- Antigen assays (enzyme immunoassay for HEV antigen) can diagnose acute and chronic infection; newer versions offer improved sensitivity; HEV antigen levels are lower in acute than chronic infection, and an **optical density 450/630 ratio >15** was suggested to discriminate acute from chronically infected individuals. HEV antigen may persist for months after ribavirin-induced HEV RNA clearance and does not necessarily correlate with infectious virions.
- Immunohistochemistry for HEV ORF2 protein can establish a histopathological diagnosis.
- **False-positive HEV serology** occurs with non-specific 'sticky' cross-reacting antibodies (a problem in older patients presenting with autoimmune hepatitis) and in Epstein-Barr virus infection.

### Blood supply
- HEV is transmitted by infected blood and blood products (documented in Europe for gt 3, Japan gt 3 and 4). Most transfusion-transmitted infection is asymptomatic; the risk that matters is **chronic infection in an immunosuppressed recipient**, easily overlooked because recipients are asymptomatic with only minor persistent liver test abnormalities.
- English study of 225,000 donors (2012–13): **79 viraemic donors (gt 3)**, 62 infected components transfused; of 43 recipients followed, **18 (42%) had evidence of infection**, more likely with high donor viral load and low donor IgG. **Minimum infective dose 2 × 10⁴ IU HEV RNA**, and **55% of components containing at least that dose transmitted infection**. Three recipients needed reduced immunosuppression (n = 1) or ribavirin (n = 2) to clear virus.
- Japanese analysis of 19 cases (mostly gt 3, two gt 4): **minimum infective dose 3.6 × 10⁴ IU**, rate of infection **50%**.
- Presence of anti-HEV IgG in recipients does not necessarily protect against transfusion-transmitted infection.
- Viraemic donor frequency ranges from **1:600 to between 1:14,799 and 1:74,131** across countries; several countries (Ireland, UK, France, Netherlands, Japan) have introduced universal, targeted or partial screening.
- **NAT is the screening method of choice** — infected donors usually have normal liver tests and are anti-HEV IgM and IgG negative. In England, **transfusion-transmitted HEV is <1% of all human HEV infections**, and transfusing 13 components equates to the annual dietary risk of HEV exposure — donor screening minimises iatrogenic infection but has minor impact on population HEV burden.
- **Solvent/detergent-treated plasma** has transmitted HEV; since 2015 in Europe it is tested for HEV. No reports of transmission by virally inactivated fractionated blood products (purified plasma proteins).
- HEV transmission by **liver and kidney grafts** is extremely rare; **there are no current recommendations for organ donor screening**.

### Treatment
- **Acute hepatitis E:** almost all cases clear spontaneously; antiviral therapy is not usually required. Very few case reports of ribavirin for severe acute hepatitis E — associated with rapid normalisation of liver enzymes and HEV RNA becoming undetectable within a few days (gt 3 and gt 1); liver synthetic function improved rapidly in one case. **Corticosteroids** have been used in individual cases of HEV-related ALF with improved liver function parameters, but evidence is currently insufficient to support corticosteroid treatment in ALF due to HEV.
- **Chronic HEV in SOT recipients — step 1: reduce immunosuppression.** Patients who spontaneously clear HEV have lower tacrolimus trough levels and lower daily steroid doses, implicating T-cell-targeting drugs. Reducing immunosuppression achieves sustained viral clearance in **nearly one-third (about 30%)** of chronically infected SOT recipients.
- **Step 2: ribavirin monotherapy.** Several small series report high sustained virologic response (SVR) rates after a **three-month course**. In a multicentre retrospective study of **59 SOT recipients** treated at a **median dose of 600 mg/day (range 29–1,200 mg/day) for a median of three months (range 1–18 months)**, the **SVR was 78%**. **Relapsers retreated for six months cleared the virus and achieved SVR.** No difference in SVR between ≤3 months and >3 months of therapy; the optimal duration remains unknown.
- Ribavirin adverse effects: **dose-dependent anaemia, dry cough, skin reactions**. Dosing must account for impaired renal function and anaemia — **dose adaptations that consider haemoglobin and estimated glomerular filtration rate are strongly recommended**.
- Mechanism: probably inhibition of HEV replication by depleting guanosine triphosphate pools (synergistic *in vitro* with mycophenolic acid; *in vivo* in SOT recipients, mycophenolic acid did not change HEV RNA kinetics and does not protect against chronic HEV).
- **Predictors of response:** a **high lymphocyte count** independently predicts SVR; a **decrease in HEV RNA concentration ≥0.5 log₁₀ IU/ml at day seven** predicts SVR; ribavirin trough level at day seven or two months did not predict SVR. **Persistence of HEV RNA in stool at the end of ribavirin therapy, despite undetectable serum HEV RNA, is associated with increased risk of viraemia relapse after stopping.**
- **Resistance:** a **G1634R mutation in the HEV polymerase** was found in two cases of ribavirin treatment failure and appeared on therapy in relapsers; pre-treatment G1634R did not affect SVR in a SOT series. Other polymerase variants increase ribavirin sensitivity, increase replication, or decrease replication; their role is uncertain. Ribavirin increases HEV heterogeneity, apparently reversibly.
- **PEGylated-interferon-α** has treated chronic HEV successfully in a small number of liver transplant recipients and a haemodialysis patient after a **three-month course**; it is **generally contraindicated in kidney, pancreas and heart/lung transplant recipients** because it stimulates the immune system and increases the risk of acute rejection.
- **No other antivirals are known to be effective.** Sofosbuvir has *in vitro* activity against HEV replication additive with ribavirin; whether this translates to clinical efficacy *in vivo* is unknown.
- **Chronic HEV in non-transplant immunosuppressed patients** (haematological disorders, HIV): PEGylated-interferon-α, ribavirin, or the combination has been effective in case reports and small series; **9 of 12 stem-cell transplant recipients treated with ribavirin achieved SVR**.

### Prevention
- Cell-culture data: heating virus stocks for **more than two minutes at 70 °C eliminates HEV infectivity**; infective HEV can be recovered after storage at room temperature for 28 days; **80 °C for one minute was required to prevent infection when heating**. How far these data translate to food preparation is unclear.
- Case-control studies identify **consumption of undercooked pig meat, wild boar and deer** as risk factors in Europe. Recommending that the general population avoid undercooked pork is **not currently justified** — immunocompetent individuals are likely to tolerate exposure without significant health threat. Safety of strawberries, spinach, shellfish and camel milk requires further investigation.
- Patient-to-patient transmission is poorly defined; sexual transmission described in men who have sex with men in one study but not found in another cohort. **Stool contains high amounts of infectious HEV and is more infectious than plasma-derived HEV** — strict hygienic recommendations should be observed to prevent spread by contaminated stool, e.g. in hospitals or nursing homes. HEV RNA is detectable in urine; whether HEV is transmitted by saliva, sweat, semen or breast milk is unclear.
- **Vaccine:** licensed in China in 2011. **Efficacy 97% for preventing episodes of symptomatic acute hepatitis**, long-term efficacy confirmed on further follow-up. Based on a protein containing **239 amino acids of HEV ORF2 (aa 368–606), derived from gt 1**; dimerises to HEV 239. It prevented symptomatic gt 4 infections (suggesting cross-genotype efficacy), but **immunity is not sterilising and subclinical infections still occur**. Appears safe in pregnant women; long-term efficacy and safety in chronic liver disease and the immunosuppressed remain to be determined. **Not licensed outside China**; WHO 'prequalification' for emergency settings is being sought.
