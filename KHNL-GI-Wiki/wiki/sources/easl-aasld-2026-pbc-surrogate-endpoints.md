## Summary

A joint EASL–AASLD multi-stakeholder consensus on **how to measure benefit in primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) drug trials and real-world studies**. Its stated aim is "a new framework to support future drug approval in PBC" (p. 584): most recommendations govern trial endpoints, real-world study methods and patient-reported outcome (PRO) instruments. Its trigger is that the three conditionally approved second-line drugs — obeticholic acid (OCA) and the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonists seladelpar and elafibranor — were approved on alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and total bilirubin, which regulators treat as "reasonably likely" to predict benefit but not validated surrogates (p. 584). The OCA confirmatory trial (COBALT) stopped early for recruitment/retention problems, functional unblinding and placebo crossover, and failed to show significant benefit on its primary outcomes; with post-marketing reports of serious liver injury in patients with and without cirrhosis, **OCA was withdrawn from the EU and US markets at the end of November 2024 and September 2025, respectively** (Introduction, p. 584).

**Domain 1 — surrogate endpoints.** ALP and total bilirubin after 12 months and albumin and platelet count after 6–12 months of ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), and ALP/bilirubin after 12 months of second-line therapy, are associated with clinical outcomes; aminotransferases and gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) have insufficient evidence. In phase III trials of ≥12 months, **complete biochemical response (normal ALP + normal total bilirubin) is the ideal endpoint and adequate response (ALP <1.5–1.67× upper limit of normal [ULN] + normal total bilirubin) an adequate one**. Liver stiffness measurement (LSM) by vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) predicts outcomes independently of biochemistry and may serve as a surrogate; biochemical response plus stable/decreased LSM is recommended as a composite endpoint in trials of ≥24 months for early-stage disease.

**Domain 2 — real-world data (RWD) and real-world evidence (RWE).** Ten methodological recommendations: early regulator engagement, single-arm trials with RWD external control arms when randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are not feasible, fit-for-use data sources, harmonised endpoints, prespecified biases and mitigation, internal validation of electronic health record (EHR) and claims data, and quantified linkage error.

**Domain 3 — PROs.** PROs are "foundational – not optional" endpoints (p. 592). **PBC-40** is the primary disease-specific quality-of-life tool; the **Worst Itch Numerical Rating Scale (WI-NRS)** is the primary pruritus tool, with **a 3-point reduction** as the within-patient meaningful-improvement threshold; the **PBC-40 fatigue domain** is the primary fatigue measure, complemented by the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) Fatigue Short Form 7a. Generic instruments must not be used alone; objective tools (actigraphy, wearables) are exploratory only.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Methods (pp. 584–585)
- Steering committee of 12 nominated a **62-member** multi-stakeholder consensus panel: 36 expert clinicians, 4 European Medicines Agency (EMA), 14 industry, 8 patient representatives (Methods, p. 584). US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) representatives were invited but could not attend.
- Three working groups (one per domain); 8 key questions in PICO (Patient, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) format; literature searches March 18–26, 2025, updated November 30, 2025 (p. 584).
- Evidence and recommendation grade: **Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine (OCEBM)** criteria. Each item carries a **level of evidence (LoE)** (this document uses LoE 2, 3 and 5) and, for recommendations, a strength (**strong** or **weak**), reflected in the wording. *Statements* = factual information; *recommendations* = actionable guidance (pp. 584–585).
- Voting: three-point Likert scale (agree / neutral / disagree); **neutral responses excluded from the denominator**. **Consensus predefined as ≥75% agreement**; 50–75% agreement and/or >10% disagreement → revision and revote (Response rates, p. 585).
- Round 1 (August 14 – September 10, 2025): 16 statements and 42 recommendations voted; 44 respondents; 349 written comments. Round 2 (19–22 September 2025): four recommendations and one statement; 30 respondents (p. 585).
- Response rates: **88% (round 1) and 60% (round 2)** in the Summary and Table 1 (denominator excludes regulatory representatives; one vote per industry organisation). See *Contradictions* for the different rates in the Results text.
- **Table 1 — Delphi panel (p. 585):** 53 members — 34 expert clinicians (64%), 8 industry (15%), 8 patient (15%), 3 regulatory (6%; discussion only, did not vote); 32 men (60%), 21 women (40%); Europe 29 (55%), North America 18 (33%), Asia-Pacific 3 (6%), South America 2 (4%), Africa 1 (2%); 18 countries.

### Domain 1 — Surrogate endpoints in PBC clinical trials

**Context (p. 585):** no PBC biomarker has met the regulatory standard for a validated surrogate endpoint; ALP and total bilirubin support conditional/accelerated approval as reasonably likely predictors.

**Evidence base (Section 1.1, p. 586):** systematic review of 69 studies (1995–2025), median 540 patients (range 63–4,845), follow-up 2.5–10 years. Every study of ALP or bilirubin at 12 months of UDCA or second-line therapy found significant associations with outcomes (multivariate p <0.05). Largest cohort (n = 4,845, Lammers et al.): hazard ratio (HR) **2.49 for ALP >2.0× ULN** and **HR 5.06 for total bilirubin >1.0× ULN** (both p <0.001); confirmed in a UK nationwide cohort (n = 3,165). RWE under second-line therapy comes only from OCA and bezafibrate; none yet for seladelpar or elafibranor. Complete response (ALP and bilirubin normalisation) gives stronger prognostic value than the 1.5–1.67× ULN thresholds (p. 586).

#### 1.2 Non-invasive fibrosis tests (Section 1.2, pp. 587–588)

- VCTE is the most extensively validated non-invasive modality in PBC. Limits: LSM is affected by inflammation, cholestasis, operator technique and body habitus; reproducibility concerns across centres; no post-test tissue reassessment (p. 587).
- **LSM >10 kPa at baseline and on treatment** associated with poor outcomes, independent of biochemistry. **≥20% increase in LSM over 1 year** associated with worse outcomes; **≥20% decrease** with improved outcomes (p. 587).
- Optimal timing of repeat LSM uncertain; Bezurso data suggest a **24-month interval** may suit trials, especially early-stage disease (p. 587).
- Enhanced liver fibrosis (ELF) score (hyaluronic acid, procollagen type III N-terminal peptide, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase): baseline value predicts outcomes; value under treatment unclear; not widely used in practice. AST-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) and FIB-4 not used as phase III endpoints, so not addressed (p. 587).
- Systematic review: 10 LSM studies and 2 ELF studies; median 426 patients (range 102–3,284); follow-up 15 months to 7.3 years; all UDCA-treated (p. 587).

**Figure 1 — surrogate endpoint framework (p. 587):** treatment → *biomarker response* (↓ ALP, normal total bilirubin, composite biochemical + LSM scores; ≥12 months, phase III RCT) → *disease modification* (↓ LSM by VCTE, fibrosis stability; ≥24 months, phase IV confirmatory) → *clinical outcome* (decompensation, liver transplantation, death; years).

**Research agenda, Domain 1 (p. 588):** standardise LSM endpoints (thresholds, meaningful change, timing by stage); prospective ELF under treatment; prospective transaminases and non-proprietary fibrosis markers; a unified approval pathway integrating biomarker validation; subgroup analyses of long-term confirmatory trials such as COBALT by responder status, stage and treatment history.

### Domain 2 — Real-world data and real-world evidence in PBC

**Context (pp. 588–589):** scope is RWD/RWE used for causal inference on safety and effectiveness of new PBC therapies (confirmatory and post-marketing). Regulatory-grade RWE needs "fit-for-use" data and prespecified protocols. Precedent: blinatumomab and avelumab received accelerated approval on single-arm trials with RWD external control arms. This domain has **no statements**; all its recommendations are numbered 2.1.x although they sit under Sections 2.1–2.3.

| Label (section) | Recommendation (condensed) | Strength · LoE · Agreement |
|---|---|---|
| Recommendation 2.1.1 (2.1, p. 589) | Early and ongoing engagement with regulatory agencies is strongly recommended for studies aiming to support approval or label expansion | Strong · LoE 5 · 100% |
| Recommendation 2.1.2 (2.1, p. 589) | When RCTs are not feasible, single-arm trials with external control arms from rigorously defined RWD should be considered acceptable to support regulatory-grade evidence of safety and effectiveness | Strong · LoE 3 · 100% |
| Recommendation 2.1.3 (2.2, p. 590) | RWD studies should use appropriate data sources — registries, EHRs, claims databases, distributed data networks — chosen by study objective and fitness-for-use | Strong · LoE 5 · 98% |
| Recommendation 2.1.4 (2.2, p. 590) | Endpoint definitions should be harmonised across data sources and aligned with regulatory standards | Strong · LoE 5 · 100% |
| Recommendation 2.1.5 (2.2, p. 590) | Quality and utility of data sources intended for regulatory-grade evidence should be enhanced by structured approaches addressing key knowledge gaps | Strong · LoE 5 · 100% |
| Recommendation 2.1.6 (2.3, p. 591) | All real-world PBC studies should prespecify anticipated biases | Strong · LoE 5 · 100% |
| Recommendation 2.1.7 (2.3, p. 591) | Mitigation strategies should be prespecified and implemented to control for biases in RWD studies | Strong · LoE 5 · 100% |
| Recommendation 2.1.8 (2.3, p. 591) | EHR and claims datasets should be internally validated (benchmarking against clinical reference standards or manual chart review) to ensure accurate identification of population, exposures, outcomes and covariates | Strong · LoE 2 · 100% |
| Recommendation 2.1.9 (2.3, p. 591) | If tokenisation links de-identified patients across datasets, justify the token(s) and quantify linkage-error risk (collisions and splits) | Strong · LoE 5 · 100% |
| Recommendation 2.1.10 (2.3, p. 591) | Unanticipated or inconclusive RWD results → post hoc investigation of bias sources should be encouraged and transparently reported | Strong · LoE 5 · 100% |

- **Data sources (Section 2.2, p. 589):** disease registries (Global PBC, UK-PBC) are the most granular but reflect tertiary-centre practice; EHRs capture routine practice; claims data capture hard endpoints (transplantation, hospitalisation) but lack laboratory and PRO detail; no single source suffices.

**Table 2 — Biases in PBC real-world studies and mitigation (p. 590):**

| Bias | Explanation | Statistical mitigation | PBC example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selection | Study population may not represent the general PBC population | Population weighting; sensitivity analyses; broader data sources | Registries over-represent engaged patients; International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes have suboptimal sensitivity/specificity |
| Confounding by indication / channelling | Sicker patients more likely to receive certain treatments | Propensity score matching/weighting; multivariable adjustment | Progressive disease more likely to start second-line therapy; access influenced by insurance |
| Immortal time | Unaccounted time between cohort entry and treatment start | Time-dependent Cox models; proper time alignment; consistent index date | Treatment start as time zero creates survival bias; match treated and controls at equal time from cohort entry |
| Information | Inaccurate/inconsistent data collection → wrong exposure or outcome | Validation studies; standardised collection; algorithm validation against chart review | Diagnostic algorithms need validation; transplant waitlisting is more accurate and proximal than transplantation as an outcome |
| Misclassification | Wrong categorisation of exposure or outcome | Validated algorithms; sensitivity analyses; independent adjudication | Compare intention-to-treat (ITT) and as-treated analyses for early discontinuation; adverse events hard to detect without chart review |
| Inconsistent follow-up timing | Uneven measurement schedules affect outcome detection | Time-varying covariates; adjust for follow-up intensity; active comparator design | Symptomatic patients visit more; treated patients visit for prescription renewal |
| Symptom/PRO documentation | Symptoms under-reported or inconsistently recorded | Structured symptom tools; standardised PRO instruments | PROs drive start/stop decisions; intractable pruritus may drive transplant listing independent of biochemical response |
| Missing data | Missing information, especially missing not at random (MNAR) | Multiple imputation; sensitivity analyses for MNAR | Both greater and lesser severity increase missingness; long gaps common in stable asymptomatic disease |

**Figure 2 (p. 592):** three-stage workflow for regulatory-grade RWE — (1) data assessment (accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity, uniqueness); (2) bias identification and mitigation design (known and unknown confounders → standardised definitions, same in/exclusion criteria, propensity weighting, restriction/matching, negative control outcomes, historical untreated cohorts as external controls, quantitative bias analysis, sensitivity analyses, test-retest with a second RWD source); (3) harmonisation with trial data (eligibility, time frame, region, endpoint definitions).

**Research agenda, Domain 2 (p. 591):** adapt target trial emulation and external control arms to PBC; strengthen registries/EHRs/claims/networks with standardised endpoints and PROs; benchmark EHR/claims data; refine tokenisation and report linkage error; account for global variability in care.

### Domain 3 — Patient-reported outcomes and symptom assessment in PBC

**Context (pp. 591–592):** fatigue, pruritus, cognitive dysfunction, emotional distress and social participation are not reflected by biochemistry or histology and often persist despite biochemical response. Routine PRO inclusion in all PBC trials is "essential". Measure comorbid confounders of fatigue (thyroid disease, depression, primary sleep disorders, autonomic dysfunction) and prespecify adjustments (p. 592).

**Table 3 — Ideal characteristics of a PRO instrument in PBC (p. 593):** validated in a PBC population (reliability, validity, responsiveness); patient-derived content; covers fatigue, pruritus, cognitive function, emotional well-being, social participation; established MCIC by anchor-based methods; responsive to change; accepted or reviewed by FDA/EMA; recall period matched to symptom variability; low burden for repeated use; linguistically validated translations; validated for electronic PRO platforms.

#### 3.2 Global quality of life (p. 593)

- PBC-40: six domains (symptoms, fatigue, itch, cognitive, emotional, social); most robustly validated PBC quality-of-life tool. Generic tools — EuroQoL 5-Dimension (EQ-5D), Short Form Health Survey (SF-36), PROMIS-29 — give health-utility and cross-disease data but miss fatigue and pruritus. Panel's hierarchy: PBC-40 core, generic tools for economic/comparative analyses, global impression measures as anchors; report PBC-40 total with domain results.

| Label | Recommendation | Strength · LoE · Agreement |
|---|---|---|
| Recommendation 3.2.1 | PBC-40 should be considered the primary disease-specific tool for global quality of life | Strong · LoE 2 · 96% |
| Recommendation 3.2.2 | EQ-5D, SF-36 and PROMIS-29 **should not be used in isolation** to assess quality of life in PBC | Strong · LoE 5 · 100% |
| Recommendation 3.2.3 | Patient global impression tools (PGI-S, PGI-C) **may** support analyses establishing meaningful within-patient change thresholds | Weak · LoE 5 · 100% |

#### 3.3 Pruritus (p. 594)

- **WI-NRS** (and the near-identical, separately validated Itch Ro score): 0–10 scale of **peak itch over the preceding 12 to 24 hours**; validated in cholestatic liver disease including PBC; accepted by FDA and EMA in pivotal trials of ileal bile acid transporter inhibitors and PPAR agonists.
- **≥3-point reduction** = individual-level benchmark for meaningful improvement, especially in moderate-to-severe itch; **≥2 points** may be meaningful for some patients; exploratory analyses at **≥2, ≥3 and ≥4 points** encouraged. Meaningful between-group difference not yet defined.
- **Placebo response 20–40% mean improvement** in pruritus trials — anticipate in design.
- 5-D Itch Scale covers duration, degree, direction, disability, distribution.

#### 3.4 Fatigue (pp. 594–597)

- Fatigue affects **approximately 40–80%** of patients; severity does not track disease stage and may persist despite biochemical response; current licensed therapies do not consistently improve it (p. 594).
- **PBC-40 fatigue domain:** score range **11 to 55**; **≥29 = clinically significant fatigue** (p. 595).
- Tiered strategy: PBC-40 fatigue domain core; PROMIS Fatigue Short Form 7a (PSF-7a) complementary; Fatigue Impact Scale (FIS), PGI/CGI, Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) and Orthostatic Grading Scale (OGS) supportive (p. 596).

### Clinically usable points
- **Response targets** (as trial endpoints, Recommendation 1.1.1): complete response = normal ALP + normal total bilirubin (ideal); adequate response = ALP <1.5–1.67× ULN + normal total bilirubin. Inadequate response after 6–12 months of UDCA, or at any time later, carries higher risk of poor outcomes (Statement 1.1.8).
- Prognostic markers on UDCA: ALP, total bilirubin (cut-points from 0.6× ULN), albumin and platelets relative to the lower limit of normal; AST/ALT/GGT not established (Statements 1.1.1–1.1.7).
- **LSM by VCTE** adds prognosis independent of biochemistry (Statement 1.2.2): >10 kPa adverse; ≥20% rise over 1 year worse, ≥20% fall better (Section 1.2 text, p. 587).
- **Pruritus:** WI-NRS = 0–10 worst itch over 12–24 h; 3-point drop = meaningful within-patient improvement (Recommendation 3.3.3).
- **Fatigue:** PBC-40 fatigue domain ≥29 (range 11–55) = clinically significant (p. 595); consider confounders (thyroid disease, depression, primary sleep disorders, autonomic dysfunction) (p. 592).
- OCA withdrawn from the US market September 2025 (Introduction, p. 584).

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Item counts:** the Summary and Results state 16 statements and 42 recommendations, but the numbered items printed are **15 statements** (1.1.1–1.1.9, 1.2.1–1.2.6) and **41 recommendations** (Domain 1: 4; Domain 2: 10; Domain 3: 27). Every printed item is listed above.
- **Panel size:** 62-member consensus panel (Summary; Methods p. 584: 36 clinicians, 4 EMA, 14 industry, 8 patient) vs 53-member Delphi panel (Results and Table 1, p. 585: 34 clinicians, 8 industry, 8 patient, 3 regulatory).
- **Response rates:** Summary and Table 1 give 88% (round 1, 44 respondents) and 60% (round 2, 30 respondents); the Results text gives "an 80% response rate" for round 1 and "response rate 68%" for round 2 (p. 585).
- **Recommendation 1.1.1 wording:** the recommendation calls complete/adequate response "ideal or adequate" endpoints; the Section 1.1 narrative says "ideal or acceptable primary endpoints" and adds eligibility defined by baseline biochemistry at screening (p. 586).
- **Second-line drugs named:** Statements 1.1.2 and 1.1.4 cover bezafibrate or OCA; Statement 1.1.9 adds fenofibrate, while the narrative says second-line RWE comes from OCA and bezafibrate studies (p. 586).
- **Pruritus threshold:** Recommendation 3.3.3 says "a 3-point reduction"; the narrative and Table 4 say "≥3-point" (pp. 594–595).
- **PBC-40 fatigue domain role:** Recommendation 3.4.2 makes it the primary fatigue measure; Table 4 lists its recommended use as "Secondary/co-primary" (p. 595).
- **Figure 3 vs Table 4:** the Figure 3B heatmap shades the 5-D Itch Scale (pruritus), Sleep Interference NRS (pruritus, sleep) and FIS (fatigue) as "validated in PBC", whereas Table 4 marks all three as not validated in PBC. SF-36 is "Yes" for PBC validation in Table 4 but sits in Tier 2 ("validated in related populations") in Figure 3A.
- **Versus [[primary-biliary-cholangitis]]:**
  - The page's line "ALP <2× ULN on treatment is a reliable predictor" ([[aasld-2018-pbc]]) is narrower than this consensus, which accepts ALP cut-points at 1×, 1.5×, 1.67× and 2× ULN; its adequate-response definition (ALP <1.5–1.67× ULN with normal bilirubin) matches the [[aasld-aga-2026-pbc]] definition already on the page.
  - Liver stiffness: the page carries >9.6 kPa ([[aasld-2018-pbc]]) and ≥10 kPa ([[aasld-aga-2026-pbc]]); this consensus states >10 kPa (Section 1.2 narrative) — consistent with 2026.
  - Fatigue prevalence: the page says 50–78%; this consensus says approximately 40–80%.
  - The page's OCA section does not mention the US market withdrawal (September 2025) stated here.
- **Scope:** recommendations govern trial and real-world study design, not individual patient management; the biochemical and LSM thresholds are framed as trial endpoints and prognostic associations.
