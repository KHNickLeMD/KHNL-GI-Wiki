## Summary

An AGA Institute Clinical Practice Update addressing a gap the major society guidelines had left open: NAFLD is the most common chronic liver disease worldwide (>25% of the US and global population; an estimated 80–100 million Americans), up to 1 in 4 of whom have NASH, and NAFLD-related HCC is rising — yet guidance on **whom to screen outside established cirrhosis**, with what tool, and how often was essentially absent. The update develops Best Practice Advice for 8 key clinical issues: whom to screen (cirrhosis; noninvasive markers of advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis), whom **not** to screen (NAFLD without advanced fibrosis), how to judge and document ultrasound adequacy, what to do when ultrasound is inadequate, and three risk-modification advices (alcohol/tobacco, diabetes/dyslipidemia, obesity).

The document deliberately uses **"screening" for both the index assessment and ongoing periodic assessment** (normally called surveillance), and says so explicitly in its introduction.

Two decision-critical contributions stand out. First, an operational way to decide screening in a NAFLD patient **without** biopsy- or imaging-proven cirrhosis: combine **at least 2 noninvasive tests, each from a different one of the 3 test groups** (point-of-care scores, specialized blood panels, imaging elastography), use **high cut-points chosen for specificity**, and screen only when both tests are concordant. Second, the **LI-RADS ultrasound visualization score (A/B/C)** with its definitions, which converts "the ultrasound was hard" into a documented category that drives the next imaging choice.

The update is **ungraded** — see the note below.

> **Grading:** this document assigns **no GRADE ratings, no strength-of-recommendation labels, and no evidence levels** to any of its Best Practice Advice statements. Each BPA is presented as a numbered section heading only. Any grade attached to these statements elsewhere is not from this source.

The statements below are reproduced exactly as the article titles them (each is a numbered section heading in the published text).

| # | Best Practice Advice (verbatim) |
|---|---|
| **1** | Screening for Hepatocellular Carcinoma Should Be Considered in All Patients With Cirrhosis Due to Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease |
| **2** | Patients With Nonalcoholic Fatty Liver Disease With Noninvasive Markers Showing Evidence of Advanced Liver Fibrosis or Cirrhosis Should Be Considered for Hepatocellular Carcinoma Screening |

## Key Findings / Claims

### BPA 1 — who is screened: NAFLD cirrhosis

- **Screening threshold:** HCC screening should be offered to patients with cirrhosis of any etiology when the risk of HCC is **approximately ≥1.5% per year**, "as has been noted with NAFLD cirrhosis."
- **Screening benefit is restricted** to patients with **compensated cirrhosis**, or **decompensated cirrhosis listed for liver transplantation**.
- Entry into a screening program also weighs **age, overall health, functional status, willingness/ability to comply**, and whether the patient would be a candidate for HCC treatment if one were found.
- **Incidence data in NAFLD cirrhosis:**
  - Literature range **1%–3% per year**.
  - Ascha et al (n = 195 NASH cirrhosis, median 3.2 y): 25 incident HCC, cumulative incidence **2.6%/y**; on multivariable analysis **older age and alcohol consumption were the only independent predictors**.
  - Japanese cohort (n = 69 NASH cirrhosis, median 5 y): 11 HCC, annual incidence **2.3%**.
  - US Veterans Affairs cohort (296,707 NAFLD patients vs 296,707 matched controls without known liver disease): NAFLD **with cirrhosis** had the highest overall annual incidence, **1.06%/y**, ranging from **0.2% in women** to **2.4% in older Hispanics with cirrhosis**.
- **Explicit non-recommendation:** most age/sex/race subgroup estimates were close to or exceeded 1%/y, but the update states it does **not** recommend using age-, sex-, or ethnicity-specific rates in the decision of whether to screen in NAFLD-related cirrhosis.
- ≈**80%** of patients with NAFLD cirrhosis have coexisting diabetes or obesity.

### BPA 2 — who is screened: noninvasive markers, no proven cirrhosis

Applies when advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis has **not** been established by histology or imaging. Liver imaging rules cirrhosis **in** but has **low negative predictive value** — absence of overt cirrhotic features does not exclude advanced fibrosis. Liver biopsy is not tenable as a primary staging method given the population size.

**The combination rule — the decision:**

- Combine **at least 2 noninvasive testing modalities, each drawn from 1 of the 3 main groups of tests.**
- Screen only if **both tests are concordant** for advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis.
- Interpret in the context of full clinical information — physical examination, laboratory profile, imaging findings.
- When using noninvasive tests for this purpose, **a higher cut-point is desirable to maximize specificity (90%)**.

**The 3 groups of noninvasive fibrosis tests:**

| Group | Tests named |
|---|---|
| **Point-of-care** (demographics + routine labs; no or little cost) | AST/ALT ratio; AST-to-platelet ratio index; BMI+AST+ALT+diabetes; **NAFLD fibrosis score** (age, BMI, AST, ALT, platelets, albumin, diabetes/impaired fasting glucose); **FIB-4** (age, AST, ALT, platelets) |
| **Specialized serum/plasma** | **Enhanced Liver Fibrosis (ELF) panel** (hyaluronic acid, PIIINP, TIMP-1); Fibrospect 2 (hyaluronic acid, α-2 macroglobulin, TIMP-1); FibroMeter; Fibrosure |
| **Imaging** | **VCTE** (vibration-controlled transient elastography); shear-wave elastography; acoustic radiation force impulse imaging; **MRE** (magnetic resonance elastography) |

**Cut-points given:**

| Test | Cut-point | What it signifies (per this source) |
|---|---|---|
| FIB-4 | **>2.67** | Higher odds of cirrhosis or bridging fibrosis; in a large VA database, associated with increased HCC risk **both in those with known cirrhosis and in those without a prior cirrhosis diagnosis** |
| ELF | **≥11.3** | Associated with cirrhosis |
| VCTE | **16.1 kPa** | Noninvasive detection of cirrhosis for purposes of HCC screening |
| MRE | **5 kPa** | Noninvasive detection of cirrhosis for purposes of HCC screening |

- Worked example given in the text: FIB-4 (point of care) **or** ELF (specialized blood test) **with** an elastography examination.
- **Why the VCTE and MRE numbers differ:** different formulas produce the kPa value — **Young's modulus for VCTE, shear modulus for MRE** — so the thresholds are not interchangeable.

### BPA 3 — who is NOT screened

- Patients with NAFLD and **earlier fibrosis stages (stage 0–2)**: HCC incidence is "extremely low and not precisely defined," so **systematic HCC screening may not be prudent at this time**.
- VA cohort incidence rates: **0.21 per 1000 person-years (0.02% annual risk) in NAFLD** vs **0.02 per 1000 person-years (0.002% annual) in controls**; adjusted **HR 7.62 (95% CI 5.76–10.09)** — relative risk is high, absolute risk is not.
- Two systematic reviews/meta-analyses (White et al; updated by Reig et al) independently concluded the risk estimate in noncirrhotic NAFLD is **too low to justify routine screening** where there is no advanced fibrosis.
- **Genetic risk–based screening is not supported.** The **PNPLA3** risk allele is enriched in NAFLD-related HCC and NAFLD-related cirrhosis, but limited test availability and absent data on using PNPLA3 status independent of cirrhosis mean the clinical utility of a genetic approach "is currently not supported." **HSD17B13** may protect against progression to cirrhosis, but whether it alters HCC risk — especially without cirrhosis — is unknown.

### BPA 4 — documenting ultrasound adequacy

- In patients with cirrhosis and a **good acoustic window**, ultrasound is highly accurate and cost-effective for HCC detection.
- Ultrasound quality is **inadequate for HCC screening in approximately 20% of all patients** on independent review by abdominal-trained radiologists; inadequacy is significantly more likely in **overweight or obese** patients, who are in turn more likely to have NASH cirrhosis. Ultrasound is also **operator-dependent** irrespective of etiology.
- **Operator standard:** ultrasound should be performed by individuals with **at least level 2 credentials** per the EFSUMB / Societatea Română de Ultrasonografie în Medicină și Biologie Classification of Level of Practice (technical experience by procedure volume, supervision, knowledge of anatomy and disease processes). How well this is followed in practice is unclear.
- **What to record consistently:** **parenchymal heterogeneity**, **visualization of the entire liver**, and **beam attenuation** — the criteria compiled by the **2017 LI-RADS ultrasound quality criteria**. The update notes no studies have systematically assessed the utility of these criteria.
- **LI-RADS ultrasound visualization score (definitions as given):**

| Score | Definition (verbatim sense) |
|---|---|
| **A** | No or minimal limitation |
| **B** | Moderate limitation — the examination **may obscure small masses** |
| **C** | Severe limitation — the examination **may miss focal liver lesions** |

### BPA 5 — what to do when ultrasound is inadequate

- If ultrasound quality is inadequate — **especially category C, and in some cases category B** — consider another imaging modality (CT or MRI) for HCC screening, **every 6 months, with or without α-fetoprotein**.
- **Reserve, don't generalize.** Cost-effectiveness of replacing ultrasound with CT or MRI for **all** NAFLD-related cirrhosis is "not likely to be favorable"; CT/MRI is best reserved for those with low ultrasound quality.
- Cost-effectiveness numbers cited: MRI incurred **$5562 incremental cost, 0.384 incremental life-years, 0.221 incremental quality life-years** vs ultrasound; **ICER <$50,000/quality life-year only when annual HCC incidence was >1.81%**. That analysis was in **HBV-related cirrhosis**, and the annual HCC risk in NAFLD-related cirrhosis **may be slightly lower — 0.5%–1.4% per year**.
- The PRIUS study suggested gadoxetic-acid MRI screening may outperform ultrasound, but **this was not specifically studied or reported in patients with poor ultrasound quality**.
- **Stated gap:** "the appropriate interval of follow-up imaging in the context of either CT or MRI and their use in combination with serum α-fetoprotein remains to be studied."

### BPA 6 — alcohol and tobacco

**Tobacco**

- Smoking is associated with increased risk of NAFLD, with advanced fibrosis in NAFLD, and with HCC; several tobacco-smoke constituents are liver carcinogens.
- Meta-analysis vs never smokers: adjusted meta-relative risk **1.51 (95% CI 1.37–1.67) for current smokers**; **1.12 (95% CI 0.78–1.60) for former smokers**.
- Liver Cancer Pooling Project (14 US prospective cohorts; 1,518,741 individuals, 1423 HCC cases): current smokers **HR 1.86 (95% CI 1.57–2.20)**; those who **quit >30 years ago had risk near that of never smokers**.
- Population attributable fraction for smoking: **13% of HCC globally, 9% in North America**.
- Smoking is also associated with lower survival in HCC.
- **All patients with NAFLD should be counseled to abstain from tobacco smoking.** The panel adds that although specific data do not exist, **e-cigarettes may turn out to be equally harmful** and patients should be counseled to abstain from those as well.

**Alcohol**

- Population attributable fraction for alcohol in HCC: **26% globally, 32% in North America** (overall PAF range 13%–32%).
- Meta-analysis of prospective cohort/nested case–control studies: **no significant increased risk for moderate use (<3 drinks/d)**; **RR 1.16 (95% CI 1.01–1.34) for heavy drinking (≥3 drinks/d)**.
- Second meta-analysis: only **heavy drinking (>50 g/d)** raised HCC risk — **RR 2.07 (95% CI 1.66–2.58)**.
- Liver Cancer Pooling Project: vs nondrinkers, only **≥7 drinks/d** was associated with an **87% increased HCC risk**; **light or moderate consumption (<3 drinks/d) was inversely associated** with HCC risk.
- Increased HCC-death risk is **attenuated after prolonged (>10 years) sobriety**.
- In NASH cirrhosis specifically, one single-center study found **any alcohol use** was an independent risk factor for cancer — **HR 3.8 (95% CI 1.6–8.9; P = .002)**.
- The precise threshold at which HCC risk rises **varies across studies**; alcohol and obesity act **synergistically** on cirrhosis and HCC risk.
- **Advice:** minimize alcohol, with complete abstinence when feasible, in NAFLD with advanced fibrosis; **all patients with NAFLD cirrhosis should abstain** — alcohol raises HCC risk as well as the risk of hepatic decompensation and death from liver disease.

### BPA 7 — diabetes and dyslipidemia

**Type 2 diabetes**

| Exposure | Association with incident HCC |
|---|---|
| T2DM (pooled Nurses' Health Study + Health Professionals Follow-Up Study; 120,826 women, 50,284 men, 32 y, 4,488,410 person-years) | **HR 4.59 (95% CI 2.98–7.07)** |
| T2DM duration **≥10 years** | **HR 7.52 (95% CI 3.88–14.58)** |
| T2DM **+ obesity + hypertension + dyslipidemia** | **HR 8.10 (95% CI 2.48–26.70)** |

**Antidiabetic drugs and HCC incidence** (meta-analysis of 10 studies, 22,650 HCC cases in patients with T2DM):

| Agent | Effect on HCC incidence |
|---|---|
| **Metformin** | **OR 0.50 (95% CI 0.34–0.73)** — decreased |
| **Sulfonylurea** | **OR 1.62 (95% CI 1.16–2.24)** — increased |
| **Insulin** | **OR 2.61 (95% CI 1.46–4.65)** — increased |

- Bottom line given: **metformin is associated with a significant decrease in incident HCC among patients with T2DM who have cirrhosis and may be used where T2DM treatment is indicated**, pending further investigation in coexisting NASH.
- Metformin, sulfonylureas, insulin, SGLT2 inhibitors, and DPP-4 inhibitors have **not** shown meaningful improvement in liver histology in NAFLD. GLP-1 receptor agonists and thiazolidinediones (PPAR α/γ/δ agonism) have shown potential activity on steatosis, inflammation, ballooning, and fibrosis; **pioglitazone and liraglutide** achieved NASH resolution in RCTs of biopsy-proven NASH — but **no association with a change in HCC risk has been demonstrated** for any of them.

**Dyslipidemia and statins**

- Dyslipidemia is a well-established risk factor for NAFLD (prevalence ~53.8% among lipid-clinic attendees; 78.0% in the subgroup with the highest TC/HDL or TG/HDL ratios) and is associated with NASH and cardiovascular disease — but has **not** been shown to be an independent risk factor for liver-related mortality, and **data are inadequate to confirm an association with HCC**.
- Statins show **no histologic improvement** in steatosis, steatohepatitis, or fibrosis in biopsy-proven NASH, and mixed cardiovascular-outcome results in NAFLD.
- Statins are **not** associated with higher DILI risk in NAFLD than in patients without NAFLD, and **can be used safely** to treat dyslipidemia.
- Outside NAFLD, statin use is associated with decreased HCC — meta-analysis of 10 studies (4298 HCC cases among 1,459,417 patients): **OR 0.63 (95% CI 0.52–0.76)**.
- **Panel position:** the benefits of statin therapy in patients with dyslipidemia and NAFLD **significantly outweigh the risk and statins should be utilized routinely.**
- Optimal management of diabetes and dyslipidemia is recommended for its established diabetic and cardiovascular benefits, and **may also** mitigate incident HCC risk; large RCTs of antidiabetic and lipid-lowering agents as HCC chemoprevention in NAFLD cirrhosis are still needed.

### BPA 8 — obesity

- Obesity is the most common and best-described risk factor for NAFLD, across the whole spectrum: **overweight BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m²**, **obesity BMI 30.0–39.9 kg/m²**, **morbid obesity BMI ≥40.0 kg/m²**.
- **>95%** of patients with morbid obesity undergoing bariatric surgery have histologic evidence of NAFLD; **severity of obesity is associated with increased risk of advanced fibrosis and of HCC in NAFLD**.
- **Hypocaloric diet — reduction of daily caloric intake by 500–1000 kcal** — with or without moderate-intensity exercise reduces hepatic steatosis in randomized trials and can generally be recommended in NAFLD and/or biopsy-proven NASH.
- **However:** neither medical weight loss nor moderate-intensity exercise has been associated with a **decreased risk of incident HCC**.
- **Bariatric surgery** is associated with **NASH resolution in up to 85%** and **fibrosis improvement in 33%** of patients; **data are inadequate to confirm any impact on HCC incidence or outcomes**.
- The impact of **pharmacotherapy, meal replacement, and bariatric endoscopy** on HCC risk **has not been studied**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **BPA 2 vs. the current wiki position on non-cirrhotic advanced fibrosis.** BPA 2 is titled "…With Noninvasive Markers Showing Evidence of **Advanced Liver Fibrosis or Cirrhosis** Should Be Considered for Hepatocellular Carcinoma Screening," and its text says patients in whom two tests are concordant for **advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis** should be considered for screening. [[aasld-2023-hcc]] GS 9 (Level 3, Weak) advises **against** routine surveillance in non-cirrhotic NAFLD/MASLD **including advanced fibrosis without cirrhosis**, and [[aga-2026-hcc-surveillance]] BPA 3 agrees. Both are newer tier-1 documents, so the **newer position is what [[hcc-surveillance]] and [[nafld-masld]] assert**; AGA 2020's overlapping content is retained as the operational method for detecting *unrecognized cirrhosis* by noninvasive testing — which is what the BPA 2 body text actually describes (its cut-points are cirrhosis cut-points, applied "in the absence of biopsy-confirmed cirrhosis or overt cirrhosis on imaging"). AASLD 2023's case-by-case exception for suspected understaging of fibrosis sits in the same space.
- **Screening threshold drift.** AGA 2020 anchors screening to an annual HCC incidence of **approximately ≥1.5%**; [[aasld-2023-hcc]] models the initiation threshold in cirrhosis at **~1.0%/y**. Newer guidance wins; the older number is what many readers still carry.
- **AFP.** AGA 2020 attaches AFP only as optional ("with or without α-fetoprotein") to the CT/MRI fallback and never states an ultrasound + AFP screening pair. [[aasld-2023-hcc]] GS 10 and [[aga-2026-hcc-surveillance]] BPA 2 both recommend **ultrasound + AFP in combination**. The newer combination recommendation stands.
- **CT as a fallback modality.** AGA 2020 BPA 5 permits **either CT or MRI** when ultrasound is suboptimal. [[aasld-2023-hcc]] GS 11a names **contrast-enhanced MRI** as the alternative in suboptimal ultrasound, and GS 11 advises against routine CT/MRI-based surveillance generally (citing repeated radiation/contrast exposure). The newer, MRI-specific framing is what the wiki asserts.
- **Metformin, sulfonylureas, and insulin and HCC risk** are observational associations (meta-analysis of observational studies), not trial evidence; the update itself calls for RCTs of antidiabetic and lipid-lowering chemoprevention in NAFLD cirrhosis. [[aasld-2023-nafld]] GS 28 separately advises against metformin **as a treatment for NASH** — a different question from HCC chemoprevention, and not in conflict.
- **Open questions the source names:** the appropriate follow-up interval for CT or MRI screening and its combination with AFP; whether genetic risk scores could identify an early-fibrosis population at high enough risk to screen; whether bariatric surgery, weight-loss pharmacotherapy, meal replacement, or bariatric endoscopy change HCC incidence.
