## Summary

The official AGA Institute guideline on the **medical management of [[opioid-induced-constipation|opioid-induced constipation]] (OIC)**. Six graded statements are issued across four drug categories — traditional laxatives, PAMORAs ([[pamoras|peripherally acting μ-opioid receptor antagonists]]), intestinal secretagogues, and selective 5-HT agonists. The structure is deliberately sequential: **laxatives are first-line for every patient with OIC (strong, moderate)**, and PAMORAs are reserved for **laxative-refractory** OIC, where naldemedine (strong, high) and naloxegol (strong, moderate) are recommended and methylnaltrexone is suggested (conditional, low). For [[lubiprostone]] and [[prucalopride]] the AGA issues **no recommendation** and labels each an **evidence gap**.

Scope is explicitly narrow. The guideline covers **medical** management only — it does **not** address psychological therapy, alternative medicine, surgery, or devices, and neither the guideline nor the technical review addresses the **diagnostic evaluation** of OIC. Combination opioid agonist/antagonist agents (e.g. oxycodone + naloxone) were not addressed by the technical review or by any recommendation statement, though the narrative notes they may produce less constipation than pure opioid agonists in chronic pain.

The narrative sections carry the decision content that the graded statements do not: the **Rome IV definition of OIC**, a consensus definition, the operational meaning of **"laxative refractory"** (scheduled laxatives from ≥2 classes, not as-needed), the **Bowel Function Index** with its **≥30** escalation cutoff, and the pharmacology that separates the three PAMORAs. OIC is estimated to affect **40%–80%** of patients on chronic opioid therapy, and constipation is described as by far the most common and debilitating GI effect of opioids.

Mechanistically, opioids act on **μ, δ, and κ** receptors in the gut — κ in the stomach and small intestine, μ in the small intestine and proximal colon. OIC arises primarily through **enteric μ-receptor activation** → increased tonic non-propulsive contractions in small and large intestine, increased colonic fluid absorption, and stool desiccation; opioids also raise the minimum rectal sensory threshold and increase anal sphincter tone. Because OIC is mechanistically distinct from other constipation, the guideline argues it deserves dedicated management.

**GRADE conventions the guideline prints (Tables 1–2)**

- Quality grades: **High** (very confident the true effect lies close to the estimate) · **Moderate** · **Low** · **Very low** · **Evidence gap** ("available evidence insufficient to determine true effect").
- Strength wording: **Strong** = "The AGA recommends…"; **Conditional** = "The AGA suggests…"; **No recommendation** = "The AGA makes no recommendation…" — used when "the confidence in the effect estimate is so low that any recommendation is speculative at this time."

**Definitions of OIC**

- **Rome IV definition** (quoted in the guideline): *new or worsening symptoms of constipation when initiating, changing, or increasing opioid therapy* that must include **2 or more** of: (1) straining >25% of defecations; (2) lumpy or hard stools >25% of defecations; (3) sensation of incomplete evacuation >25% of defecations; (4) sensation of anorectal obstruction/blockage >25% of defecations; (5) manual maneuvers to facilitate >25% of defecations (e.g. digital evacuation, support of the pelvic floor); or (6) fewer than 3 spontaneous bowel movements per week.
- **Consensus definition:** "a change when initiating opioid therapy from baseline bowel habits that is characterized by any of the following: reduced bowel movement frequency, development or worsening of straining to pass bowel movements, a sense of incomplete rectal evacuation, or harder stool consistency."
- These definitions include **not only a change in stool frequency, but also changes in stool consistency and/or difficulty with defecation**. OIC is defined variably in the literature and some studies do not include an explicit definition.
- **Opioid-induced bowel dysfunction (OIBD)** = the set of GI adverse effects of opioid therapy: constipation, gastroesophageal reflux disease, nausea and vomiting, bloating, and abdominal pain.

**Epidemiology**

- ~9–12 million Americans suffer from chronic pain annually; **4%–5%** of the US population uses prescription opioids regularly.
- OIC affects an estimated **40%–80%** of patients taking chronic opioid therapy; some degree of constipation is near universal in patients taking opioid medications.

**"Laxative refractory" — the operational definition**

- In some OIC studies, "inadequate laxative response" was defined as **moderate or severe symptoms of constipation despite use of laxatives from 1 or more laxative classes for a minimum of 4 days over a 2-week period.**
- Based on this, the panel **favored use of a combination of at least 2 types of laxatives before escalating therapy**, and held that **scheduled** use of laxatives (vs "as needed") is required before determining whether alternative OIC therapy is necessary. Example given: **daily osmotic laxative + stimulant laxative at least 2–3 times per week.**
- The panel acknowledged that **strong RCT-level evidence supporting any particular laxative combination or titration regimen is lacking.**
- Laxatives with demonstrated efficacy in OIC and chronic idiopathic constipation trials: **PEG** (osmotic), **bisacodyl** and **sodium picosulfate** (stimulants).
- **Rescue therapy** was offered in the majority of OIC clinical trials when the patient did not have a bowel movement within a specified time, **usually 48–72 hours**; most commonly **oral bisacodyl or bisacodyl suppositories**.

**Bowel Function Index (Table 5)** — a simplified 3-question tool validated in the OIC patient population; each item scored 0–100 over the last 7 days; **total score = mean of the 3 scores**.

| Item | Question | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | During the last 7 days, how would you rate your **ease of defecation** on a scale from 0 to 100? | 0 = easy or no difficulty; 100 = severe difficulty |
| 2 | During the last 7 days, how would you rate your **feeling of incomplete bowel evacuation** on a scale from 0 to 100? | 0 = not at all; 100 = very strong |
| 3 | During the last 7 days, how would you rate your **constipation** on a scale from 0 to 100? | 0 = not at all; 100 = very strong |

- A score of **≥30** is consistent with clinically significant constipation; a consensus panel recommended using this cutoff to determine which patients have **inadequately responded to first-line laxatives** and would benefit from escalation of therapy.
- The **Patient Assessment of Constipation Symptoms** is another validated symptom measure used in OIC studies, though it may be less practical for clinical use.

**Classes of agents and mechanisms (Table 4)** — osmotic (PEG, lactulose, magnesium citrate, magnesium hydroxide); stimulant (bisacodyl, sodium picosulfate, senna); detergent/surfactant stool softener (docusate); lubricant (mineral oil); PAMORAs (naldemedine, naloxegol, methylnaltrexone); intestinal secretagogue (lubiprostone); selective 5-HT agonist (prucalopride). Fiber is a bulking agent that does not affect colonic motility and has a **limited role in OIC**, except possibly in persons with fiber-deficient diets; soluble fiber is more effective than insoluble for constipation. Enemas are occasionally prescribed as rescue therapy but are not used as regularly as other laxative classes. There is little evidence that routine use of stimulant laxatives is harmful to the colon.

**Non-pharmacologic and opioid-directed measures (narrative, ungraded)**

- Confirm the indication for opioid therapy is appropriate, that the patient is in a pain management program (ideally with a pain specialist), and that the **minimum necessary opioid dose** is being taken.
- **Opioid switching** or changing to an **equianalgesic dose of an alternative, less-constipating opioid**. Oral or parenteral morphine preparations may induce more constipation than **transdermal opioids such as fentanyl**. Combination opioid agonist/antagonist agents (e.g. oxycodone + naloxone) are also associated with lower risk of constipation.
- **Lifestyle modifications are an appropriate first step for all those with constipation**: increasing fluid intake, regular moderate exercise as tolerated, toileting as soon as possible in response to the urge to defecate.
- History should evaluate defecation patterns, dietary patterns, stool consistency, symptoms of dyssynergic defecation (e.g. a sensation of incomplete evacuation), and alarm symptoms such as blood in stool or accompanying weight loss; medical history should assess comorbid illnesses and regular medication use. Other potential causes/contributors should be explored and excluded: **pelvic outlet dysfunction, mechanical obstruction, metabolic abnormalities, and contributions of other diseases or medications.**

**PAMORA pharmacology and efficacy**

- PAMORAs do not enter the central nervous system but block gut μ-opioid receptors, effectively restoring enteric nervous system function. **Use should be avoided in conditions that compromise the blood–brain barrier**, due to potential for serious withdrawal or reversal of anesthesia.
- **Naloxegol** — pegylated derivative of naloxone; pegylation increases oral bioavailability and enhances peripheral selectivity; also a **substrate for P-glycoprotein**, which limits CNS entry. FDA-approved **2014**, the first PAMORA for management of OIC in adult patients with chronic non-cancer pain.
- **Methylnaltrexone** — quaternary ammonium cation that cannot cross the blood–brain barrier; has opioid antagonist effects throughout the body, **reversing itching as well as constipation**; available in **oral and subcutaneous injection** forms.
- **Naldemedine** — structurally related to naltrexone; latest PAMORA approved by the FDA, in **March 2017**, for management of OIC.
- **Naldemedine evidence:** 4 randomized double-blind placebo-controlled trials — 1 phase 2b (Webster et al) and 3 phase 3 (COMPOSE 1, 2, 3) — **>2400 patients** total. Primary endpoint in 3 of them was achieving **≥3 SBM/week**: ~**52%** with naldemedine vs **35%** with placebo; pooled **RR 1.51 (95% CI 1.32–1.72)**. In COMPOSE 3 (52 weeks of follow-up), naldemedine gave roughly **1 more SBM/week** than placebo (0.95 more; 95% CI 0.57 to 1.33 more). Statistically significant improvements in straining, stool consistency, and quality of life were reported, but magnitudes were not clearly clinically meaningful. Adverse events leading to discontinuation were more common with treatment (**RR 1.44; 95% CI 1.03–2.03**) — infection, abdominal pain, diarrhea, flatulence, nausea, back pain — but the absolute increase was low (**2 more per 100 treated patients vs placebo**), below the threshold for clinically meaningful harm. Naldemedine is the **only prescription agent in this guideline with long-term (52-week) safety data**. Overall quality **high**. Patient and provider use may be limited by **cost**.
- **Naloxegol evidence:** 1 phase 2 trial (Webster et al) and 2 phase 3 double-blind RCTs (KODIAC-04 and KODIAC-05). Primary endpoints differed, so only phase 3 data were used for treatment effect. **Response = 3 SBMs/week with an increase from baseline of at least 1 SBM, for at least 3 of the 4 final weeks of the 12-week study period.** **41.9%** responded with naloxegol vs **29.4%** with placebo → **13 more patients with response per 100 (95% CI 6 to 21 more)**; **RR 1.43 (95% CI 1.19–1.71)**. In safety extension studies, most common treatment-emergent side effects were generalized or upper abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, headache, and flatulence (**RR 2.33; 95% CI 1.62–3.35**). Two deaths occurred in the safety study, **1 in each study arm**; neither was considered related to study medication. Evidence rated down for **imprecision** → moderate. **Use of naloxegol should be judicious, given the cost of this drug.**
- **Methylnaltrexone evidence:** technical review identified **5 RCTs**; only **3** examined the outcome of **≥3 rescue-free bowel movements per week** (the FDA-recommended outcome for studies of constipation agents), and only **2** studied non-cancer pain. Pooled **RR 1.43 (95% CI 1.21–1.68)** for the rescue-free bowel movement outcome — a 43% improvement, or an absolute improvement of **16 more patients per 100** achieving the outcome. Also improved "laxation response," defined generally as **a bowel movement within 4 hours of taking the medication** (**RR 3.16; 95% CI 2.18–4.58**). No statistically significant increase in adverse events leading to discontinuation. Quality **low** — rated down for indirectness, inconsistency, and imprecision; hence the conditional recommendation. Use may be limited by **high cost relative to other agents**, though the availability of a **subcutaneous** version may offer an advantage in some clinical situations.

**Lubiprostone (no recommendation, evidence gap)**

- Intestinal chloride ion secretagogues act through the guanylate cyclase C receptor with associated secretion of water into the intestinal lumen. Lubiprostone is a **bicyclic fatty acid derived from prostaglandin E1** that activates **apical membrane chloride channels** to stimulate intestinal and colonic secretion of chloride-rich fluid; it accelerates intestinal and colonic transport **without significantly impacting colonic motility or sensation**.
- **3 large phase 3 RCTs** in adults with non-cancer pain on **stable opiate doses for at least 30 days before enrollment**. **Lubiprostone 24 μg twice daily with meals and 8 ounces of fluid, for 12 weeks** in each study.
- Pooled SBM response **RR 1.15 (95% CI 0.97–1.37)**; **38%** lubiprostone vs **32.7%** placebo achieved SBM response; **0.6 to 0.8 more SBMs**. In Spierings et al, no improvement in SBM frequency, but a small reduction in straining and improvement in stool consistency of unclear clinical significance. **No meaningful changes in quality of life.**
- **6.4%** of lubiprostone patients had adverse events leading to discontinuation vs **3.0%** placebo — mostly diarrhea, nausea, abdominal pain, headache, and vomiting.
- Quality low, with concern about **selective reporting bias** across studies and **imprecision** → no recommendation, evidence gap.

**Prucalopride (no recommendation, evidence gap)**

- The **5-HT₄ receptor** is a target for GI motility disorders including IBS, chronic idiopathic constipation, functional dyspepsia, and gastroparesis; selective 5-HT receptor agonists regulate GI motility, enteric neuronal signaling, and visceral pain.
- Evidence: one **4-week phase 2, double-blind, placebo-controlled study of 196 patients** randomized to placebo, **prucalopride 2 mg, or 4 mg**, plus a **12-week clinical trial terminated early by the manufacturer** (data obtained from ClinicalTrials.gov).
- **126 of 216 (58.3%)** prucalopride patients responded vs **62 of 149 (41.6%)** placebo; pooled **RR 1.36 (95% CI 1.08–1.70)**. Mean difference in SBM frequency from baseline in a phase 2 study: **2.2 (2 mg)** and **2.5 (4 mg)** vs **1.5** placebo. Improvements in symptoms such as painful defecation or straining were not reported; a decrease in the percentage of hard stools was reported without data.
- Most common adverse events: abdominal pain and nausea. Discontinuation for adverse events in **8 of 130 (6.2%)** prucalopride patients vs **7 of 66 (10.6%)** placebo.
- Quality rated down for **suspected publication bias** (the trial "Prucalopride Effects on Subjects With Chronic Non-Caner Pain Suffering from Opioid Induced Constipation" was terminated by the manufacturer in 2014 before recruitment completed and the results were never published) and **imprecision**. Continued development of highly selective 5-HT₄ agonists, including prucalopride, in the treatment of OIC is warranted.

**Evidence gaps and research priorities named by the panel**

- Laxative therapy is widely used, but contemporary RCT-level data in OIC are relatively scarce, as are data comparing effectiveness of laxatives to newer agents, or on combining laxatives with prescription OIC medications.
- Limited published data on **long-term** use of prescription OIC medications — most trials studied 4–12-week treatment durations, despite OIC being a chronic condition.
- Additional studies are needed to establish the benefit of **lubiprostone and prucalopride** in OIC.
- There is a **paucity of comparative effectiveness studies comparing drugs to each other**, and **cost-effectiveness studies are lacking**, particularly for newer, more expensive agents.
- Pipeline agents for OIC are in development, so the topic will need to be revisited.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Lubiprostone and prucalopride: CIC ≠ OIC.** [[aga-acg-2023-constipation|AGA-ACG 2023]] gives prucalopride a **strong** recommendation (moderate certainty) and lubiprostone a **conditional** recommendation (low certainty) for chronic idiopathic constipation. This guideline makes **no recommendation** for either in OIC and calls both an evidence gap. Not a true contradiction — the 2023 guideline explicitly **excluded** opioid-induced constipation from its population — but the two statements sit close enough together that the population qualifier is the decision. The newer 2023 recommendations stand unchanged on [[chronic-idiopathic-constipation]]; the OIC-specific "no recommendation" is stated on [[opioid-induced-constipation]].
- **Rome version.** This guideline quotes the **Rome IV** definition of OIC. [[rome-v-2026-dgbi|Rome V]] (2026) is newer and places opioid-induced constipation in its own bowel-DGBI category **C6**, but the Rome V process paper prints the taxonomy without the numbered symptom items. The Rome IV items reproduced here are therefore the operative symptom criteria on the wiki, with the C6 categorisation taken from Rome V.
- **Diagnostic evaluation is out of scope.** The guideline and its technical review explicitly do not address the diagnostic evaluation of OIC, so its diagnostic content is narrative and ungraded. The broader workup (anorectal testing, transit testing, alarm features) comes from [[chronic-constipation]] and [[aga-2026-refractory-constipation]].
- **Combination opioid agonist/antagonists** (e.g. oxycodone + naloxone) are mentioned as lower-constipation options but were **not addressed** by the technical review or any recommendation statement, so no graded position exists.
- **Figure 1 — clinical decision support tool.** The text refers readers to an accompanying clinical decision support tool (Figure 1); the tool is not reproduced within the article pages, so the treatment sequence on [[opioid-induced-constipation]] is built from the printed recommendations and narrative rather than from the figure.
- **Internal labelling inconsistencies** in the published article: the boxed lubiprostone statement is numbered "3." while Table 3 numbers it 3a.; the boxed statement 2c spells the drug "methylnatrexone" while Table 3 and the text use methylnaltrexone. Neither affects content.
