## Summary

This ACG guideline gives a framework for evaluating abnormal liver chemistries. It insists on the terminology: alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin are **liver chemistries or liver tests**, not liver function tests — they are markers of liver injury, not of hepatic function. True measures of function (albumin, prothrombin time [PT], bilirubin) are separate. ALT is present primarily in the liver and is therefore a more specific marker of hepatocellular injury than AST, which is also found in cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, kidney and brain; AST increase without ALT elevation suggests cardiac or muscle disease.

A central contribution is a **lower upper limit of normal (ULN) for ALT**: in prospectively studied populations without identifiable risk factors for liver disease, a true healthy normal ALT ranges from **29–33 IU/l for males and 19–25 IU/l for females**, and levels above this should be assessed. Laboratory ULN values are higher largely because their reference populations were never screened for non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), alcohol use, viral hepatitis, or body mass index (BMI) — and ALT rises linearly with BMI. A normal ALT does not exclude significant liver disease. The guideline grades AST/ALT elevation as borderline, mild, moderate, severe and massive, and pairs each tier with a defined panel (algorithm home: [[abnormal-liver-chemistries]]).

For an elevated alkaline phosphatase, hepatic origin is confirmed with gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) or fractionation, followed by right upper quadrant (RUQ) ultrasound; a dilated biliary system points extrahepatic, a non-dilated one intrahepatic. Intrahepatic cholestasis is then worked up with anti-mitochondrial antibody (AMA), anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) and anti-smooth antibody (ASMA) for [[primary-biliary-cholangitis|primary biliary cholangitis (PBC)]], [[primary-sclerosing-cholangitis|primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)]] and [[autoimmune-hepatitis|autoimmune hepatitis]]. Elevated total bilirubin must be fractionated: elevated conjugated (direct) bilirubin implies hepatocellular disease or biliary obstruction in most settings; unconjugated (indirect) elevation points to Gilbert's syndrome, hemolysis, or ineffective erythropoiesis.

The guideline then walks the specific diseases behind abnormal chemistries — [[chronic-hepatitis-b|chronic hepatitis B]] and [[hepatitis-c|chronic hepatitis C]], acute hepatitis A/B/C/E, [[nafld-masld|NAFLD]] and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), [[alcohol-associated-liver-disease|alcoholic liver disease]], autoimmune liver disease, [[hereditary-hemochromatosis|hereditary hemochromatosis]], [[wilson-disease|Wilson's disease]], alpha-1 anti-trypsin (A1AT) deficiency, [[drug-induced-liver-injury|drug and supplement-induced liver injury (DILI)]], PBC and PSC — and offers five algorithms (Figures 1–5) plus cause tables for elevated AST/ALT (Table 4), alkaline phosphatase (Table 5) and bilirubin (Table 6). **All 19 recommendations carry the same grade: strong recommendation, very low level of evidence.**

## Key Findings / Claims

### Truly normal ALT

- True healthy normal ALT: **29–33 IU/l (males), 19–25 IU/l (females)** — from prospectively studied populations without identifiable risk factors
- Laboratory ULNs are wide and inconsistent: the National Institutes of Health NASH Clinical Research Network found a defined ALT ULN range of **35–79 IU/l for men and 31–55 IU/l for women** across its labs; one state survey of 67 reference laboratories found ALT ULN from **31 to 72 IU/l**
- Published proposals (Table 2): Prati 30/19 IU/l · Lee 33/25 IU/l (all with normal liver biopsies) · Ruhl and Everhart 29/22 IU/l (from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey [NHANES]) · Wright 33 IU/l · Neuschwander-Tetri 40 IU/l
- Mortality signal (Table 3): ALT above ULN (defined 30 IU/l men, 19 IU/l women) in NHANES was associated with an **11.2× increase in liver-related mortality** and a **3.3× increase in diabetes-related mortality**; ALT 30–39 carried relative risk 2.9 (liver mortality) and 9.5 (in men, 3.8–7.7 range reported for women); AST >18 U/l carried a **3× increase in all-cause mortality**
- ALT is higher in males than females; ALT rises linearly with BMI; a normal ALT may not exclude significant liver disease
- Applying the 29/22 IU/l cut-points to NHANES would reclassify **36% of men and 28% of women** as having an elevated ALT
- Clinicians may rely on local laboratory ULN ranges for alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin (unlike ALT/AST)

### Patterns and the R ratio

- **Hepatocellular injury** — disproportionate elevation of AST and ALT vs alkaline phosphatase
- **Cholestatic injury** — disproportionate elevation of alkaline phosphatase vs AST and ALT
- **Mixed** — elevation of both; **isolated hyperbilirubinemia** — bilirubin up with normal alkaline phosphatase and AST/ALT
- **R ratio = (ALT value ÷ ALT ULN) ÷ (alkaline phosphatase value ÷ alkaline phosphatase ULN)** — R **>5** hepatocellular, **<2** cholestatic, **2–5** mixed
- AST:ALT — ALT > AST in most liver conditions including chronic viral hepatitis and NAFLD; **~90% of alcoholic liver disease patients have AST > ALT and >70% have an AST/ALT ratio >2**
- AST > ALT is also seen in [[cirrhosis]] of any etiology, but there the ratio is typically **not >2:1** (one hepatitis C virus [HCV] study: ratio 0.60 in non-cirrhotics vs mean 1.05 in cirrhotics)

### Magnitude tiers (definitions)

| Tier | AST and/or ALT |
|---|---|
| Borderline | <2× ULN |
| Mild | 2–5× ULN |
| Moderate | 5–15× ULN |
| Severe | >15× ULN |
| Massive | >10,000 IU/l |

- Tier-by-tier workup panels and Figures 1–3 live on [[abnormal-liver-chemistries]]
- **Fulminant hepatic failure / [[acute-liver-failure|acute liver failure]]** — rapid development of acute liver injury with severe impairment of synthetic function (prolonged PT plus hepatic encephalopathy) in a patient *without obvious, previous liver disease* → **immediate evaluation regardless of ALT level**
- Massive elevation (>10,000 U/l) is generally only seen with shock liver/ischemic hepatopathy, drug-induced or toxic hepatitis

### Bilirubin

- Total serum bilirubin is usually **<1.1 mg/dl**; unconjugated bilirubin accounts for **~70%** of the total
- Fractionation is most useful when ALT, AST and alkaline phosphatase are normal or near normal
- **Gilbert's syndrome** — most common cause of elevated unconjugated bilirubin, affecting **3–7% of the US population**; genetic defect of uridine 5′-diphospho-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT1A1); total bilirubin **almost never exceeds 6 mg/dl and is usually <3 mg/dl**. In asymptomatic healthy individuals with mild unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia (**<4 mg/dl**), exclude causative medications and hemolysis and confirm normal transaminases and alkaline phosphatase → presumptive diagnosis, additional evaluation not routinely necessary
- Fasting or significant illness can increase unconjugated bilirubin **2- to 3-fold**; it falls with eating or phenobarbital
- Hemolysis infrequently causes bilirubin **>5 mg/dl** unless renal disease, liver disease or severe acute hemolysis coexists
- Conjugated hyperbilirubinemia implies parenchymal liver damage or biliary obstruction; total bilirubin may exceed **30 mg/dl** in severe injury
- **Dubin–Johnson and Rotor syndromes** — rare inherited direct hyperbilirubinemias with direct bilirubin ~50% of total and otherwise normal liver tests including alkaline phosphatase and GGT; **it is not necessary to distinguish the two**
- **Delta bilirubin** — conjugated bilirubin bound to albumin; explains prolonged [[jaundice]] in recovery because its decay tracks the **3-week** albumin half-life

### Alkaline phosphatase and GGT

- Alkaline phosphatase originates in hepatocytes (canalicular membrane, not the bile duct cell), bone, placenta, intestine and kidney; physiologically higher in children during rapid bone growth and in pregnancy (placental synthesis); may rise after a fatty meal in blood types O and B
- **GGT is not specific for cholestatic liver disease** — elevated in **>50% of alcoholic patients without obvious liver disease**, and in **pancreatic disease, myocardial infarction, renal failure, emphysema, diabetes**, and with **phenytoin and barbiturates**
- ~80% of isolated alkaline phosphatase elevations can be diagnosed on history, physical examination, routine labs and chest X-ray alone (one hospitalized-patient study)
- Figure 4 decision points — **normal bilirubin and transaminases** arm: confirm with GGT → GGT normal, evaluate non-hepatobiliary causes; GGT abnormal, obtain RUQ ultrasound, review hepatotoxic medications, check AMA/ANA/SMA
  - Evaluation negative and alkaline phosphatase **>2× ULN** → consider liver biopsy; **1–2× ULN** → consider observation
  - Ductal dilatation → [[ercp|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]] or magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)
  - AMA positive → evaluate for PBC
  - Persistent elevation after **6 months** of observation → consider liver biopsy
- Figure 4 — **elevated transaminases ± elevated bilirubin** arm: RUQ ultrasound first; ductal dilatation → ERCP/MRCP; no dilatation → AMA, ANA, SMA. AMA negative with alkaline phosphatase >2× ULN → consider liver biopsy or MRCP; 1–2× ULN → observe; persistence at 6 months → liver biopsy or MRCP

### Specific diseases

- **Hepatitis C** — ~4.1 million Americans antibody-positive, ~3.0 million with chronic infection. Anti-HCV sensitivity **92–97%**; false positives in ~5% overall and up to **30%** of those without reported risk factors, so confirm with HCV RNA by nucleic acid testing. Anti-HCV typically turns positive **6–8 weeks** after exposure; acute hepatitis C requires anti-HCV **plus** HCV RNA
- **Hepatitis B** — chronic infection in ~1.5 million Americans and >280 million worldwide. Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) identifies infection; hepatitis B core antibody (total) indicates prior exposure; hepatitis B surface antibody indicates immunity. Acute hepatitis B = immunoglobulin M (IgM) hepatitis B core antibody **plus** HBsAg. Further characterization: hepatitis B e antigen, hepatitis B e antibody, genotype, quantitative viral load, fibrosis assessment
- **NAFLD** — strongly considered with mild AST/ALT elevation plus obesity, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, hypertension. **No unique pattern**; ALT generally higher than AST; levels **rarely above 300 IU/l**. Liver biopsy is required to establish NASH; it is not practical to biopsy all, so guidelines exist to select. Vibration-controlled transient elastography (FibroScan, Echosens) was recently approved by the Food and Drug Administration and may help identify advanced fibrosis
- **Alcoholic liver disease** — significant intake suggested as **>210 g/week in men, >140 g/week in women**. AST:ALT ratio of at least **2:1**, with AST or ALT rarely exceeding **300 IU/l**; a ratio **>3:1** further increases likelihood, reflecting lower ALT activity from pyridoxine deficiency. GGT is complementary but not by itself helpful for diagnosis. Complete cessation should be recommended in all
- **Autoimmune liver disease** — may occur with other autoimmune disorders (hypothyroidism, [[ulcerative-colitis|ulcerative colitis]], synovitis, Sjögren's, rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis). **Systemic lupus erythematosus is often mentioned in association with autoimmune hepatitis, but the association is not strong.** Female predominance **4:1**; lower prevalence than viral hepatitis or alcoholic/NAFLD (**1:6,000**). Serologic markers: ANA, anti-smooth antibody, and less commonly anti-liver kidney microsomal antibody or anti-soluble liver antigen antibody. Hypergammaglobulinemia on immunoglobulin G (IgG) or serum protein electrophoresis is suggestive but not diagnostic. **Diagnosis requires histologic confirmation on liver biopsy**, which may confirm the diagnosis, exclude an overlap syndrome, and stage fibrosis
- **Hereditary hemochromatosis** — HFE homozygous C282Y prevalence **1 in 220–250**; C282Y heterozygote prevalence **1 in 10**. Penetrance of C282Y homozygosity is quite low. Screen with an **iron panel from which transferrin saturation and serum ferritin can be determined**; if transferrin saturation is **≥45%** or ferritin is elevated, consider HFE gene mutation analysis. Ferritin is an acute phase reactant — elevation with an acute hepatitis likely does not mean iron overload. Homozygous C282Y confirms HFE-related disease; compound C282Y/H63D heterozygotes rarely manifest chronic liver disease from iron overload. **Liver biopsy should be considered in homozygous C282Y or compound heterozygous C282Y/H63D patients with elevated AST/ALT levels or ferritin >1,000 µg/l, or in non-HFE hemochromatosis** — to stage liver fibrosis and quantify hepatic iron overload
- **Wilson's disease** — rare autosomal recessive defect of the ATP7B copper transporter, ~**1:30,000**; most commonly diagnosed in young males but can present at any age; hepatic, neurologic and/or psychiatric presentations. Screen persistently elevated AST/ALT with **serum ceruloplasmin**; if low, pursue **24-h urine copper and/or serum copper, slit-lamp examination for Kayser–Fleischer rings**, and possible liver biopsy to confirm the diagnosis, stage fibrosis, and quantify copper overload. **Over 32 different mutations** identified with **no predominant mutation**; molecular ATP7B analysis for diagnostic uncertainty
- **Alpha-1 anti-trypsin deficiency** — rare in adults but the **most common genetic cause of liver disease in children**, prevalence **1:2,500** in North American Caucasians. Defective production may cause panacinar emphysema and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease as well as progressive liver disease, cirrhosis and [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|hepatocellular carcinoma]]. Test with **low quantitative alpha-1 anti-trypsin and genotype for the PiZZ mutation** (severe enzyme deficiency); heterozygotes (most commonly PiMZ) may also be at risk, particularly with concomitant steatosis or viral hepatitis
- **DILI** — nearly all medications carry at least a small risk of elevated ALT/AST/alkaline phosphatase or bilirubin. Common classes: antibiotics, antiepileptics, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents, HMG-Co-A-reductase inhibitors (statins), anti-tuberculosis drugs, anti-retroviral treatment for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), biologic agents such as anti-tumor necrosis factor drugs, some cancer chemotherapeutics. **Statins have an extensive safety literature in chronic liver disease with only rare hepatotoxicity.** With ALT **>1,000 IU/l** an accurate acetaminophen history (single agent or analgesic-narcotic combination) is essential. Herbal supplements associated with hepatotoxicity: **chaparral, ephedra, ji bu huan, germander, green tea extract, shark cartilage**. Reference resource: livertox.nih.gov
- **PBC** — uncommon chronic liver disease **primarily affecting intralobular bile ducts at the microscopic level**; more common in women; fatigue and pruritus. Biochemical cholestasis (elevated alkaline phosphatase ± bilirubin) plus **AMA positive in over 95%**. Liver biopsy is **not routinely required** but may benefit AMA-negative disease and stage fibrosis
- **PSC** — chronic immune-mediated inflammation and fibrosis of intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts, fixed multifocal strictures, progressive fibrosis and cirrhosis, with increased risk of **both hepatocellular carcinoma and cholangiocarcinoma**. **No serologic hallmark**; IgG and anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody are frequently elevated, and IgG4 may signal a specific IgG4-associated form. Diagnosis rests on **characteristic imaging** — thickened, inflamed bile ducts, intrahepatic ductal dilatation and focal strictures on MR cholangiography or ERCP. Periductal concentric or "onion-skin" fibrosis is characteristic histologically but uncommonly seen, so biopsy is rarely required. **Common association with inflammatory bowel disease of the colon.** Chronic cholestatic liver injury without PBC or PSC should prompt investigation for bile duct obstruction, medications, sepsis, total parenteral nutrition, vanishing bile duct syndrome and sarcoidosis

### Rare and non-hepatic causes

- **[[celiac-disease|Celiac disease]]** — may cause modest transaminase elevation; screen persistently elevated liver chemistries with **tissue transglutaminase IgA plus serum IgA level**, or tissue transglutaminase IgA plus anti-deamidated gliadin peptide IgG
- **Muscle disorders** — inborn or acquired muscle disease, muscle trauma, strenuous sport/exercise; AST-predominant. Screen **after commoner etiologies are excluded**, initially with creatine kinase and/or aldolase
- **Thyroid disease** — both hypo- and hyperthyroidism cause hepatocellular and cholestatic patterns, particularly in myxedema or thyrotoxicosis; screen with thyroid stimulating hormone and selective free T4 and free/total T3
- **Pregnancy** — trimester-specific consideration of cholelithiasis, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, acute fatty liver of pregnancy, pre-eclampsia, and exacerbation of other chronic liver disease; biopsy rarely required; early delivery may be needed in severe cases
- **Tick-borne illness** — Lyme disease, babesiosis, ehrlichiosis in endemic US regions, usually hepatocellular; test *Borrelia burgdorferi*, ehrlichiosis (human granulocytic anaplasmosis or human monocyte forms), babesiosis
- Non-hepatic causes of elevated AST (Table 4): skeletal muscle damage/rhabdomyolysis, cardiac muscle damage, thyroid disease, **macro-AST**, strenuous exercise, heat stroke, hemolysis, adrenal insufficiency
- Other AST>ALT hepatic causes (Table 4): alcoholic liver disease, cirrhosis of any etiology, ischemic hepatitis, congestive hepatopathy, acute [[budd-chiari-syndrome|Budd–Chiari syndrome]], hepatic artery damage/thrombosis/occlusion, total parenteral nutrition
- A hypercoagulable state with abnormal liver tests and ascites should suggest Budd–Chiari syndrome; recent chemotherapy for hematologic malignancy suggests sinusoidal obstruction syndrome

### Liver biopsy

- **Most commonly done to assess grade and stage of disease severity** — hepatitis B, hepatitis C, NAFLD
- May also **confirm a suspected specific diagnosis**: Wilson's disease (quantitative copper), hemochromatosis (hepatic iron index), alpha-1 anti-trypsin deficiency (periodic acid–Schiff-positive globules)

## Recommendations

**All 19 recommendations in Table 1 carry the identical grade printed by the guideline: *Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence*** (recommendation 17 is printed with a lowercase "strong"). Verbatim below, numbered as published.

1. Before initiation of evaluation of abnormal liver chemistries, one should repeat the lab panel and/or perform a clarifying test (e.g., GGT if serum alkaline phosphate is elevated) to confirm that the liver chemistry is actually abnormal. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
2. Testing for chronic hepatitis C is conducted with anti-HCV and confirmation is performed with HCV-RNA by nucleic acid testing. Risk factors for hepatitis C include history of intranasal or intravenous drug use, tattoos, body piercings, blood transfusions, high risk sexual conduct, and those born between 1945 and 1965. Testing for acute hepatitis C is with anti-HCV and HCV RNA by nucleic acid testing. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
3. Testing for chronic hepatitis B is conducted with HBsAg testing. Testing for acute hepatitis B is with HBsAg and IgM anti-HBc. The following groups are at highest risk: persons born in endemic or hyperendemic areas (HBsAg prevalence >2%), men who have sex with men, persons who have ever used injection drugs, dialysis patients, HIV-infected individuals, pregnant women, and family members, household members, and sexual contacts of HBV-infected persons. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
4. Testing for acute Hepatitis A (IgM HAV) should occur in patients presenting with acute hepatitis and possible fecal-oral exposure. Testing for acute hepatitis E (IgM HEV) should also be considered in those returning from endemic areas and whose tests for acute hepatitis A, B, and C are negative. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
5. Patients with elevated BMI and other features of metabolic syndrome including diabetes mellitus, overweight or obesity, hyperlipidemia, or hypertension with mild elevations of ALT should undergo screening for NAFLD with ultrasound. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
6. Women consuming more than 140 g per week or men consuming more than 210 g per week who present with AST>ALT should be considered at risk for alcoholic liver disease and should be counseled for alcohol cessation. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
7. All patients with abnormal liver chemistries in the absence of acute hepatitis should undergo testing for hereditary hemochromatosis with an iron level, transferrin saturation, and serum ferritin. HFE gene mutation analysis should be performed in patients with transferrin saturation ≥45% and/or elevated serum ferritin. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
8. Patients with abnormal AST and ALT levels, particularly patients with other autoimmune conditions, should undergo testing for autoimmune liver disease including ANA, ASMA, and globulin level. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
9. Patients with persistently elevated AST and ALT levels, especially patients <55 years of age, should undergo screening for Wilson's disease with serum ceruloplasmin testing. In the setting of low ceruloplasmin, confirmatory testing with 24-h urinary copper and slit-lamp eye examination to identify pathognomonic Kayser–Fleischer rings should occur. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
10. Patients with persistently elevated AST or ALT should undergo screening for alpha-1 anti-trypsin (A1AT) deficiency with alpha-1 anti-trypsin phenotype. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
11. Physicians should ask patients with abnormal liver chemistries about prescribed and over-the-counter medications, non-prescribed complementary or alternative medicines, and dietary or herbal supplements which may be associated with DILI. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
12. A liver biopsy may be considered when serologic testing and imaging fails to elucidate a diagnosis, to stage a condition, or when multiple diagnoses are possible. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
13. An elevation of alkaline phosphatase should be confirmed with an elevation in GGT. Given its lack of specificity for liver disease, GGT should not be used as a screening test for underlying liver disease in the absence of other abnormal liver chemistries. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
14. Patients with alkaline phosphatase elevation with or without elevation of bilirubin should undergo testing for PBC (formerly named primary biliary cirrhosis) with testing for anti-mitochondrial antibody. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
15. Patients with alkaline phosphatase elevation with or without elevation of bilirubin should undergo testing for PSC with MR cholangiography or ERCP in conjunction with IgG4. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
16. In those with ALT and/or AST levels <5X ULN, the history and laboratory testing should assess for viral hepatitis B and C, alcoholic and NAFLD, hemochromatosis, Wilson's disease, alpha-1-anti-trypsin deficiency, autoimmune hepatitis and consider drugs/supplement related injury. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
17. In those with ALT and/or AST levels 5–15X ULN, evaluation should also assess for acute hepatitis A, B, and C in addition to all etiologies for AST/ALT elevation less than 5x ULN. *(strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
18. In those with ALT and/or AST levels >15X ULN, or massive elevation ALT of >10,000 IU/l, evaluation should also assess for acetaminophen toxicity and ischemic hepatopathy (shock liver). *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*
19. A patient presenting with acute hepatitis with an elevated prothrombin time, and/or encephalopathy requires immediate referral to liver specialist. *(Strong recommendation, very low level of evidence).*

## Summary Statements (ungraded)

The guideline carries a second, separate series of **Summary statements**. These are numbered per section and are printed **without any strength or evidence grade** — they are not recommendations.

**Introduction — what liver chemistries are**

1. Liver chemistries including ALT, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin are markers of liver injury, not liver function, and should be referred to as liver chemistries, or liver tests.
2. Albumin, bilirubin, and prothrombin time are markers of hepatocellular function that can be influenced by extrahepatic factors.
3. The laboratory measurements of ALT, AST, and alkaline phosphatase are highly reproducible.
4. Elevations of AST and/or ALT, alkaline phosphatase, and bilirubin suggest hepatocellular injury and are the abnormal liver chemistries that require assessment and potential evaluation.
5. ALT is a more specific marker of hepatic injury than AST.
6. An elevated alkaline phosphatase level of hepatic origin may be confirmed by elevation of gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) or fractionation of alkaline phosphatase.

**What are truly normal liver chemistry tests?**

1. A true healthy normal ALT level in prospectively studied populations without identifiable risk factors for liver disease ranges from 29 to 33 IU/l for males and 19 to 25 IU/l for females, and levels above this should be assessed by physicians.
2. Elevated ALT or AST above the upper limit of normal (ULN) in a population without identifiable risk factors is associated with increased liver-related mortality.
3. There is a linear relationship between ALT level and body mass index (BMI) that should be assessed by physicians.
4. A normal ALT level may not exclude significant liver disease.
5. ALT levels are higher in males than females.
6. AST and ALT ULN ranges can vary between different labs.
7. Clinicians may rely on local lab ULN ranges for alkaline phosphatase and bilirubin.

**Clinical assessment of the patient with abnormal liver chemistries**

*Lead-in as printed: "Clinical assessment of the patient with elevated liver tests should begin with a thorough history and physical examination."*

1. History should include risk factors for underlying liver disease, associated medical conditions, use of alcohol, and use of medications including over-the-counter products and herbal supplements.
2. Physical examination should assess for stigmata of chronic liver disease, as well as signs or symptoms pointing to a specific liver disease etiology.

**Patterns of liver chemistry test elevations**

1. Hepatocellular injury is defined as disproportionate elevation of AST and ALT levels as compared with the alkaline phosphatase level.
2. Cholestatic injury is defined as disproportionate elevation in alkaline phosphatase level as compared with AST and ALT levels.
3. Mixed pattern of injury is defined as elevation of both alkaline phosphatase and AST/ALT levels.
4. Isolated hyperbilirubinemia is defined as elevation of bilirubin with normal alkaline phosphatase and AST/ALT levels.
5. The R ratio is calculated by the formula R=(ALT value÷ALT ULN)÷(alkaline phosphatase value÷alkaline phosphatase ULN) with an R ratio of >5 defined as hepatocellular injury, <2 cholestatic injury, and 2–5 mixed pattern.

**Approach to evaluation for those with elevated AST and ALT**

1. (Table 4 and Figures 1–3) A borderline AST and/or ALT elevation is defined as <2X ULN, a mild AST and/or ALT elevation as 2–5X ULN, moderate AST and/or ALT elevation 5–15X ULN, severe AST and/or ALT elevation >15X ULN, and massive AST and/or ALT >10,000 IU/l.
2. Fulminant hepatic failure or acute liver failure, defined as the rapid development of acute liver injury with severe impairment of the synthetic function as manifested by prolonged prothrombin time and hepatic encephalopathy in a patient without obvious, previous liver disease requires immediate evaluation regardless of ALT level.

**Elevation of total bilirubin level**

1. (Table 6 and Figure 5) Elevated serum total bilirubin levels should be fractionated to direct and indirect bilirubin.
2. An elevated serum conjugated bilirubin implies hepatocellular disease or biliary obstruction in most settings.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Terminology.** This 2017 document uses "NAFLD/NASH" and refers to PBC as "formerly named primary biliary cirrhosis" (Tables 5 and 6 still print "primary biliary cirrhosis"). Current wiki nomenclature is MASLD/MASH and primary biliary cholangitis.
- **ALT ULN.** The guideline asserts 29–33 IU/l (men) and 19–25 IU/l (women) as the true healthy normal, while acknowledging local laboratory ULNs run 35–79 IU/l (men) and 31–55 IU/l (women). It also records the counter-argument: lowering the ULN increases costs, evaluations, anxiety, and shrinks the blood donor pool, and in one study of 235 asymptomatic workers 27% had abnormal liver tests by lower criteria but only six were found to have liver disease on further evaluation. The authors' stated belief is that clinicians should be educated about these historically non-elevated levels and that a national standardization effort should be undertaken.
- **HCV screening.** The screening advice here is birth-cohort based (born 1945–1965), attributed to the Centers for Disease Control and United States Preventive Services Task Force as of 2017. Any broader age-based screening is outside this document.
- **Alkaline phosphatase confirmation.** Recommendation 13 requires GGT confirmation, while the body text notes that when alkaline phosphatase is elevated *alongside other abnormal liver chemistries*, confirmation of hepatic origin is not required — the confirmation step applies to isolated elevation.
- **Immune checkpoint inhibitor hepatotoxicity.** Not addressed anywhere in this document; see [[immune-checkpoint-inhibitor-hepatitis]] for the wiki's coverage from other sources.
- **Alpha-1 anti-trypsin deficiency.** Recommendation 10 specifies the *phenotype*, while the body text describes low quantitative alpha-1 anti-trypsin plus *genotype* for PiZZ. No dedicated wiki page exists for this entity yet.
