## Summary

This is the AGA's official position on the role of **vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE)** — and, in two questions, **magnetic resonance elastography (MRE)** — in staging liver fibrosis in adults with chronic liver disease. The guideline panel framed **14 numbered focused questions** across four etiologies (chronic hepatitis C, chronic hepatitis B, NAFLD, chronic alcoholic liver disease) plus two portal-hypertension applications (ruling out high-risk esophageal varices; ruling out clinically significant portal hypertension before elective nonhepatic surgery). The comparators are the two nonproprietary serum indices, **APRI** and **FIB-4**; proprietary serum fibrosis assays, other imaging elastography techniques (pSWE/ARFI, 2D-SWE), and combinations of more than one noninvasive test were explicitly **not** addressed.

The guideline's distinctive contribution is that it commits to a **single kPa cutoff per etiology**, and states openly why: for each question the panel deliberately chose the threshold that **minimizes false negatives**, judging that the harm of missing cirrhosis exceeds the harm of overdiagnosis. The consequence — a substantial number of patients falsely labeled high-risk, especially in low-prevalence settings — is accepted and quantified question by question. The panel repeatedly cautions that a cutoff should not be used in isolation: for hepatitis C it states that "caution should be exercised in solely utilizing a cutoff of 12.5 kPa to diagnose cirrhosis, and the result of VCTE should be considered in context of other clinical information to guide management," and for both hepatitis B and C it warns that **downstream invasive decisions (e.g. screening endoscopy for varices) should not be based solely on the cirrhosis cutoff**.

Evidence quality is low throughout. Almost every cutoff recommendation is **conditional, low- or very-low-quality evidence**, rated down for **indirectness** (the cutoffs come from post-hoc analysis of cross-sectional diagnostic-accuracy studies rather than from studies comparing cutoffs against downstream patient-important outcomes) and for **significant heterogeneity**. Only one recommendation is strong: VCTE over APRI/FIB-4 to detect cirrhosis in chronic hepatitis C (moderate quality). **NAFLD is where the guideline declines to act** — the panel found pooled VCTE/serum-test estimates in NAFLD subject to unacceptable bias (exclusion of obese patients with BMI ≥30 kg/m², exclusion of unsuccessful/inadequate liver-stiffness measurements which are more common in NAFLD, and per-protocol rather than intention-to-diagnose analysis) and issued **"no recommendation — knowledge gap"** for the role of VCTE in diagnosing cirrhosis in NAFLD.

MRE is compared head-to-head with VCTE in only two settings. In **hepatitis C**, VCTE is preferred over MRE — MRE added little or no accuracy for identifying true cirrhosis but had poorer specificity and higher false-positive rates, and VCTE is cheaper and available at the point of care. In **NAFLD**, the direction reverses but only for high-risk patients: MRE is suggested over VCTE where cirrhosis prevalence is high (~30%, referral setting), because MRE would yield **119 fewer per 1000 patients incorrectly labeled as having cirrhosis**; in low-risk NAFLD the panel again makes no recommendation, noting both tests perform poorly there (2.5-fold [MRE] to 6-fold [VCTE] more false positives than true positives).

## Key Findings / Claims

### How the recommendations are labeled in the original

**The document numbers its 14 focused Questions; it does not number its recommendations.** Each boxed "Recommendation" is tied to the question(s) above it, carries a "GRADE:" line, and some carry a "Comment:" paragraph. The labels below (*Q1–Q2*, *Q3*, …) are the document's own question numbers, used here to identify each box. There are **11 recommendation boxes containing 12 recommendation statements** — 10 actionable statements and 2 explicit "no recommendation — knowledge gap" statements.

### Recommendations (verbatim / near-verbatim, with GRADE as printed)

**Q1–Q2** — *Should VCTE vs aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index (APRI) be used to diagnose cirrhosis in adults with chronic hepatitis C? / Should VCTE vs fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) be used to diagnose cirrhosis in adults with chronic hepatitis C?*
> **Recommendation:** In patients with chronic hepatitis C, the AGA recommends VCTE, if available, rather than other nonproprietary, noninvasive serum tests (APRI, FIB-4) to detect cirrhosis.
> **GRADE:** *Strong recommendation, moderate quality evidence.*

**Q3** — *In adults with hepatitis C virus (HCV) undergoing VCTE, at what liver stiffness cutoff can we accurately diagnose cirrhosis (and initiate downstream management), obviating the need for liver biopsy?*
> **Recommendation:** In patients with chronic hepatitis C, the AGA suggests a VCTE cutoff of 12.5 kPa to detect cirrhosis.
> **GRADE:** *Conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence.*

**Q4** — *In adults with HCV who have achieved sustained virologic response (SVR) with antiviral therapy undergoing VCTE, at what liver stiffness cutoff can we accurately rule out advanced fibrosis and consider discharging patients from a dedicated liver clinic?*
> **Recommendation:** In noncirrhotic patients with HCV who have achieved SVR after antiviral therapy, the AGA suggests a post-treatment vibration controlled transient elastography cutoff of 9.5 kPa to rule out advanced liver fibrosis.
> **GRADE:** *Conditional recommendation, very-low-quality evidence.*
> **Comment:** *Noncirrhotic patients with VCTE <9.5 kPa who place a low value on the inconvenience and risks of continued laboratory and fibrosis testing, and a high value on avoiding the small risk of developing HCC, may reasonably select to continue specialty care rather than being discharged from the specialty clinic.*

**Q5–Q6** — *Should VCTE vs APRI be used to diagnose cirrhosis in adults with chronic hepatitis B? / Should VCTE vs FIB-4 be used to diagnose cirrhosis in adults with chronic hepatitis B?*
> **Recommendation:** In patients with chronic hepatitis B, the AGA suggests VCTE rather than other nonproprietary noninvasive serum tests (ie, APRI and FIB-4) to detect cirrhosis.
> **GRADE:** *Conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence.*

**Q7** — *In adults with HBV undergoing VCTE, at what liver stiffness cutoff can we accurately diagnose cirrhosis, obviating the need for liver biopsy?*
> **Recommendation:** In patients with chronic hepatitis B, the AGA suggests a VCTE cutoff of 11.0 kPa to detect cirrhosis.
> **GRADE:** *Conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence.*

**Q8–Q9** — *Should VCTE vs APRI be used to diagnose cirrhosis in adults with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)? / Should VCTE vs FIB-4 be used to diagnosed cirrhosis in adults with NAFLD?*
> **Recommendation:** The AGA makes no recommendation regarding the role of VCTE in the diagnosis of cirrhosis in adults with NAFLD.
> **GRADE:** *no recommendation — knowledge gap.*

**Q10** — *In adults with chronic alcoholic liver disease undergoing VCTE, at what liver stiffness cutoff can we accurately diagnose cirrhosis, obviating the need for liver biopsy?*
> **Recommendation:** In patients with chronic alcoholic liver disease, the AGA suggests a VCTE cutoff of 12.5 kPa to detect cirrhosis.
> **GRADE:** *Conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence.*

**Q11** — *In adults with suspected compensated cirrhosis undergoing VCTE, at what liver stiffness cutoff can we accurately rule out high-risk esophageal varices, obviating the need for routine endoscopic screening?*
> **Recommendation:** In patients with suspected compensated cirrhosis, the AGA suggests a vibration controlled transient elastography cutoff of 19.5 kPa to assess the need for esophagogastroduodenoscopy to identify high risk esophageal varices.
> **GRADE:** *Conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence.*
> **Comment:** *Patients, particularly those at higher risk, with VCTE <19.5 kPa who place a low value on the inconvenience and risks of endoscopy, and a high value on avoiding the small risk of acute variceal hemorrhage associated with VCTE values of <19.5 kPa, may reasonably select to undergo screening endoscopy.*

**Q12** — *In adults with suspected chronic liver disease undergoing elective nonhepatic surgery, at what VCTE-identified liver stiffness cutoff can we accurately rule out clinically significant portal hypertension (identified as presence of any esophageal varices), potentially minimizing the need for routine invasive testing for portal hypertension (endoscopy, hepatic venous pressure gradient measurement)?*
> **Recommendation:** In patients with suspected chronic liver disease undergoing elective nonhepatic surgery, the AGA suggests a VCTE cutoff of 17.0 kPa to detect clinically significant portal hypertension to inform preoperative care.
> **GRADE:** *Conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence.*
> **Comment:** *Patients, particularly those at higher risk, with VCTE <17.0 kPa who place a low value on the inconvenience and risks of interventions (endoscopy, hepatic venous pressure gradient measurement) to detect clinically significant portal hypertension, and a high value on avoiding the small risk of operative morbidity and mortality associated with elective nonhepatic surgery, may reasonably select to undergo screening endoscopy.*

**Q13** — *Should MRE vs VCTE be used to diagnose cirrhosis in adults with chronic hepatitis C?*
> **Recommendation:** In adult patients with chronic hepatitis C, the AGA suggests using VCTE rather than MRE for detection of cirrhosis.
> **GRADE:** *Conditional recommendation, very-low-quality evidence.*

**Q14** — *Should MRE vs VCTE be used to diagnose cirrhosis in adults with NAFLD?* (one box, two statements)
> **Recommendation:** In adults with NAFLD and a higher risk of cirrhosis, the AGA suggest using MRE, rather than VCTE, for detection of cirrhosis
> **GRADE:** *Conditional recommendation, low quality evidence.*
>
> In adults with NAFLD and a lower risk of cirrhosis, the AGA makes no recommendation regarding the role of MRE or VCTE for detection of cirrhosis.
> **GRADE:** *no recommendation — knowledge gap.*
> **Comment:** *High-risk populations are NAFLD with advanced age, obesity, particularly central adiposity, diabetes, alanine elevated >2× upper limit of normal with an estimated cirrhosis prevalence of 30% (typically seen in a referral setting); low-risk population are those with NAFLD and signs of fatty liver on imaging only and an estimated cirrhosis prevalence of ≤5% (typically seen in a primary care setting).*

### VCTE technique and validity criteria

- Probe applied to the **intercostal skin in the 9th to 11th intercostal space**, sampling a region **25–65 mm below the skin surface (M-probe)** or **35–75 mm (XL-probe)**.
- A **minimum of 10 valid liver stiffness measurements** is obtained to derive the composite score.
- Result is of **adequate quality** when there are **at least 10 validated measurements** *and* the **interquartile range/median ratio of liver stiffness is ≤30%**.
- Score range **2.5–75 kPa**; acceptable intra- and inter-observer reproducibility; validated in hepatitis B, hepatitis C, fatty liver disease, and autoimmune liver disorders.
- **Technical limits for performance:** diameter of the intercostal space; obesity.
- **Inaccurate readings** in: **acute hepatitis**, **alcohol abuse**, **food intake within 2–3 hours**, **congestive heart failure**, and **extrahepatic cholestasis**.
- Diagnostic performance varies across liver conditions, with **differing cutoffs** to establish advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis.

### Misclassification at each cutoff, by pretest risk

The panel interpreted every cutoff in explicit prevalence scenarios:

- **HCV, 12.5 kPa:** misclassifies **<5%** of patients as not having cirrhosis when they do, and **<10%** as having cirrhosis when they do not.
- **Post-SVR, 9.5 kPa:** misclassifies **~1%** of low-risk and **~7%** of high-risk patients as not having advanced fibrosis — who may then be falsely reassured, discharged from a dedicated liver clinic, and not receive appropriate post-treatment supportive care. Low-risk = 5% prevalence of advanced fibrosis (SVR, no ongoing risk factors); high-risk = 30% prevalence (obesity, diabetes, excessive alcohol, HIV or HBV co-infection).
- **HBV, 11.0 kPa:** misclassifies **<1%** of low-risk and **~5%** of high-risk patients as not having cirrhosis, and **~10%–15%** as having cirrhosis when they do not.
- **Alcoholic liver disease, 12.5 kPa:** in a low-risk cohort (5% advanced-fibrosis prevalence), **0.2%** false negatives and **27.5%** false positives; in a high-risk cohort (30% prevalence, ongoing liver injury — obesity, fatty liver, HIV or HBV co-infection), **~1.5%** false negatives and **20.3%** false positives.
- **High-risk varices, 19.5 kPa:** misclassifies **0.6%** of low-risk and **~2.2%** of high-risk patients as not having high-risk varices, who may then miss nonselective β-blocker or band-ligation prophylaxis. Low-risk = 5% prevalence of high-risk varices (newly diagnosed compensated cirrhosis, platelet count >150,000/µL, no overt features of portal hypertension); high-risk = 20% prevalence (compensated cirrhosis, platelet count <150,000/µL, no overt features of portal hypertension).
- **CSPH, 17.0 kPa:** misclassifies **0.1%** of very-low-risk, **0.8%** of low-risk, and **~6.8%** of high-risk patients as not having CSPH. Very-low-risk = 0.5% prevalence of any varices (chronic liver disease, very low cirrhosis risk — e.g. prior noncirrhotic HCV with SVR); low-risk = 5% prevalence (chronic liver disease at risk for cirrhosis, no clinical evidence of portal hypertension); high-risk = 40% prevalence (known or suspected compensated cirrhosis).
- **NAFLD, MRE vs VCTE:** in a high-prevalence setting, little or no difference in false negatives, but MRE gives **119 in 1000 fewer patients incorrectly classified as having cirrhosis**. In a low-prevalence setting **both perform poorly**, with **2.5× (MRE) to 6× (VCTE) as many false-positive as true-positive tests**.

### Scope limits the guideline states about itself

- **Do not apply the alcoholic-liver-disease cutoff to acute alcoholic hepatitis** — the 12.5 kPa estimate was derived in chronic alcoholic liver disease and "should not be applied to patients with acute alcoholic hepatitis."
- **Decompensated cirrhosis, and compensated cirrhosis with known esophageal varices or portal hypertension, were not analyzed** for Q11/Q12 — endoscopic evaluation should be pursued in these patients as per standard clinical practice **without consideration of a VCTE cutoff of 19.5 kPa**.
- **Cirrhosis cutoffs should not by themselves drive invasive downstream decisions** (e.g. upper endoscopy to screen for varices) — that decision has its own question and cutoff (Q11).
- **HCC surveillance with noninvasive tests should ideally include patients with advanced fibrosis (F3), not only cirrhosis** — patients falsely labeled as having cirrhosis frequently have F3 and can still benefit from screening.
- **In hepatitis C, repeating elastography after viral cure frequently lowers liver stiffness**, reducing the number of patients falsely classified as cirrhotic over time.
- **Not addressed:** proprietary serum fibrosis assays; other imaging elastography (2D-SWE, ARFI/pSWE); combinations of more than one noninvasive test; a *range* of thresholds rather than a single false-negative-minimizing cutoff. Liver biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosis and staging of nonalcoholic steatohepatitis.
- The guideline cites the **Baveno VI Consensus Workshop** as further supporting risk stratification based on a **VCTE cutoff <20 kPa and platelet count >150,000/µL**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Superseded by newer tier-1 sources on the fibrosis cutoffs.** [[aasld-2024-nilda-blood]]/[[aasld-2024-nilda-imaging]] and [[aasld-2023-nafld]] set VCTE rule-out <8 kPa, rule-in ≥12 kPa, cirrhosis ≥20 kPa as a cross-etiology ladder, and [[baveno-viii-2026-portal-hypertension]] sets the cACLD/CSPH ladder (<10 rules out cACLD; ≤15 + platelets ≥150 ×10⁹/L rules out CSPH; ≥25 rules CSPH in). Those newer numbers govern the wiki pages. AGA 2017's contribution that survives is **etiology-specific** and is recorded as the historical/contrasting position.
- **Cirrhosis cutoff for MASLD/NAFLD.** AGA 2017 issues *no recommendation — knowledge gap*; the later AASLD guidelines do give MASLD VCTE cut-points. The knowledge gap was closed by newer evidence, not by disagreement.
- **The post-SVR question, against [[aga-2017-care-after-svr-hcv]] (same year, same society).** This guideline suggests a post-treatment VCTE **<9.5 kPa** to rule out advanced fibrosis and **consider discharging** noncirrhotic SVR patients from a dedicated liver clinic. The AGA 2017 Clinical Practice Update on care after SVR states that **no elastography score identifies a post-SVR patient whose HCC risk has fallen far enough to alter surveillance**, and declines to endorse Baveno VI sparing criteria post-SVR. The two are reconcilable only because they answer different questions — *discharge from a fibrosis clinic in a noncirrhotic* vs *stopping HCC surveillance in someone with pretreatment F3/cirrhosis* — and the elastography guideline's own Comment already offers the patient the option of staying in specialty care. Where they overlap, the CPU's restriction (surveillance is driven by **pretreatment** fibrosis stage, never de-escalated by improved stiffness) governs the wiki.
- **Varices/CSPH cutoffs vs Baveno.** AGA 2017's 19.5 kPa endoscopy-sparing threshold and 17.0 kPa CSPH threshold are both superseded — Baveno VIII uses LSM <20 kPa + platelets ≥150 ×10⁹/L (or SSM <40 kPa) to spare endoscopy, and ≥25 kPa to rule CSPH in. Note the specificity of the AGA thresholds is poor (0.56 and 0.52), which is the direct consequence of the panel's decision to minimize false negatives.
- **MRE in hepatitis C.** AGA 2017 prefers VCTE over MRE (cost, point-of-care availability, better specificity). Newer wiki sources rank MRE as the most accurate fibrosis NIT (AUROC ~0.93) without making a cost-based preference statement — the AGA position is a resource-use judgment rather than an accuracy claim, and is not in direct conflict.
- **Open question the guideline itself raises:** every cutoff was limited to a **single threshold optimized to minimize false negatives** rather than a range of thresholds; the panel calls for studies tying elastography results to downstream patient-important outcomes, and for defining the role of **serial** measurement.
