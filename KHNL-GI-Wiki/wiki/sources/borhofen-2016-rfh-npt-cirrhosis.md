## Summary

The Royal Free Hospital-Global Assessment (RFH-GA) assesses nutritional status in cirrhosis accurately but is **time-consuming and requires a trained dietician**. The **RFH-Nutritional Prioritizing Tool (RFH-NPT)** was derived from it to be answerable by **non-specialist staff**, and shifts the question from *what is this patient's nutritional status* to *what is this patient's risk of malnutrition* — a question simple clinical items can answer.

This study asked whether that risk score carries **prognostic** information in chronic liver disease, comparing it with the general-purpose **Nutritional Risk Screening (NRS)** tool and the **Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ)**.

**Cohort.** Of 646 patients receiving nutritional counselling at a single German centre (Bonn, Nov 2013–Dec 2014), 193 had chronic liver disease and **84 consented and were analysed**; **75 were re-evaluated** after a median of **500 days**. A separate **validation cohort of 64** patients was recruited (Jan 2013–Oct 2015). Median Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) 13, median Child score 9, 40% Child B, 46.4% alcohol-related, 74% on diuretics.

**Findings.** RFH-NPT classified **50.7% as high risk**, against **44.6% moderate-to-high by NRS**. RFH-NPT correlated with **Child score (p = 0.001)** and **MELD (p = 0.002)** and with ascites, hepatorenal syndrome and episodes of hepatic encephalopathy; CLDQ did not correlate with clinical parameters at all, showing only a weak inverse correlation with RFH-NPT. On multivariate analysis **RFH-NPT was an independent predictor of clinical deterioration and transplant-free survival**. Most notably, **improvement in RFH-NPT within 500 days was associated with improved survival** — the tool is repeatable and its change carries information, not just its baseline value.

The authors are measured about what this supports: the study is **observational and retrospective in character**, **small**, patients were treated per guidelines rather than stratified by RFH-NPT, and the scores are **semiquantitative**, allowing "a certain level of subjective validation". They call for prospective interventional studies.

## Key Findings / Claims

### What this paper does state about the RFH-NPT's structure

- **Risk bands by points: low = 0 points · moderate = 1 point · high = 2–7 points.**
- **It is a three-step tool:**
  1. **First, acute alcoholic hepatitis or tube feeding is evaluated — either condition qualifies the patient as high risk immediately.**
  2. **Second, patients are separated by the presence or absence of ascites or oedema.**
  3. **Finally the score is computed and the patient assigned to the corresponding risk group.**
- **Inputs:** nutritional history — **unplanned weight loss, dietary intake, body mass index (BMI)** — plus **current complications of liver disease (acute hepatitis, ascites, general fluid overload)**.
- Designed around **simple clinical questions easy to answer by non-specialist staff**, unlike the RFH-GA it was modified from.

### The comparator — Nutritional Risk Screening (NRS)

- **Maximum 3 points** for dietary status (dietary intake, weight loss, BMI) and severity of disease; **1 additional point for age above 70 years**.
- **Bands: 0 = no risk · 1–2 = low risk · 3–7 = medium to high risk.**
- ⚠ **The role of NRS has not been investigated specifically for liver cirrhosis** — it is a general inpatient tool.

### Prognostic performance

- **RFH-NPT was an independent predictor of clinical deterioration and of transplant-free survival** in multivariate (Cox, enter and forward stepwise) analysis.
- **Amelioration of the RFH-NPT was associated with significantly improved survival time.**
- RFH-NPT correlated significantly with **Child score (p = 0.001)** and **MELD (p = 0.002)**, and with **ascites, hepatorenal syndrome and hepatic encephalopathy** episodes.
- Higher RFH-NPT scores were associated with **low sodium (p = 0.046)**, **low total protein (p = 0.001)** and **low albumin (p = 0.011)**.
- RFH-NPT scores at inclusion and follow-up correlated with each other in **both** the first and the validation cohort.
- **Transplant-free survival** by Kaplan–Meier: **median MELD discriminated survivors from non-survivors (p = 0.046)**; **median Child score did not (p = 0.068)**; **NRS risk groups did (p = 0.047)**.
- Risk classification differed between the tools: in the first cohort **68 high risk by RFH-NPT** vs **41 low / 40 moderate-to-high by NRS**; in the validation cohort **RFH-NPT identified 48 at moderate-to-high risk** vs **26 by NRS**.

### Cohort detail and limits

- **84 analysed at inclusion, 75 re-evaluated** at a median of 500 days; **28 died**, **5 transplanted** during follow-up.
- Validation cohort (n = 64) had **lower MELD (median 10)**, fewer Child C patients and less severe ascites.
- Stated limitations: **small size limits predictive power**; **observational**; patients treated per current guidelines, **not stratified by RFH-NPT**; scores are **semiquantitative**, "easier to apply than quantitative scores and also allowing a certain level of subjective validation".

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **⚠ The paper does not contain the algorithm the wiki needed it for.** Its Methods say the RFH-NPT "was assessed as previously described" and point to **Supplementary Figure 1** of **[Amodio P, Bemeur C, Butterworth R, et al. The nutritional management of hepatic encephalopathy in patients with cirrhosis: International Society for Hepatic Encephalopathy and Nitrogen Metabolism (ISHEN) Consensus. *Hepatology* 2013;58:325–336](https://doi.org/10.1002/hep.26370)**. **That ISHEN consensus is the document that would close the gap**, and it is not in `raw/`; the `needed-sources.md` row has been corrected to name it instead of this paper.
- **No contradiction with [[aasld-2021-malnutrition-cirrhosis]]**, which identifies RFH-NPT as the screening tool most consistently associated with a diagnosis of malnutrition in cirrhosis and reports the same direction of association with outcomes. This paper is a source of that evidence.
- **NRS is not validated in cirrhosis**, as this paper states — yet it discriminated transplant-free survival here (p = 0.047) while the Child score did not (p = 0.068). The authors do not claim NRS is adequate for liver disease, and neither does the wiki.
- **Prognostic association is not a treatment indication.** The study was not stratified or randomised by RFH-NPT, so "improvement in RFH-NPT is associated with improved survival" is an association; whether acting on the score changes outcome is untested, and the authors say so.
