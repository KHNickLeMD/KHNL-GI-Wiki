## Summary

GRADE guideline on **how and when to repair a bile duct injury (BDI) after cholecystectomy** — not on preventing one (SAGES covered prevention and referral in its 2020 safe-cholecystectomy document). Population: adults with a **major BDI**, defined as **Strasberg grades B through E**. Target users are surgeons, and every recommendation is written on the assumption that the repair is done by a surgeon experienced in hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) pathology.

Four key questions, each answered with a **conditional recommendation** — there is no strong recommendation anywhere in the document, and certainty of evidence is **very low** for all four. The panel used the GRADE vocabulary deliberately: *"the guideline panel recommends"* for strong, *"the guideline panel suggests"* for conditional.

Only KQ 1 (timing) resolved to a direction: **delayed repair, >6 weeks**, with the evidence showing early repair worse on stricture, surgical re-intervention, quality of life and mortality, and better only on length of stay (a difference the panel judged trivial). KQ 2 (minimally invasive vs open), KQ 3 (operative vs nonoperative) and KQ 4 (hepaticoduodenostomy vs hepaticojejunostomy) all resolved to **"either"**, with the choice turned over to injury type, anatomy and local expertise — and the discussion sections, not the recommendation lines, carry the clinical criteria that actually decide those cases.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Recommendations (the guideline's own numbering; all conditional, all very low certainty)

**KQ 1 — early or delayed definitive repair?**
> The panel suggests **delayed repair (greater than 6 weeks)** by an experienced surgeon in HPB pathology for the management of BDI following cholecystectomy.

- **The qualifier that travels with it:** the recommendation supports **temporizing measures in a patient presenting within 6 weeks of the index operation — including intraoperative detection — if no hepatobiliary surgery expert is available at the local facility.** Temporizing = drainage, percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography, stent placement; this buys time to transfer the patient without compromising outcomes. If the injury is found at the index operation, safe temporizing is **wide drainage for sepsis control and biliary diversion if possible**.
- **Early repair (<6 weeks) may be favored** when the surgeon is highly skilled at reconstruction, the injury type is well defined, intra-abdominal sepsis is controlled or eradicated, and expertise for adjunct procedures is available — **especially if the injury is discovered at the index operation or within a week of injury**.
- **Delay may not be possible** with uncontrolled sepsis, or in a facility without interventional radiology and/or therapeutic endoscopy *even if surgical expertise is present*.
- **Delay is of extra benefit** in highly severe injuries and in concomitant vascular injury — it allows time for revascularization of the common bile duct, reducing postoperative stricture risk. (The RCT excluded vascular injury.)

**KQ 2 — minimally invasive (MIS) or open repair?**
> The panel suggests **either MIS or open** definitive repair by an experienced surgeon in HPB pathology.

- **MIS is preferred in patients who are stable, with favorable anatomy, and in those with high body mass index or obesity** — and only by surgeons experienced in **both** MIS and HPB surgery.

**KQ 3 — operative or nonoperative definitive management?**
> The panel suggests **either definitive operative management or nonoperative management for amenable injuries with expertise available**.

- **Favors nonoperative (endoscopic/radiologic):** less severe injuries **with a dilated biliary system**. Endoscopic management lends itself to **type D and lower E1–E2 injuries**; in the one low-risk-of-bias study, endoscopy was favored in **C, D and E1**.
- **Favors operative:** **decompressed biliary system, severe sepsis, complex biliary or vascular injury**; surgery was favored in **E3 and E5** in that same study.
- **A dilated biliary system is typically needed** for endoscopic candidacy — patients whose injury is discovered promptly may therefore be poorer endoscopic candidates.
- Outcome split: nonoperative better on **mortality and cholangitis** (very low certainty, very wide intervals, three high-risk-of-bias studies — treat with caution); operative better on **stricture** (OR 2.44, CI 1.45–4.09, low certainty), **surgical re-intervention** (OR 16.6, CI 2.28–120.9, low certainty) and **endoscopic/radiologic re-intervention**.
- The panel's resolution: **if expertise is available and it is safe for the patient, an endoscopic attempt is reasonable** given the lower mortality signal — but complex injuries only at select centres with advanced endoscopic expertise.
- **Set expectations:** endoscopic management often needs **several re-interventions over months**, which patients weigh heavily.

**KQ 4 — hepaticoduodenostomy (HD) or hepaticojejunostomy (HJ)?**
> The panel suggests **either HD or HJ**, with the decision dependent upon **location of injury and expertise of the surgeon**. Long-term outcomes data are required to better understand this question.

- **HJ:** widely performed, technically harder, typically used for **more proximal, higher injuries**; requires **two anastomoses** (the HJ and the jejunojejunostomy). Extremely challenging in obesity, short mesentery, congenital aberrant anatomy, or previous enteric bypass/extensive adhesions.
- **HD:** technically more straightforward, more physiological, and gives **easier postoperative endoscopic or cholangiographic access** to the repair. Challenging with substantial porta hepatis inflammation (duodenal mobilization may be unsafe); **the higher the injury, the more mobilization a tension-free HD needs**, which HJ generally does not.
- **Contraindications to HD: E5 injuries or severe pancreatitis.** Long-term HD complications include **sump syndrome and alkaline reflux**.
- Biliary sepsis may favour the **less complex** operation (HD).
- Evidence: 2 observational studies, both high risk of bias. HD showed **no benefit** over HJ on any outcome; HD was worse on endoscopic/radiologic re-intervention (OR 2.85, CI 0.13–64.89, 21 patients) and stricture (OR 1.59, CI 0.16–15.40, 47 patients) — all very low certainty.

### Background facts the guideline supplies

- **Strasberg classification, A–E:** class **A** is least severe (bile leak from the cystic duct or small ducts in the liver bed); class **E** is most severe (circumferential injury to a major bile duct with **loss of continuity**), subclassified **E1–E5**. **Concomitant vascular injury is not part of the system** and is present in **26–32%** of these patients.
- **Only 15–30% of injuries are diagnosed intraoperatively.**
- **Long-term stricture occurs in about 10%** of treated patients, so **follow-up is recommended for at least 10 years** postoperatively.
- Outcomes are better with specialty care at a tertiary centre with surgeons and physicians versed in hepatobiliary intervention.
- Therapeutic decision-making turns on: **type of injury · concomitant vascular injury · control of sepsis · timing of diagnosis or referral · expertise availability**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **The recommendation lines and the discussion say different amounts.** Three of four recommendations are "either/or"; the criteria that actually pick a side (dilated vs decompressed biliary tree, Strasberg type, E5 and severe pancreatitis as HD contraindications, MIS for the stable obese patient with favourable anatomy) appear only in the evidence-discussion and conclusion paragraphs. Those criteria are what reaches the wiki pages.
- **Mortality favours nonoperative management, stricture and re-intervention favour operative** — the panel explicitly distrusts the mortality signal (three high-risk-of-bias studies, likely selection by injury severity) and gives more weight to the stricture and re-intervention outcomes, which carried higher certainty.
- **Length of stay is the only outcome favouring early repair**, and the panel judged the combined magnitude of that benefit **trivial** against a **large** combined harm.
- **No strong recommendation and no outcome above low certainty** anywhere in the document — the sole RCT (277 participants) was judged high risk of bias for a 17% unexplained dropout in its septic cohort.
- Publication year ambiguity: the title and the online publication date are **2025**, the journal issue is dated **2026**. Cited here as 2025.
