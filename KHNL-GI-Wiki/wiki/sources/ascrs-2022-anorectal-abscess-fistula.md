## Summary

ASCRS clinical practice guideline updating the society's 2016 document on anorectal abscess, fistula-in-ano, and rectovaginal fistula (RVF). 25 graded recommendations across four sections: initial evaluation, anorectal abscess, anal fistula, RVF, and anorectal fistula associated with Crohn's disease. Built from a literature search of 841 screened articles (December 2015 – November 2021), of which 269 were referenced. Grading uses the GRADE system with the 1A–2C notation (1 = strong, 2 = weak; A/B/C = high/moderate/low-very low quality evidence).

The pathophysiologic premise is the cryptoglandular theory: an anorectal abscess results from obstruction of an anal gland, and a fistula results from chronic infection and epithelialization of the abscess drainage tract. Abscesses are named for the anatomic space in which they develop — ischiorectal (ischioanal) most common, followed by intersphincteric, supralevator, and submucosal. Abscess is more common in males, with peak incidence at 20–40 years. 30–70% of patients with an anorectal abscess present with a concomitant fistula-in-ano, and of those who do not, roughly 30–50% are eventually diagnosed with a fistula over the following months to years.

Treatment of fistula-in-ano is framed as a trade-off between obliterating the tract and preserving sphincter function, so the simple-versus-complex distinction governs the operation. The 2022 update reverses or alters several 2016 positions: antibiotics after abscess drainage moved from 2C to 2B, the cutting seton was downgraded from 2B to 2C, the anal fistula plug and fibrin glue were upgraded in *strength* of the recommendation against them (2B → 1B), and in the Crohn's section draining setons (1C → 1B), endorectal advancement flap/LIFT (2B → 1B) and lay-open fistulotomy (1C → 2C) were all re-graded. Three recommendations are entirely new in 2022: minimally invasive endoscopic/laser fistula closure, multidisciplinary combined surgical–medical management of Crohn's fistula, and local mesenchymal stem cell therapy.

The Crohn's section is the most clinically consequential addition for gastroenterology: it formalizes combined surgical and medical management, keeps asymptomatic fistulas out of the operating room, endorses long-term ("destination") draining setons for disease control, and for the first time in an ASCRS guideline endorses local mesenchymal stem cell administration for refractory fistulizing anorectal Crohn's disease.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Recommendations (the document's own numbering and grades)

**Initial evaluation of anorectal abscess and fistula**

1. A disease-specific history and physical examination should be performed, evaluating symptoms, relevant history, abscess and fistula location, and presence of secondary cellulitis. *Strong recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **1C***.
2. Routine diagnostic imaging is **not** typically necessary for anorectal abscess or fistula. Imaging **may be considered** in selected patients with an occult anorectal abscess, recurrent or complex anal fistula, immunosuppression, or anorectal Crohn's disease. *Strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence, **1B***.

**Anorectal abscess**

3. Patients with acute anorectal abscess should be treated promptly with incision and drainage. *Strong recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **1C***.
4. Abscess drainage with concomitant fistulotomy may be performed in **selected patients with simple anal fistulas**. *Weak recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence, **2B***.
5. Antibiotics should typically be reserved for patients with an anorectal abscess complicated by **cellulitis, systemic signs of infection, or underlying immunosuppression**. *Weak recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence, **2B*** (2016 grade was 2C).

**Anal fistula**

6. Patients with a **simple** fistula-in-ano and **normal anal sphincter function** may be treated with lay-open fistulotomy. *Strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence, **1B***.
7. Fistula-in-ano may be treated with endorectal advancement flap. *Strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence, **1B***.
8. **Transsphincteric** fistulas may be treated with ligation of the intersphincteric fistula tract (LIFT). *Strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence, **1B***.
9. A cutting seton may be used **selectively** in the management of complex cryptoglandular anal fistulas. *Weak recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **2C*** (2016 grade was 2B).
10. The anal fistula plug and fibrin glue are **relatively ineffective** treatments for fistula-in-ano. *Strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence, **1B*** (2016 grade was 2B).
11. Minimally invasive approaches to treat fistula-in-ano that use **endoscopic or laser closure** techniques have reasonable short-term healing rates but **unknown long-term fistula healing and recurrence rates**. *Weak recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **2C*** (new in 2022).

**Rectovaginal fistula**

12. **Nonoperative** management is typically recommended for the **initial** care of obstetrical RVF and may also be considered for other benign and minimally symptomatic fistulas. *Weak recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **2C***.
13. A draining seton may facilitate resolution of acute inflammation or infection associated with RVF. *Strong recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **1C***.
14. Endorectal advancement flap **with or without sphincteroplasty** is the procedure of choice for most patients with an RVF. *Strong recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **1C***.
15. **Episioproctotomy** may be used to repair obstetrical or cryptoglandular RVF in patients with **anal sphincter defects**. *Strong recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **1C***.
16. A **gracilis muscle or bulbocavernosus (Martius) flap** is typically recommended for recurrent or otherwise complex RVF. *Strong recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **1C***.
17. RVF resulting from **colorectal anastomotic complications** often require a **transabdominal** approach for repair. *Strong recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **1C***.
18. **Completion proctectomy** with or without colonic pull-through or coloanal anastomosis may be required to treat **radiation-related or recurrent complex** RVF. *Weak recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **2C***.

**Anorectal fistula associated with Crohn's disease**

19. Anorectal fistula associated with Crohn's disease is typically managed with a **combination of surgical and medical** approaches. *Strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence, **1B*** (new in 2022).
20. **Asymptomatic** fistulas in patients with Crohn's disease typically do **not** require surgical treatment. *Strong recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **1C***.
21. **Draining setons** are typically useful in the multimodality therapy of fistulizing anorectal Crohn's disease and may be used for **long-term disease control**. *Strong recommendation based upon moderate-quality evidence, **1B*** (2016 grade was 1C).
22. **Symptomatic, simple, low** anal fistulas in carefully selected patients with Crohn's disease may be treated by lay-open fistulotomy. *Weak recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **2C*** (2016 grade was 1C).
23. **Endorectal advancement flaps and the LIFT procedure** may be used to treat fistula-in-ano associated with Crohn's disease. *Strong recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence, **1B*** (2016 grade was 2B).
24. Patients with **uncontrolled symptoms** from complex anorectal fistulizing Crohn's disease may require **fecal diversion or proctectomy**. *Strong recommendation based on low-quality evidence, **1C***.
25. **Local administration of mesenchymal stem cells** is a safe and effective treatment for selected patients with **refractory** anorectal fistulas in the setting of Crohn's disease. *Weak recommendation based on moderate-quality evidence, **2B*** (new in 2022).

### Definitions and classification

- **Parks classification of fistula-in-ano (Table 1)** — submucosal, intersphincteric, transsphincteric, suprasphincteric, extrasphincteric; reproduced on [[anal-fistula]].
- **Complex anal fistula:** transsphincteric fistulas involving **>30% of the external sphincter**, suprasphincteric, extrasphincteric, or horseshoe fistulas, and fistulas associated with IBD, radiation, malignancy, preexisting fecal incontinence, or chronic diarrhea. Recurrent or branching fistulas may also be described as complex. **Anterior fistulas in women** deserve special consideration and may also be considered complex.
- **Simple anal fistula:** none of the above complex features; in general, intersphincteric and **low transsphincteric** fistulas involving **<30% of the external sphincter**.
- **RVF level:** **low** = tract between the distal anal canal (at or below the dentate line) and the inside of the posterior fourchette; **high** = tract connecting the upper vagina (at the level of the cervix) with the rectum; **middle** = tract in between. "Anovaginal fistula" and "low rectovaginal fistula" may be used interchangeably.
- **Simple RVF:** low, small-diameter (**<2 cm**) communication between anal canal and vagina, typically from obstetrical injury or infection. **Complex RVF:** higher tract between rectum and vagina, larger diameter, or resulting from radiation, cancer, or complications of pelvic surgical procedures.
- **Goodsall's rule:** an anterior fistula has a radial tract to the anus and a posterior fistula a curvilinear tract — generally accurate for anterior fistulas but **less accurate for posterior fistulas**.

### Epidemiology and natural history

- Anorectal abscess: more often in males; peak incidence **20–40 years**.
- **30–70%** of patients with anorectal abscess present with a concomitant fistula-in-ano; of those who do not, approximately **30–50%** are ultimately diagnosed with a fistula in the months to years after drainage.
- Abscess recurs in up to **44%** of patients after drainage, most within **1 year**. Risk factors for recurrence: inadequate drainage, loculations, horseshoe-type abscess, not performing a primary fistulotomy.
- Crohn's disease: fistula-in-ano incidence **10–20%** in population-based studies and **50%** in longitudinal studies; nearly **80%** of patients with Crohn's disease cared for at tertiary referral centers have a history of fistula-in-ano.

### Diagnostic performance

- CT for anorectal abscess: overall sensitivity **77%** (113 patients); **70%** in immunosuppressed patients.
- MRI in anorectal Crohn's disease (54 patients): **all** abscesses and **82%** of fistulas correctly identified.
- MRI in suspected anal fistula (2014 prospective, 50 patients): sensitivity **95%**, specificity **80%**, positive predictive value **97%** for detecting and grading the primary fistula tract.
- MRI provided significant findings more often in complex than simple fistulas (**54% vs 5%**, *p* < 0.001); MRI contribution rose with Parks grade (5% grade 1; 48% grade 2; 86% grade 3; 87.5% grade 4).
- Endoanal ultrasound (EAUS) concordance with operative findings: **73–100%**.
- 2001 blinded study, 34 patients with anorectal Crohn's: EAUS accurate in **91%**, MRI **87%**, examination under anesthesia **91%**; **100%** when any **2** techniques were combined.
- Transperineal ultrasound (TPUS) vs EAUS vs MRI (23 patients with Crohn's): diagnostic accuracy of all 3 nearly identical.

### Treatment outcomes

- **Fistulotomy:** resolution rates **>90%** for simple fistula; fecal incontinence minimal in appropriately selected low-lying fistulas, but **10–40%** significant incontinence when used for high-lying or complex fistulas. Marsupialization after fistulotomy reduced postoperative bleeding and improved wound healing in 4 RCTs.
- **Endorectal advancement flap:** healing in **66–87%**; **57–100%** when repeated after a failed initial procedure. A diverting stoma has **not** improved outcomes and is not typically recommended.
- **LIFT:** meta-analysis of 1378 procedures (26 studies) — overall success **76%**, complication rate **14%**, fecal incontinence rate **1.4%**. Failure risk factors: horseshoe anatomy, Crohn's fistula, prior fistula surgery. Modifications of standard LIFT are **not** typically recommended.
- **Cutting seton:** healing **~90%** with sphincter preservation in most; a pooled review of 37 studies (1460 patients) reported postoperative fecal incontinence ranging **0–67%**.
- **Anal fistula plug:** early data 70–100% success, more recently **≤50%**; plug dislodgement in 4–41%. Plug failure more common in Crohn's disease, anovaginal fistula, and active smokers.
- **Fibrin glue:** 2019 RCT — complete healing in only 10 of 24 (**41%**).
- **VAAFT** (video-assisted anal fistula treatment): healing **71–85%**, follow-up typically <12 months. **FiLaC** (fistula laser closure): meta-analysis of 7 series, 454 patients — **65%** healed at median 24 months, 4% complications, mean incontinence rate 1%. **OTSC clip**: primary healing 79–90% in small single-institution series; 79% at 6 months for first-time therapy vs **26%** for recurrent fistulas in a German series of 100.
- **Nonoperative RVF management:** healing **52–66%**; baths, wound care, debridement, antibiotics for infection, stool-bulking agents, usually over **3 to 6 months**.
- **Episioproctotomy:** fistula healing **78–100%**.
- **Gracilis flap for RVF:** healing **50–92%**; complication rates 28–47% (surgical site infection, thigh numbness, hematoma). **Martius flap:** overall success ~91% (24 patients), 65% (20 patients), 93% with diverting ostomy (14 patients).
- **Fecal diversion for refractory Crohn's fistula:** initial response **64–81%**, but only **26–50%** sustain remission; **45–68%** ultimately require proctectomy. In a meta-analysis of 556 patients, early clinical response after diversion in **64%** (95% CI 54.1–72.5); stoma reversal attempted in 34.5% and successful in only **17%** (95% CI 11.8–22.9); **42%** (95% CI 32.6–51.2) required proctectomy.
- **Mesenchymal stem cells:** largest phase III RCT — 212 patients, placebo vs 120 million allogeneic MSCs; fistula healing **50% vs 34%** at 6 months (*p* = 0.02), with equivalent rates of anorectal pain and abscess between arms. Across trials, efficacy **50–83%** at 6 months to 1 year; doses ranged **20 million to 120 million cells**; only reported adverse events were anorectal pain and abscess.
- **Infliximab in Crohn's fistula:** initial fistula healing **38–55%**, long-term healing **39%**. Certolizumab pegol: anal fistulas healed in **36%** vs 17% with placebo (*p* = 0.03) in the PRECiSE trial; when success was defined as ≥50% closure at 2 consecutive visits ≥3 weeks apart, no difference between groups. Adalimumab: 2 randomized trials showed no benefit, but a subsequent randomized double-blind trial showed **33%** healing vs 13% placebo (*p* < 0.05).
- **Seton + anti-TNF:** retrospective series of 32 patients — lower fistula recurrence with seton placement (**44% vs 79%**, *p* < 0.001) and longer time to recurrence (13.5 vs 3.6 months, *p* < 0.001). A systematic review/meta-analysis of 10 studies including 4 RCTs concluded anti-TNF + temporary seton was likely beneficial. In the subgroup of the 2 RCTs with >4 weeks follow-up, complete fistula healing was higher with anti-TNF (46% vs 13%, *p* = 0.003 and 30% vs 13%, *p* = 0.03).
- **Antibiotics alone in Crohn's fistula without drainage:** metronidazole and fluoroquinolones give at least temporary symptomatic improvement in **>90%**.
- **Lay-open fistulotomy in Crohn's:** healing **62–100%**, mild incontinence **6–12%**; some studies report incontinence up to **50%**, especially with active proctitis.
- **Advancement flap in Crohn's:** systematic review of 91 patients — healing **64%** (range 33–93%) at median 29 months; incontinence 9.4% (range 0–29%). **LIFT in Crohn's:** 15 patients, 67% remained healed at 12 months; a later retrospective study of 23 patients found 48% healed at median 23 months, median time to failure 9 months in failures.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Antibiotics after abscess drainage.** ASCRS 2022 reserves antibiotics for cellulitis, systemic signs of infection, or immunosuppression (rec 5, 2B), citing two contradictory randomized trials: Ghahramani et al (307 patients) found fistula formation in 14% with a 7-day ciprofloxacin/metronidazole course vs 30% without (*p* < 0.001), while Sözener et al (334 patients, multicenter, placebo-controlled, double-blind) showed **no** protective effect on fistula formation. The guideline does not resolve the discrepancy.
- **Primary fistulotomy at the time of abscess drainage** is explicitly described as controversial (rec 4). A 2010 Cochrane review of 479 patients across 6 RCTs found sphincter division at drainage reduced recurrence, persistent fistula/abscess, or need for subsequent surgery (RR 0.13, 95% CI 0.07–0.24) with an increased but not statistically significant incidence of continence disturbance at 1 year — but those trials **excluded** patients with complex fistulas, recurrent abscesses, IBD, preexisting incontinence, or prior anorectal surgery.
- **Mesenchymal stem cells.** Neither [[acg-2025-crohns|ACG 2025]] nor [[aga-2021-crohns-pharm|AGA 2021]] addresses local mesenchymal stem cell therapy for perianal fistula; ASCRS 2022 rec 25 does, and is the source for that content on [[perianal-fistulizing-crohns-disease]].
- **Lay-open fistulotomy in Crohn's disease.** ASCRS 2022 *downgraded* this from a strong 1C in 2016 to a weak 2C. [[acg-2025-crohns|ACG 2025]] — newer, and the higher-priority source where the two overlap — states that a simple fistula without active rectal mucosal involvement may respond well to fistulotomy or mucosal advancement flap. The two are compatible in substance, both requiring careful selection; ACG 2025's framing governs, with ASCRS's selection criteria (<30% external sphincter involvement, absence of proctitis) supplying the operative detail.
- **Routine imaging.** Rec 2 says routine imaging is not typically necessary, yet ACG 2025 requires cross-sectional imaging before advanced therapy in Crohn's perianal disease. No conflict — ASCRS rec 2 names anorectal Crohn's disease as one of its explicit exceptions.
