## Summary

This is the TG18 companion article to the management flowchart ([[tg18-2018-cholangitis-flowchart]]) and the one that actually **prints the criteria tables**: the diagnostic criteria (Table 2) with their laboratory thresholds, and the severity assessment criteria (Table 4) with the Grade II and Grade III definitions. Its stated purpose is validation rather than revision — the committee systematically reviewed the literature on the Tokyo Guidelines 2013 (TG13) criteria, tested them against a large Japan–Taiwan multicentre case series, and asked whether they should be carried forward unchanged.

The answer is yes, twice. **The TG13 diagnostic criteria and the TG13 severity grading criteria are adopted as the TG18 criteria without revision.** The supporting data: applied to 6,063 patients clinically suspected of acute cholangitis, the TG13 criteria yielded a definite or suspected diagnosis in 90.0% versus 79.4% for the Tokyo Guidelines 2007 (TG07) criteria (P<0.0001). At the public hearing, 98.6% of attendees agreed to adopt the diagnostic criteria unchanged and 93.7% agreed to adopt the severity grading unchanged.

The diagnostic criteria are built as three domains — **A systemic inflammation** (mandatory), **B cholestasis**, **C imaging** — because Charcot's triad, while highly specific, has sensitivity too low (reported 26.4% and 21.2% in large multicentre series) to serve as a diagnostic rule. Each domain carries explicit numeric thresholds. A **suspected** diagnosis needs one item in A plus one in either B or C; a **definite** diagnosis needs one item in each of A, B and C.

Severity grading is justified by its therapeutic consequence rather than by prognostic elegance. In the same case series, early or urgent biliary drainage reduced 30-day mortality **only in Grade II** patients (1.7% vs 3.4% for drainage within 24 h, P<0.05) — no difference in Grade I or Grade III. That is the article's explicit rationale for keeping the grading: it identifies the patients whose prognosis drainage can change. The paper is candid about the criteria's weak points: all six Grade III organ-dysfunction factors carry equal weight despite disturbance of consciousness being the strongest single predictor, diagnosis rates are lowest in mild disease (83.6% of Grade I patients), specificity was never assessed, and severity grade does not correlate with 30-day mortality when the cholangitis is caused by a malignant tumour.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Diagnostic criteria for acute cholangitis (TG18/TG13 — Table 2)

| Domain | Item | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Systemic inflammation** | **A-1** Fever and/or shaking chills | body temperature (BT) **>38 °C** |
| | **A-2** Laboratory data: evidence of inflammatory response | white blood cell (WBC) count (×1,000/μL) **<4 or >10** · C-reactive protein (CRP) **≥1 mg/dL** |
| **B. Cholestasis** | **B-1** Jaundice | total bilirubin **≥2 mg/dL** |
| | **B-2** Laboratory data: abnormal liver function tests | alkaline phosphatase (ALP, IU) **>1.5 × STD** · γ-glutamyl transpeptidase (γ-GTP/GGT, IU) **>1.5 × STD** · aspartate aminotransferase (AST, IU) **>1.5 × STD** · alanine aminotransferase (ALT, IU) **>1.5 × STD** |
| **C. Imaging** | **C-1** Biliary dilatation | — |
| | **C-2** Evidence of the etiology on imaging | stricture, stone, stent etc. |

- **STD = upper limit of normal value** (Table 2 footnote). *Note the opposite convention in the severity table below.*
- **Suspected diagnosis:** one item in A **+** one item in either B **or** C.
- **Definite diagnosis:** one item in A, one item in B **and** one item in C.
- **Systemic inflammation must be present** for either level of diagnosis.
- Table 2 notes, verbatim in substance:
  - A-2 covers abnormal WBC counts, raised serum CRP, and other changes indicating inflammation.
  - B-2 covers increased serum ALP, γ-GTP (GGT), AST and ALT.
  - **Other helpful factors** (not criteria): abdominal pain (right upper quadrant or upper abdominal) and a history of biliary disease — gallstones, previous biliary procedures, placement of a biliary stent.
  - **Acute hepatitis** rarely shows a marked systemic inflammatory response; virological and serological tests are needed when the differential is difficult.
- Fever is defined as **38 °C or above**, but mild cases may show only minor temperature increases — hence diagnosis can rest on the blood-test items instead.
- Jaundice is present in only **60–70%** of patients, so a diagnosis can still be made in its absence on the strength of raised ALP, GGT, leucine aminopeptidase and transaminases.

### Severity assessment criteria (TG18/TG13 — Table 4)

**Grade III (severe)** — acute cholangitis associated with the onset of dysfunction in **at least any one** of these organs/systems:

| # | Organ/system | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cardiovascular dysfunction | hypotension requiring **dopamine ≥5 μg/kg per min**, or **any dose of norepinephrine** |
| 2 | Neurological dysfunction | disturbance of consciousness |
| 3 | Respiratory dysfunction | **PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio <300** |
| 4 | Renal dysfunction | oliguria, serum **creatinine >2.0 mg/dL** |
| 5 | Hepatic dysfunction | **PT-INR >1.5** |
| 6 | Hematological dysfunction | **platelet count <100,000/mm³** |

**Grade II (moderate)** — acute cholangitis associated with **any two** of the following five:

| # | Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Abnormal WBC count | **>12,000/mm³** or **<4,000/mm³** |
| 2 | High fever | **≥39 °C** |
| 3 | Age | **≥75 years old** |
| 4 | Hyperbilirubinemia | total bilirubin **≥5 mg/dL** |
| 5 | Hypoalbuminemia | **< STD × 0.7** |

- **STD = lower limit of normal value** for the albumin criterion (Table 4 footnote) — the opposite of the Table 2 convention, so the two tables cannot share one definition of "STD".
- **Grade I (mild)** — acute cholangitis that **does not meet** the Grade III or Grade II criteria **at initial diagnosis**.
- **The rules differ:** Grade III is an any-one rule; Grade II is an any-two-of-five rule. One Grade II criterion alone (age ≥75, bilirubin ≥5 mg/dL) does not make cholangitis moderate.
- **Ungraded statement printed beneath Table 4:** early diagnosis, early biliary drainage and/or treatment for the etiology, and antimicrobial administration are fundamental treatment for acute cholangitis of **every** grade — Grade I included. Patients who do not respond to initial medical treatment (general supportive care and antimicrobial therapy) are therefore recommended to undergo **early biliary drainage or treatment for the etiology**.

### Diagnostic performance of the criteria

- **TG13 vs TG07 in 6,063 patients clinically diagnosed with acute cholangitis** (Japan/Taiwan multicentre, admissions 2011–2013, before TG13 publication) — Table 1:

| Diagnostic status | TG13 | TG07 | P |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definite | 4,430 (73.1%) | 3,977 (65.6%) | |
| Suspected | 1,024 (16.9%) | 838 (13.8%) | |
| **Cholangitis (definite or suspected)** | **5,454 (90.0%)** | **4,815 (79.4%)** | **<0.0001** |
| Non-cholangitis | 609 (10.0%) | 1,248 (20.6%) | |

- **Where TG13 misses:** of the 609 patients not captured, **511 (83.9%) had no systemic inflammation**. Among the 2,523 Grade I patients, the TG13 diagnosis rate was only **83.6%** — mild disease is the weak point. Diagnosis rates were high where the cause was clear (stones, malignant tumour, stent occlusion), low where the cause was unknown.
- **Specificity of the TG13 criteria has not been assessed** in any study; the committee notes sensitivity matters more for a potentially lethal condition.
- **Charcot's triad:** reported sensitivity on the order of 50–70%, with much lower diagnosis rates (**26.4%** and **21.2%**) in more recent multicentre case series.
- **TG07 criteria:** three clinical manifestations based on Charcot's triad gave good sensitivity (**95.1%**) but poor specificity (**66.3%**), with a **38.8%** false-positive rate for acute cholecystitis. Dropping abdominal pain — leaving two of Charcot's triad — gave **91.8%** sensitivity and **77.7%** specificity with a **5.9%** false-positive rate for acute cholecystitis; that combination became the TG13 criteria.

### Imaging

- **Ultrasound** should be done first in suspected acute cholangitis: minimally invasive, widely available, convenient, cost-effective. Meta-analysis performance — dilated common bile duct: sensitivity **42%** (95% CI 28–56), specificity **96%** (95% CI 94–98); bile duct stones: sensitivity **38%** (95% CI 27–49), specificity **100%** (95% CI 99–100). In obstructive jaundice, another report gave sensitivity/specificity **100%/89%** for bile duct stones and **98.78%/83.33%** for bile duct cancer.
- **CT** is unaffected by intestinal gas and images a wide area, so it is often preferred when a patient presents with acute abdominal pain and other diseases must be excluded. **Detection sensitivity for bile duct stones is only 25–90%**, depending on stone calcium content. CT identifies bile duct dilatation and the cause of stenosis well (biliary carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, sclerosing cholangitis) and detects local complications (liver abscess, portal vein thrombosis).
- **MRCP** is non-invasive and best for the cause of obstruction: benign and malignant disease identified in **98% and 98%** of cases, vs **82.86% and 91.43%** with CT and **88% and 88%** with ultrasound. Limited by accessibility; generally used when ultrasound or CT leave the diagnosis uncertain.
- **Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography is performed for treatment (drainage), not as a first-choice diagnostic test.** Plain X-ray is not suited to diagnosis.
- **No imaging finding diagnoses acute cholangitis directly.** Imaging identifies the stenosis/blockage that causes it, with cholangiectasis as an indirect supporting finding.
- **Transient hepatic attenuation difference (THAD)** on early-phase dynamic CT (and the equivalent transient periductal signal difference on dynamic MRI) is seen in **67.9–85%** of acute cholangitis patients vs **1.78–5%** of healthy individuals. Evidence is inconsistent — one 123-patient study found no relationship between THAD degree and severity, and THAD occurs in pancreatitis, pyelonephritis and pneumonia, so it is not expected to be specific. Future research question.

### Severity grading — what it predicts and what it is for

- **Grade distribution** in the 6,063-patient series: Grade III 1,521 (**25.1%**), Grade II 2,019 (**33.3%**), Grade I 2,523 (**41.6%**).
- 30-day mortality rose significantly with higher grade — **except** in cholangitis caused by malignant tumours, where no correlation with grade was seen.
- **30-day mortality by grade and drainage timing (Table 3).** *Urgent* = performed on the admission day (within 24 h); *early* = the day following admission (24–48 h):

| Grade | Urgent, within 24 h | After 24 h or no drainage | P | Urgent or early, within 48 h | After 48 h or no drainage | P |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **III** (n=1,521) | 5.4% (42/781) | 4.9% (36/740) | 0.727 | 4.9% (50/1,017) | 5.6% (28/504) | 0.622 |
| **II** (n=2,019) | **1.7% (16/939)** | **3.4% (37/1,080)** | **<0.05** | **2.0% (25/1,272)** | **3.7% (28/747)** | **<0.05** |
| **I** (n=2,523) | 1.3% (13/989) | 1.2% (18/1,534) | 0.853 | 1.1% (16/1,441) | 1.4% (15/1,082) | 0.586 |
| **Total** (n=6,063) | 2.6% (72/2,709) | 2.7% (91/3,354) | 0.873 | 2.4% (91/3,730) | 3.0% (71/2,333) | 0.164 |

- **This table is the stated rationale for the grading system:** drainage changed 30-day mortality only in Grade II. Grade II patients have not yet progressed to organ dysfunction but are at risk of doing so, which is why identifying them is the grading criteria's most important job — the article says an indicator for biliary drainage is more meaningful in practice than a prognostic prediction.
- **Why Grade III showed no drainage benefit is unresolved.** The study defined urgent drainage as within 24 h of admission, whereas two other studies used 12 h; prognosis in Grade III may improve with drainage at an even earlier stage, and further research is warranted. It has also been suggested that some patients needing early drainage were misclassified as Grade I, but that came from a small case series and the proportion is unknown.
- **Weighting problem:** all six Grade III factors carry equal weight, yet univariate analysis identified **disturbance of consciousness** as the most important predictor of risk. On multivariate analysis in the multicentre study, the Grade III factors other than hepatic dysfunction had roughly equal weights.
- **Grade II prognostic factors:** abnormal WBC count and hypoalbuminemia were significant.
- **Predictors of poor outcome** (separate case series): univariate — renal failure, hepatic dysfunction, intrahepatic biliary stenosis, cholangitis caused by malignant disease, hypoalbuminemia; **multivariate — only intrahepatic biliary stenosis and hypoalbuminemia**. Studies weight the individual factors differently and conclusions are inconsistent.
- **Procalcitonin:** three case series found serum procalcitonin higher in severe cholangitis (by TG07 and by TG13 grading) and rising with severity grade; all were small single-institution studies, so further research is needed. Future research question.

### What changed from TG13 to TG18

- **Nothing in either criteria set.** Both the diagnostic criteria and the severity grading criteria are adopted from TG13 into TG18 **unchanged** — the article's work is validation, not revision.
- The TG07→TG13 change is the one that mattered: TG07's Charcot's-triad-based criteria were insufficiently sensitive to identify potentially life-threatening cases, and TG07 severity grading defined moderate disease ambiguously as "not responding to initial treatment" with no method for rapid bedside assessment. TG13 replaced both.
- **Public hearing votes:** 98.6% agreed the TG13 diagnostic criteria should be adopted as the TG18 criteria without revision (Fig. 1); 93.7% agreed the same for the severity grading criteria (Fig. 10).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **"STD" means opposite things in the two tables.** Table 2 footnote defines STD as the **upper** limit of normal (for the ALP/GGT/AST/ALT multiples); Table 4 footnote defines it as the **lower** limit of normal (for the albumin criterion). Read each table with its own footnote.
- **Resolves two thresholds previously recorded as uncertain.** [[tg18-2018-cholangitis-flowchart]] transcribed the platelet criterion from running text as "<10⁴/μL" and flagged it as implausibly low; the published Table 4 gives **<100,000/mm³**, confirming the lower figure was a transcription loss. The albumin criterion is confirmed as **< STD × 0.7** with STD = lower limit of normal.
- **Drainage timing — TG18 vs [[asge-2021-cholangitis]].** Table 3 here is the dataset behind TG18's within-24-h signal in moderate cholangitis (1.7% vs 3.4%, P<0.05). ASGE 2021 — the newer same-tier guideline — sets the window at **within 48 h**, chosen on the weight of the literature plus weekend-workforce considerations, and [[jagtap-2026-urgent-vs-early-ercp-cholangitis]] found no mortality advantage and more adverse events when the window was compressed below 24 h. Note that this same Table 3 also shows the Grade II benefit persisting at the **48-h** cut (2.0% vs 3.7%, P<0.05), so the two guidelines are less far apart than the headline numbers suggest. [[acute-cholangitis]] follows ASGE 2021.
- **Bilirubin cut-point for cholestasis differs from the trial that used these criteria.** TG18 B-1 is total bilirubin **≥2 mg/dL**; [[jagtap-2026-urgent-vs-early-ercp-cholangitis]] enrolled on obstructive jaundice defined as total bilirubin **≥2.5 mg/dL** plus ALP >1.5 × upper limit of normal plus a dilated duct on cross-sectional imaging. The trial applied a stricter entry threshold than TG18 requires for diagnosis.
- **The criteria are validated for sensitivity only.** Specificity of the TG13/TG18 diagnostic criteria has never been assessed, and the validation cohort was clinically diagnosed cases — so the false-positive rate in an undifferentiated population is unknown.
- **Grade III carries no demonstrated drainage benefit in this dataset**, which sits awkwardly beside the flowchart's instruction to drain severe cholangitis as soon as the patient is stabilised. The article attributes this to the 24-h definition of "urgent" possibly being too late for Grade III, not to drainage being unnecessary.
- **Severity grade does not track mortality in malignant obstruction.** No correlation between TG13 grade and 30-day mortality was observed in cholangitis caused by malignant tumours — consistent with [[jagtap-2026-urgent-vs-early-ercp-cholangitis]], where malignant obstruction rather than drainage timing dominated 30-day mortality.
- **Question numbering in the article is discontinuous** (Q1–Q3, Q5, Q8–Q10 appear; Q4, Q6 and Q7 are not printed), and the recommendation numbers restart by topic. The article prints no legend for its `Recommendation 1/2` labels or its `level A–D` evidence grades.
