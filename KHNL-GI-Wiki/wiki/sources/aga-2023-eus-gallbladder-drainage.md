## Summary

[[acute-cholecystitis|Acute cholecystitis]] is definitively treated by cholecystectomy, but comorbid patients may be inoperable. Percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage (PT-GBD) is the most common nonsurgical decompression method but carries **50%–75% morbidity** and **recurrent cholecystitis in up to 15.4%**, driving development of endoscopic alternatives: **endoscopic transpapillary gallbladder drainage (ET-GBD)** at [[ercp|ERCP]] (cystic duct cannulation + plastic stent) and **[[eus-guided-gallbladder-drainage|EUS-guided gallbladder drainage (EUS-GBD)]]** — transmural stenting from duodenum or stomach into the gallbladder, now facilitated by lumen-apposing metal stents (LAMS).

The Update's core message: endoscopic GBD is a feasible, efficacious alternative to percutaneous drainage for **high-risk surgical candidates**; choosing the optimal drainage route depends on individual patient characteristics (comorbidities, gallbladder position/cystic duct anatomy, in-dwelling metal biliary stents, ascites, future surgical candidacy) and local expertise; EUS-GBD must be done by experts in advanced therapeutic [[endoscopic-ultrasound|EUS]]; and multidisciplinary collaboration with interventional radiology and surgery is paramount. Comparative data (5 studies, 488 patients, incl. the DRAC-1 RCT) show EUS-GBD has technical/clinical success comparable to PT-GBD with shorter stays, less pain, fewer reinterventions, and less recurrent cholecystitis; EUS-GBD also outperforms ET-GBD for technical/clinical success when cystic duct pathology is present.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Description statement (verbatim):** "Endoscopic gallbladder drainage is a feasible and efficacious alternative to percutaneous drainage in the management of acute cholecystitis for high-risk surgical candidates. Endoscopic ultrasound–guided gallbladder drainage and per-oral cholecystoscopy is facilitated by the use of lumen-apposing metal stents. Endoscopic ultrasound–guided gallbladder drainage should be performed by those expert in advanced therapeutic endoscopic ultrasound. Multidisciplinary collaboration between interventional radiology and surgery is paramount in the care of these patients. Choosing the optimal drainage method is dependent on individual patient characteristics."

**Indications for EUS-GBD:**

1. Gallbladder drainage in high-surgical-risk patients with acute cholecystitis
2. Removal of percutaneous cholecystostomy drains in non-cholecystectomy candidates (conversion to internal drainage)
3. Drainage of malignant biliary obstruction in select patients with failed ERCP **and** failed EUS-guided biliary drainage (rescue)

**Patient selection:**

- EUS-GBD candidates: high surgical risk, **no free gallbladder perforation**, able to tolerate anesthesia and a therapeutic endoscopic procedure.
- Favor **ET-GBD first** when: already requiring ERCP for other reasons ([[choledocholithiasis]], suspected cholangitis); potential **future surgical candidate** (preserves normal anatomy); coagulopathy; large-volume [[ascites]]; comorbidities precluding optimal percutaneous drain or EUS-GBD.
- Favor **EUS-GBD** when: in-dwelling **uncovered metal biliary stent obstructing the cystic duct takeoff**; malignant cystic duct obstruction; **large burden of cholelithiasis** or other indication for per-oral cholecystoscopy; poor candidate for repeat interventions.
- **Contraindications to EUS-GBD:** gallbladder perforation, biliary peritonitis, large-volume ascites, significant coagulopathy; intolerance of anesthesia contraindicates all endoscopic GBD.
- **Surgical discussion before EUS-GBD is imperative** — cholecystectomy after EUS-GBD requires fistula repair with postoperative leak risk; clarify future surgical candidacy first.

**Technique of EUS-GBD:**

- LAMS overcame limitations of pigtail plastic/biliary SEMS (leak, contralateral wall injury, migration): short saddle, large flanges, diameters 6–20 mm — allow stone passage and endoscope passage for therapeutic cholecystoscopy. Three LAMS designs in Europe/Asia; only 1 commercially available in the US (Axios, Boston Scientific; fully covered, 10.5F delivery catheter, needs 3.7-mm channel therapeutic echoendoscope; saddle lengths 10 and 15 mm; inner diameters 10, 15, 20 mm; flanges 21, 24, 29 mm).
- **Two techniques:** (1) non–cautery-enhanced: 19-gauge FNA puncture → guidewire → tract dilation → stent under fluoroscopy; (2) direct (cautery-enhanced LAMS): freehand entry and deployment without guidewire/exchange — can be done entirely under EUS guidance without fluoroscopy.
- **Enteral puncture site–to–gallbladder distance must be <10 mm** for safe deployment.
- Prefer **over-the-wire technique** (retained access if misdeployment) in fibrotic/contracted gallbladders, existing percutaneous drains, or difficult scope positions.
- Duodenum vs stomach: no significant AE difference; authors prefer **duodenal** placement (antral LAMS occlude with food more easily; higher inward migration from gastric contraction), but the most important factor is the site of closest gallbladder–GI wall apposition. Distal stomach is easier for surgeons to access if the site later needs closure.
- Coaxial **double-pigtail plastic stent through the LAMS** is routine in the authors' practice (mitigates bleeding, stent obstruction, contralateral wall injury).
- Informed consent: LAMS for GBD is **off-label**; specific AEs = puncture hemorrhage, bile leak with peritonitis, GB perforation, stent migration; late relapsing cholecystitis with migration/occlusion; future cholecystectomy may be harder or impossible.

**Outcomes:**

- EUS-GBD technical success 90%–98.7%; clinical success 89%–98.4%; adverse events 4.8%–22% (bleeding, recurrent cholecystitis, stent migration, occlusion). Meta-analysis: morbidity 20.4%, mortality 3.9% (mostly ongoing sepsis).
- **vs PT-GBD** (5 studies, 488 pts, incl. DRAC-1 RCT): comparable technical/clinical success; PT-GBD had longer stays, longer time to resolution, more reinterventions, higher pain scores, trend to more AEs at 30 d and 1 y; recurrent cholecystitis 20% (8/40) PT-GBD vs 2.6% (1/39) EUS-GBD (P = .029); PT-GBD readmissions from drain leaks/obstruction/dislodgement.
- **vs ET-GBD:** ET-GBD has lower technical (84% vs 98%) and clinical (91% vs 97%) success — cystic duct occlusion (tumor, stone, uncovered metal stent) is the main ET-GBD failure mode; EUS-GBD has lowest recurrent-cholecystitis risk of the 3 modalities (network meta-analysis RR: EUS 1.089 vs PT 2.02 vs ET 2.891), while **ET-GBD had the lowest mortality** (RR: EUS 2.62 vs PT 2.09 vs ET 1.29).

**Follow-up after EUS-GBD — 2 options:**

1. **Per-oral cholecystoscopy at 4–6 weeks** for stone clearance + exchange LAMS for double-pigtail plastic stents to maintain the fistula long-term (technical success 93.1%; 56% spontaneous stone passage; overall stone clearance 88% after mean 1.25 sessions).
2. **Leave LAMS in permanently** (frail patients, refuse second procedure): delayed AEs 7.1% at median 275 d; 3-year stent patency 86%.

**Training:** limited to high-volume centers; minimum ~**19–25 procedures** for competency (published estimates); authors' pathway = master LAMS in pancreatic fluid collections (the FDA-approved indication; ≥10 independent deployments) before EUS-GBD, starting with distended gallbladders without intervening tissue.

**Figure 3 — approach to cholecystitis in high-surgical-risk patients (algorithm):** perforation suspected on imaging or unable to tolerate sedation → PT-GBD. No perforation + tolerates sedation → ET(P)-GBD initially if uncontrolled ascites, potential future surgical candidate, undergoing ERCP for other reasons, or GB >10 mm from GI tract wall; EUS-GBD initially (after surgeon discussion) if duodenal obstruction/gastric outlet obstruction, in-dwelling metal stent occluding cystic duct, or large gallbladder stone burden; each rescues the other on failure, then PT-GBD if both fail. ## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Consistent with [[asge-2024-therapeutic-eus-biliary]] (EUS-GBD favored over percutaneous cholecystostomy in nonsurgical candidates where expertise exists) — this CPU adds the ET-GBD arm and the selection criteria between all 3 routes.
- **Mortality signal diverges from recurrence signal:** network meta-analysis found lowest recurrent cholecystitis with EUS-GBD but lowest mortality with ET-GBD — no head-to-head RCT of ET-GBD vs EUS-GBD; the authors call for randomized/prospective comparisons incl. cost analysis.
- LAMS for gallbladder drainage remains **off-label** (FDA approval pending more outcome/safety data).
- No formal evidence grading (commentary format).
