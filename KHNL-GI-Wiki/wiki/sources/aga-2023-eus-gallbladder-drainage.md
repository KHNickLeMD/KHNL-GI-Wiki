## Summary

[[acute-cholecystitis|Acute cholecystitis]] is definitively treated by cholecystectomy, but comorbid patients may be inoperable. Percutaneous transhepatic gallbladder drainage (PT-GBD) is the most common nonsurgical decompression method but carries **50%–75% morbidity** and **recurrent cholecystitis in up to 15.4%**, driving development of endoscopic alternatives: **endoscopic transpapillary gallbladder drainage (ET-GBD)** at [[ercp|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]] (cystic duct cannulation + plastic stent) and **[[eus-guided-gallbladder-drainage|endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided gallbladder drainage (EUS-GBD)]]** — transmural stenting from duodenum or stomach into the gallbladder, now facilitated by lumen-apposing metal stents (LAMS).

The Update's core message: endoscopic gallbladder drainage (GBD) is a feasible, efficacious alternative to percutaneous drainage for **high-risk surgical candidates**; choosing the optimal drainage route depends on individual patient characteristics (comorbidities, gallbladder position/cystic duct anatomy, in-dwelling metal biliary stents, ascites, future surgical candidacy) and local expertise; EUS-GBD must be done by experts in advanced therapeutic [[endoscopic-ultrasound|EUS]]; and multidisciplinary collaboration with interventional radiology and surgery is paramount. Comparative data (5 studies, 488 patients, incl. the DRAC-1 randomized controlled trial [RCT]) show EUS-GBD has technical/clinical success comparable to PT-GBD with shorter stays, less pain, fewer reinterventions, and less recurrent cholecystitis; EUS-GBD also outperforms ET-GBD for technical/clinical success when cystic duct pathology is present.

## Key Findings / Claims

This Update is a commentary: it issues a single unnumbered **DESCRIPTION** statement and narrative "suggested" advice. It numbers no recommendations and assigns no evidence grades or strength ratings — the authors state the work was not a systematic review and that no formal rating of quality of evidence or strength of the presented considerations was performed. Any numbering or ordering below is the wiki's.

**Description statement (verbatim):** "Endoscopic gallbladder drainage is a feasible and efficacious alternative to percutaneous drainage in the management of acute cholecystitis for high-risk surgical candidates. Endoscopic ultrasound–guided gallbladder drainage and per-oral cholecystoscopy is facilitated by the use of lumen-apposing metal stents. Endoscopic ultrasound–guided gallbladder drainage should be performed by those expert in advanced therapeutic endoscopic ultrasound. Multidisciplinary collaboration between interventional radiology and surgery is paramount in the care of these patients. Choosing the optimal drainage method is dependent on individual patient characteristics."

**Suggested indications for EUS-GBD** (the Update's own term; presented as an unnumbered list):

- Gallbladder drainage in high-surgical-risk patients with acute cholecystitis
- Removal of percutaneous cholecystostomy drains in non-cholecystectomy candidates (conversion to internal drainage)
- Drainage of malignant biliary obstruction in select patients with failed ERCP **and** failed EUS-guided biliary drainage (rescue)

**Patient selection:**

- EUS-GBD candidates: high surgical risk, **no free gallbladder perforation**, able to tolerate anesthesia and a therapeutic endoscopic procedure.
- Favor **ET-GBD first** when: already requiring ERCP for other reasons ([[choledocholithiasis]], suspected cholangitis); potential **future surgical candidate** (preserves normal anatomy); coagulopathy; large-volume [[ascites]]; comorbidities precluding optimal percutaneous drain or EUS-GBD.
- Favor **EUS-GBD** when: in-dwelling **uncovered metal biliary stent obstructing the cystic duct takeoff**; malignant cystic duct obstruction; **large burden of cholelithiasis** or other indication for per-oral cholecystoscopy; poor candidate for repeat interventions.
- **Contraindications to EUS-GBD:** gallbladder perforation, biliary peritonitis, large-volume ascites, significant coagulopathy; intolerance of anesthesia contraindicates all endoscopic GBD.
- **Surgical discussion before EUS-GBD is imperative** — cholecystectomy with fistula repair after EUS-GBD appears safe and feasible in limited data, but carries postoperative leak risk; clarify both future surgical candidacy **and the surgeon's comfort dealing with a transmural stent** before placing one.
- **Route choice overall** (Conclusions) turns on: comorbidities · patient anatomy (gallbladder position, cystic duct characteristics, in-dwelling metal biliary stent) · presence of ascites · potential future surgical candidacy · available local expertise (endoscopic, radiographic, surgical).
  - ET-GBD prioritized: needs ERCP for another reason · large-volume ascites · **drainage needed only as a temporizing measure**.
  - EUS-GBD preferred: poor candidate for repeat intervention · uncovered metal biliary stent or tumor occluding the cystic duct · high-volume cholelithiasis addressable by per-oral cholecystoscopy.

**Table 1 — Summary of procedure description, patient selection, and special consideration for PT-GBD, ET-GBD, and EUS-GBD:**

| | PT-GBD | ET-GBD | EUS-GBD |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Description** | Ultrasound- (or computed tomography [CT]-) guided percutaneous drain placement into the gallbladder | ERCP with selective cannulation of the cystic duct and transpapillary stent placement | EUS-guided placement of a stent from the duodenum or the stomach into the gallbladder |
| **Patient selection** | Conventional treatment in patient with acute cholecystitis and significant medical comorbidities that would make laparoscopic cholecystectomy excessively risky | Consider: patients requiring ERCP for other indications; future cholecystectomy candidates; large-volume ascites; coagulopathy | Consider: in-dwelling uncovered metal biliary stent; malignant obstruction of the cystic duct takeoff; large burden of cholelithiasis |
| **Advantages** | Widely available | Can simultaneously address choledocholithiasis and cholangitis; safer in patients with ascites | Least likely to need reintervention |
| **Disadvantages** | Higher reintervention rate (drain exchanges); higher risk of recurrent cholecystitis; dyscosmesis | Lower technical success; lowest clinical success; risk of pancreatitis; may need stent changes or replacement if migration occurs | May require fistula closure if patient undergoes cholecystectomy; may become occluded with food; contraindicated in gallbladder perforation |

**Technique of EUS-GBD:**

- LAMS overcame limitations of pigtail plastic/biliary self-expandable metal stents (SEMS) (leak, contralateral wall injury, migration): short saddle, large flanges, diameters 6–20 mm — allow stone passage and endoscope passage for therapeutic cholecystoscopy. Three LAMS designs in Europe/Asia; only 1 commercially available in the US (Axios, Boston Scientific; fully covered, 10.5F delivery catheter, needs 3.7-mm channel therapeutic echoendoscope; saddle lengths 10 and 15 mm; inner diameters 10, 15, 20 mm; flanges 21, 24, 29 mm).
- **Two techniques:** (1) non–cautery-enhanced: 19-gauge fine-needle aspiration (FNA) puncture → guidewire → tract dilation → stent under fluoroscopy; (2) direct (cautery-enhanced LAMS): enter the gallbladder directly and deploy through the duodenal/gastric wall without guidewire or device exchange — distal (gallbladder) flange deployed, pulled back snugly, then the duodenal/gastric flange under EUS or endoscopic view. Can be done entirely under EUS guidance **without fluoroscopy unless a salvage maneuver is needed**.
- **Enteral puncture site–to–gallbladder distance must be <10 mm** for safe deployment.
- Prefer **over-the-wire technique** (retained access if misdeployment) in fibrotic/contracted gallbladders, existing percutaneous drains, or difficult scope positions.
- Duodenum vs stomach: no significant adverse event (AE) difference; authors prefer **duodenal** placement (antral LAMS occlude with food more easily; higher inward migration from gastric contraction), but the most important factor is the site of closest gallbladder–gastrointestinal (GI) wall apposition. Distal stomach is easier for surgeons to access if the site later needs closure.
- Coaxial **double-pigtail plastic stent through the LAMS** is routine in the authors' practice (mitigates bleeding, stent obstruction, contralateral wall injury).
- Informed consent: LAMS for GBD is **off-label**; specific AEs = puncture hemorrhage, bile leak with peritonitis, gallbladder (GB) perforation, stent migration; late relapsing cholecystitis with migration/occlusion; future cholecystectomy may be harder or impossible.

**Outcomes:**

- EUS-GBD technical success 90%–98.7%; clinical success 89%–98.4%; AEs 4.8%–22% (bleeding, recurrent cholecystitis, stent migration, occlusion). Meta-analysis morbidity 20.4% (stent dislodgement, stent blockage, leak, peritonitis, pneumoperitoneum, abdominal abscess, recurrent cholecystitis); mortality 3.9%, predominantly ongoing sepsis.
- **vs PT-GBD** (5 studies, 488 pts, incl. DRAC-1 RCT; 4 studies used LAMS, 1 used fully covered SEMS): comparable technical/clinical success; PT-GBD had longer stays, longer time to resolution, more reinterventions, higher pain scores, trend to more AEs at 30 d and 1 y; recurrent cholecystitis 20% (8/40) PT-GBD vs 2.6% (1/39) EUS-GBD (P = .029); PT-GBD readmissions from drain leaks/obstruction/dislodgement.
- **vs ET-GBD:** ET-GBD has lower technical (84% vs 98%) and clinical (91% vs 97%) success — cystic duct occlusion (tumor, stone, uncovered metal stent) is the main ET-GBD failure mode; ET-GBD also cannot treat gallstones and patients may need stent exchanges; EUS-GBD has lowest recurrent-cholecystitis risk of the 3 modalities (network meta-analysis relative risk [RR]: EUS 1.089 vs PT 2.02 vs ET 2.891), while **ET-GBD had the lowest mortality** (RR: EUS 2.62 vs PT 2.09 vs ET 1.29).

**Follow-up after EUS-GBD — 2 options:**

1. **Per-oral cholecystoscopy at 4–6 weeks** for stone clearance + exchange LAMS for double-pigtail plastic stents to maintain the fistula long-term (technical success 93.1%; 56% had spontaneous stone passage, the rest needed stone-retrieval baskets and/or laser lithotripsy; overall stone clearance 88% after mean 1.25 cholecystoscopy sessions, SD 0.46).
2. **Leave LAMS in permanently** (frail patients, refuse second procedure): delayed AEs 7.1% at median 275 d; 3-year stent patency 86%.

**Training:**

- Still limited to centers of high volume and expertise in biliary disease and interventional EUS; requires advanced EUS training.
- Two publications suggest minimum ~**19–25 procedures** for competency.
- Authors' pathway: master LAMS in pancreatic fluid collections (the Food and Drug Administration [FDA]–approved indication) with **≥10 independent successful deployments** before moving to EUS-GBD; start with significantly distended gallbladders with no intervening tissue.
- The authors do not endorse a specific case number — observed learning-curve factors are prior experience, prior training on models, and the individual trainee.

**Figure 3 — approach to cholecystitis in high-surgical-risk patients (algorithm):** perforation suspected on imaging or unable to tolerate sedation → PT-GBD. No perforation + tolerates sedation → ET(P)-GBD initially if uncontrolled ascites, potential future surgical candidate, undergoing ERCP for other reasons, or GB >10 mm from GI tract wall; EUS-GBD initially (after surgeon discussion) if duodenal obstruction/gastric outlet obstruction, in-dwelling metal stent occluding cystic duct, or large gallbladder stone burden; each rescues the other on failure, then PT-GBD if both fail. ## Contradictions / Open Questions

- Consistent with [[asge-2024-therapeutic-eus-biliary]] (EUS-GBD favored over percutaneous cholecystostomy in nonsurgical candidates where expertise exists) — this Clinical Practice Update (CPU) adds the ET-GBD arm and the selection criteria between all 3 routes.
- **Mortality signal diverges from recurrence signal:** network meta-analysis found lowest recurrent cholecystitis with EUS-GBD but lowest mortality with ET-GBD — no head-to-head RCT of ET-GBD vs EUS-GBD; the authors call for randomized/prospective comparisons incl. cost analysis.
- LAMS for gallbladder drainage remains **off-label** (FDA approval pending more outcome/safety data).
- **Scope limit stated by the authors:** despite expanding use (salvage biliary drainage, gallstone removal) and ongoing studies, endoscopic management of cholecystitis and symptomatic gallstones is *not* offered as mainstream treatment — "we are still a long way from abandoning standard of care with cholecystectomy."
- The Update numbers no recommendations and grades no evidence; it is an expert commentary, not a systematic review.
