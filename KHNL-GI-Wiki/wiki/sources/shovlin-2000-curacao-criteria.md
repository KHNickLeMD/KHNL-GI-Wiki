## Summary

Two pages, one table, and the instrument by which hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT) is diagnosed worldwide. [[hht-2020-second-international-guidelines]] makes the Curaçao criteria one of its **two routes to diagnosis** (G1) and asks clinicians to consider HHT when **one or more** are present (G2), but prints them only in a supplement — so this short paper is the only document in `raw/` that states what the four criteria are.

The criteria exist to solve a two-sided problem the authors describe plainly. HHT is easy to recognise in a patient with the classical triad of epistaxis, telangiectasia and a family history — but that accounts for only a proportion of cases, and **subtle manifestations are often overlooked**, leaving silent pulmonary and cerebral arteriovenous malformations (AVMs) untreated when effective therapy exists. In the other direction, within known HHT families there has been concern about **overdiagnosis** — labelling a relative affected on the basis of **epistaxis alone**, which is common in the general population, or on a misread cutaneous vascular lesion. The four-criterion scheme is explicitly **more stringent** than what was used before.

The scoring is a straightforward count of the four: **3 or more = definite**, **2 = possible or suspected**, **fewer than 2 = unlikely**. The middle band is deliberate — "possible" and "suspected" exist to keep clinical suspicion high rather than to close the question, and the authors warn that **fewer than two criteria makes HHT unlikely in an adult but not in a child**, because the disease is age-dependent in its penetrance.

The footnote carries as much decision content as the table. **Every offspring of an affected individual is at risk**, HHT may not manifest until late in life, **coagulation disorders should be excluded**, visceral abnormalities found in a child should prompt careful checking of other family members, and any doubt about whether a physical sign is present should be resolved by an experienced physician rather than assumed either way.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Table I — The Curaçao Criteria (verbatim)**

| The HHT diagnosis is | |
|---|---|
| **Definite** | if **3 criteria** are present |
| **Possible or suspected** | if **2 criteria** are present |
| **Unlikely** | if **fewer than 2** criteria are present |

| # | Criterion | As specified |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Epistaxis** | **Spontaneous, recurrent** nose bleeds |
| **2** | **Telangiectases** | **Multiple**, at characteristic sites: **lips · oral cavity · fingers · nose** |
| **3** | **Visceral lesions** | Such as: **gastrointestinal telangiectasia** (with or without bleeding) · **pulmonary AVM** · **hepatic AVM** · **cerebral AVM** · **spinal AVM** |
| **4** | **Family history** | A **first-degree relative** with HHT according to these criteria |

**Footnote to the table (decision content, not ornament)**

- **All offspring of an affected individual are at risk** of having the disease, since **HHT may not manifest until late in life**.
- **If there is any concern regarding the presence of a physical sign, an experienced physician should be consulted.**
- **Coagulation disorders should be excluded.**
- **Visceral abnormalities in children should prompt a particularly careful check of other family members.**
- The authors expected the criteria to be **refined as molecular diagnostic tests became available**.

**How the authors intend the criteria to be used**

- **No numerical threshold is set for epistaxis** — there is no agreement on the precise number of episodes or severity needed. The criterion "merely highlights that epistaxis should occur **spontaneously on more than one occasion**"; **night-time bleeds are particularly suspicious**.
- **Telangiectases are defined by site, not by count** — the characteristic lesions have a predilection for the sites listed, so specifying site avoids a numerical threshold.
- **Visceral criteria may manifest predictably**, by **hemorrhage or by radiological detection**.
- ⚠ **Anemia alone does not contribute to the diagnosis** — stated explicitly.
- **Within HHT families, a firm diagnosis can be made on two separate visceral manifestations** — but **this does not apply to the general population**. Strict criteria are required to diagnose an affected relative.
- The diagnosis of HHT **remains a clinical one**: locus heterogeneity and largely family-unique mutations complicate molecular diagnosis. Known disease genes at publication were ***endoglin*** (chromosome 9) and ***ALK-1*** (chromosome 12).
- Visceral involvement is **frequently asymptomatic at the time of radiological detection**; pulmonary AVMs may cause neurological sequelae through paradoxical embolism across right-to-left shunts, and hepatic AVMs may mimic high-output cardiac failure or cause porto-caval shunting.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No contradiction with [[hht-2020-second-international-guidelines]]**, which adopts these criteria unchanged; the 2020 document is the higher-priority and newer source for everything downstream of diagnosis, and this paper is cited by it.
- ⚠ **The "unlikely" band does not apply to children.** HHT is age-dependent in penetrance, so fewer than two criteria in a child of an affected parent is **not** reassuring — [[hht-2020-second-international-guidelines]] G3 handles this by treating such a child as **possible HHT unless excluded by genetic testing**.
- **The criteria are counted, not weighted**, and the paper sets no threshold for how much epistaxis or how many telangiectases count — a deliberate choice, but it leaves the judgement with the clinician.
- Published in 2000 and explicitly expected to be refined as molecular testing matured; the 2020 guideline retains them unaltered.
