## Summary

ESD is a mature endoscopic resection technique, standard in Japan and nearby Asian countries for early luminal GI neoplasia, whose adoption in the United States has been slow. This expert review frames the current US practice of ESD around 16 best practice advice points spanning organ-specific indications, post-resection coagulation and closure, specimen handling and pathology, training, and the education of referring physicians.

The core rationale is a size limit: because every form of EMR uses a snare, the specimen that can be removed safely en bloc is generally limited to **1.5 to 2 cm**. Larger lesions must be taken piecemeal, and piecemeal resection leads to higher rates of residual/recurrent neoplasia and cannot reliably establish R0 status. ESD dissects with an electrosurgical knife and therefore removes large, irregular lesions in one piece, with high en-bloc and R0 rates, which in turn permits accurate histopathologic staging (margin involvement, differentiation, lymphovascular invasion, depth of submucosal invasion, tumor budding).

Indications are organ-specific (Table 2). Early gastric cancer is the best-established indication, built on the Japanese Gastric Cancer Association absolute and expanded criteria and their measured lymph-node-metastasis (LNM) risks. In the esophagus, ESD is the primary modality for squamous dysplasia and cancer confined to superficial mucosa, and is used selectively in [[barretts-esophagus|Barrett's esophagus]] where EMR cannot achieve en-bloc removal or where preprocedural histology is equivocal. In the colorectum, most lesions are adequately treated by EMR; ESD is reserved for lesions with features predicting submucosal invasion, fibrosis, or residual/recurrent disease. Duodenal ESD carries the highest complication burden and is explicitly discouraged early in a practitioner's ESD career.

The update also addresses the US-specific barriers to ESD (Table 1) — underestimation of need, referral patterns favoring surgery, steep learning curve, scarcity of experts and training centers, limited magnification chromoendoscopy, reimbursement, and Western pathologists' unfamiliarity with ESD specimens — and proposes a stepwise Western training model in place of the Japanese master–apprentice pathway.

## Key Recommendations (Best Practice Advice 1–16, verbatim)

> **Best Practice Advice 1**: Endoscopic submucosal dissection should be recognized as a mature endoscopic technique that enables complete removal of lesions that are too large for en bloc endoscopic mucosal resection or are at increased risk of containing cancer.

> **Best Practice Advice 2**: The safety and feasibility of endoscopic submucosal dissection for early gastric cancer is well established. The absolute indications for curative endoscopic resection include moderately and well-differentiated, nonulcerated, mucosal lesions that are ≤2 cm in size.

> **Best Practice Advice 3**: Other relative (expanded) indications for gastric endoscopic submucosal dissection include moderately and well-differentiated superficial cancers that are >2 cm, lesions ≤3 cm with ulceration or that contain early submucosal invasion, and poorly differentiated superficial cancers ≤2 cm in size. The risk of lymph node metastasis when endoscopic submucosal dissection is performed for these indications is higher than when it is performed for absolute indications but remains acceptably low.

> **Best Practice Advice 4**: Endoscopic submucosal dissection may be considered in selected patients with Barrett's esophagus with the following features: large or bulky area of nodularity, lesions with a high likelihood of superficial submucosal invasion, recurrent dysplasia, endoscopic mucosal resection specimen showing invasive carcinoma with positive margins, equivocal preprocedural histology, and intramucosal carcinoma.

> **Best Practice Advice 5**: Endoscopic submucosal dissection is the primary modality for treatment of squamous cell dysplasia and cancer confined to the superficial esophageal mucosa. Any degree of submucosal invasion caries an increased risk of lymph node metastasis and alternative/additional therapy should be considered. *(spelling "caries" as printed in the source)*

> **Best Practice Advice 6**: Duodenal endoscopic submucosal dissection is associated with an increased risk of intraprocedural perforation and delayed adverse events. Duodenal endoscopic submucosal dissection should be limited to endoscopists with extensive experience in performing endoscopic submucosal dissection in other locations. It is strongly suggested that endoscopists in the United States refrain from performing duodenal endoscopic submucosal dissection during the early phase of their endoscopic submucosal dissection practice.

> **Best Practice Advice 7**: All colorectal lesions should be evaluated for suitability for endoscopic resection. Accumulating evidence has shown that the majority of colorectal neoplasms without signs of deep submucosal invasion or advanced cancer can be treated by advanced endoscopic resection techniques.

> **Best Practice Advice 8**: Colorectal neoplasms containing dysplasia confined to the mucosa have no risk for lymph node metastasis and endoscopic resection should be considered as the criterion standard.

> **Best Practice Advice 9**: Large (>2 cm) colorectal lesions frequently (>43%) require piecemeal removal when endoscopic mucosal resection is used, which is associated with increased (up to 20%) rates of recurrent neoplasia. Endoscopic submucosal dissection enables higher rates of en bloc resection and lower recurrence rates for these lesions. Patients with large complex colorectal polyps should be referred to a high-volume, specialized center for endoscopic removal by endoscopic mucosal resection or endoscopic submucosal dissection.

> **Best Practice Advice 10**: Endoscopic resection for colorectal lesions offers significant cost benefit compared with surgery, and case-based endoscopic submucosal dissection selection for high-risk lesions could offer cost savings.

> **Best Practice Advice 11**: Endoscopists in the United States embarking on performing endoscopic submucosal dissection should be familiar with currently available endoscopic tissue closure devices. Both clip closure and endoscopic suturing techniques have been shown to be effective in managing intraprocedural perforation. Complete closure of a post–endoscopic submucosal dissection site may be considered in certain circumstances based on patient factors, procedural factors, and the location of the lesion.

> **Best Practice Advice 12**: Careful coagulation of exposed blood vessels in the resection site may reduce the risk of delayed bleeding after endoscopic submucosal dissection. The use of low-voltage coagulation current is recommended for this technique.

> **Best Practice Advice 13**: Endoscopists should affix the endoscopic submucosal dissection specimen to a flat surface (eg, pin the specimen to cork board) and immerse it in formalin. An expert gastrointestinal pathologist should evaluate the specimen for margin involvement, degree of differentiation, presence or absence of lymphovascular invasion, depth of submucosal invasion (if present), and tumor budding.

> **Best Practice Advice 14**: Acquiring high-level competency in endoscopic submucosal dissection is achievable in the United States. Alternative educational models should be used in the United States because of the limited number of experts and the differing prevalence of gastrointestinal luminal diseases as compared with Asia.

> **Best Practice Advice 15**: The endoscopic submucosal dissection educational model most suited for the current environment in the United States is a stepwise approach consisting of didactic self-study, attending training courses with increasing levels of complexity, self-practice on animal models, and observation of live cases performed by experts. Endoscopists should perform their initial endoscopic submucosal dissections on patients with lesions that have well-established indications for endoscopic submucosal dissection and are of the lowest technical complexity.

> **Best Practice Advice 16**: Endoscopists in the United States who perform endoluminal resection should educate referring physicians to avoid practices that may induce submucosal fibrosis hampering future endoscopic mucosal resection or endoscopic submucosal dissection. These practices include tattooing in close proximity to or beneath a lesion for marking and partial snare resection of a portion of a lesion for histopathology.

## Table 1 — Factors Impeding ESD Training in the United States

| Domain | Factors |
|---|---|
| **Underestimating the need and benefit from ESD** | Reluctance to accept the need and benefit from ESD in the United States where gastric cancer is relatively uncommon; bias of medical and surgical oncologists toward surgical resection; established patient-referral patterns among endoscopists geared toward sending patients with more complex superficial luminal lesions to surgery; bias of advanced endoscopists toward EMR |
| **Procedure-related factors** | Steep learning curve; ESD requires a higher level of expertise to endoscopically evaluate and select appropriate lesions; requires in-depth understanding of electrosurgical principals; time-consuming procedure; higher rate of adverse events compared with EMR; slow release of ESD tools in the United States; scarcity of scopes with optical magnification; limited options for chromoendoscopy in the United States; lack of proper reimbursement |
| **Training-related factors** | Lack of ESD experts; lack of ESD training centers; lack of easier-to-remove gastric lesions to gain experience early in the learning curve; Western pathologists are unaccustomed to evaluating ESD specimens |

## Key Findings / Claims

**Why ESD instead of EMR**
- Because all forms of EMR use a snare, the specimen that can be removed safely en bloc is **generally limited to 1.5 to 2 cm**. Larger lesions must be removed piecemeal → increased residual/recurrent neoplasia.

**Gastric**
- JGCA **absolute (standard) indication**: differentiated adenocarcinoma limited to mucosa (T1a), **≤2 cm**, **without ulceration** — extremely low LNM risk. Landmark basis: Gotoda 2000, 5265 gastrectomy-with-node-dissection patients, which characterized expanded-criteria groups with negligible LNM.
- Meta-analysis of **9798 patients**: LNM incidence **0.2% for absolute criteria vs 0.7% for expanded criteria**.
- Within the **4 expanded-criteria categories**, **undifferentiated mucosal** lesions and **submucosal** lesions had significantly higher LNM (**2.6%** and **2.5%**, respectively) than absolute-criteria lesions — so these two subcategories should be balanced against the risk of surgical resection.
- **Western LNM risk may be higher than Asian**, with limited US data. US SEER cohort (1577 patients, early gastric adenocarcinoma): for **well/moderately differentiated (low grade, G1, G2) T1a**, LNM **1.7% for tumors <2 cm**, **<4.5% for tumors <4 cm**, **20% for tumors ≥4 cm**. For **low-grade T1b**, LNM **began at 8.4% for tumors <1 cm** and increased dramatically with size.
- In the United States, ESD should be considered **first-line therapy for visible, endoscopically resectable, superficial gastric neoplasia**; indistinct margins, relative gastric-wall thickness, and ESD precision make it **superior to EMR for lesions larger than 1 cm**.

**Barrett's esophagus**
- Both EMR and ESD can be applied; **EMR is considered the first-line therapy for BE**. High-quality direct EMR-vs-ESD comparisons are scarce.
- **RCT (N = 40)** in HGD or early adenocarcinoma with **lesion size ≤3 cm**: **ESD gave higher R0 and curative resection rates than EMR**; over **short-term (3 mo)** follow-up there was **no difference in complete remission**; long-term recurrence/recurrence-free survival were not evaluated.
- **Curative endoscopic resection in BE** requires: negative lateral **and** deep margins, **well- to moderately differentiated** histology, **no lymphovascular invasion**, and **no submucosal invasion or only superficial (<500 µm)** submucosal invasion.
- **High-risk for LNM**: positive deep margins, infiltration **deep into the submucosa (≥500 µm)**, poorly differentiated histology, or lymphovascular invasion.
- Significant discrepancies between **preresection and postresection histology in 30% to 60% of cases** — which is why ESD (more accurate histopathologic evaluation) is considered when an unrecognized invasive component is possible.
- If an EMR specimen shows neoplasia at a **deep margin**, extant residual neoplasia should be assumed and surgical, systemic, or additional endoscopic therapy considered; **ESD is the best-suited endoscopic modality to provide that additional therapy**.

**Esophageal SCC**
- SCC has a **higher propensity for LNM at earlier stages** than BE-related neoplasia → accurate histopathologic evaluation matters even more; the aim of endoscopic resection in esophageal SCC should **always be en bloc removal with ESD as first-line therapy**.
- ESD has repeatedly been shown **superior to EMR** for en bloc, R0, and curative resection with lower recurrence in esophageal SCC.

**Duodenal**
- Duodenal wall is **highly vascular, has a thin muscularis propria, and is exposed to gastric acid, bile, and pancreatic juice** → duodenal ESD carries high rates of immediate and delayed bleeding and of perforation.

**Colorectal**
- Colorectal mucosa **does not possess lymphatic drainage** → in situ/intramucosal colorectal cancer is considered equivalent to HGD, and dysplasia confined to the mucosa carries **no LNM risk**.
- **The first attempt at endoscopic resection has the highest rate of success** — attempting EMR without high confidence that complete resection is achievable should be avoided.
- For colorectal lesions **≥2 cm**, en bloc resection by EMR was impossible in **more than 43%** of cases. Piecemeal resection → neoplastic recurrence **10%–20%** vs **1%–2%** with en bloc ESD (**odds ratio 8.2**).
- Japanese multicenter study of colorectal polyps ≥2 cm: **9.9%** were staged T1 after excluding lesions with features of deeply invasive cancer; **two thirds of those T1 carcinomas were confined to superficial submucosa (<1000 µm)** and potentially curable by ESD.
- For selected early colorectal cancers with **<1000 µm submucosal invasion and favorable histologic features**, en bloc R0 ESD may obviate additional surgery.
- Australian Colonic Endoscopic Resection data — features associated with submucosal invasive cancer in lesions **≥20 mm**: **type V Kudo pit pattern, depressed component (Paris 0-IIc), complex morphology (0-Is or 0-IIa+Is), rectosigmoid location, nongranular surface morphology, and increasing size**.
- US **NCCN** guidance: colon/rectal polyps containing invasive cancer removed endoscopically with a **"fragmented specimen or margin[s] [that] cannot be assessed" undergo additional surgical resection**.
- ESGE: ESD can be considered for colorectal lesions with **high suspicion of limited submucosal invasion, particularly those >20 mm**, or those that cannot otherwise be optimally and radically removed by snare-based techniques. JGES: colorectal ESD for lesions where en bloc snare-based EMR would be difficult, mucosal tumors with submucosal fibrosis, locally residual or recurrent early carcinoma after endoscopic resection, and sporadic lesions in chronic inflammation.
- High-quality EMR remains an essential skill and will likely remain the procedure of choice for endoscopic resection of **noninvasive** colorectal neoplasia in most US settings.

**Coagulation and closure after ESD**
- Careful inspection and coagulation of **exposed nonbleeding visible vessels** (little/no overlying submucosa) in the post-ESD site is recommended to prevent delayed bleeding in the **upper GI tract**; effectiveness after **large colorectal** resections is **equivocal**.
- Use **low-voltage ("soft") coagulation current** to reduce perforation risk and postpolypectomy burn syndrome.
- Standard endoclips close most ESD perforations (typically small). **Over-the-scope clip systems potentially can close large luminal defects approaching 2 cm** in size.
- Comparative study of **21 patients** with iatrogenic colonic perforations (11 from ESD, 7 from EMR): **all 5** patients closed with endoclips had worsening abdominal pain and **4 required laparoscopic surgery**; only **2 of 16** in the endoscopic suturing group had worsening pain (small study).

**Specialized pathology**
- ESD specimens should be **pinned/needled to a flat surface before fixation** — unpinned EMR specimens curl, distorting the muscularis mucosae; pinning yields straight edges and a nondistorted muscularis mucosae layer (Figure 1).
- Interobserver and intraobserver variability in pathologic evaluation of dysplastic lesions and early cancers is documented throughout the GI tract → all ESD specimens should be evaluated by an **expert GI pathologist**, and **evaluation by a second expert GI pathologist should be given strong consideration**.

**Training and practice in the United States**
- The Japanese **master–apprentice** model is not applicable in the US (Table 1). No standardized US ESD training approach exists; the commonly followed pathway is ESD courses → self-study and animal-model practice → observation at a high-volume Asian center.
- Suitable first lesions in the US are typically located in the **rectum**, because early gastric cancer is uncommon; **colorectal ESD can be performed safely and effectively by an endoscopist with no previous experience in gastric ESD** (contrasting with Japanese practice, where colorectal ESD is reserved for trainees with extensive gastric ESD experience).
- Endoscopists should strongly consider performing their **initial ESDs on patients who are at least fair surgical candidates** and have lesions with well-established indications of lowest technical complexity.
- A crucial component of responsible US practice will be development of **benchmarks and quality metrics** — en bloc, R0, and curative resection rates plus acceptable adverse-event rates. Asian benchmarks may not be directly applicable to the US.
- ESD use is included in **NCCN guidelines** for treatment of superficial GI malignancies; as of 2016 there were **more than 2500 PubMed publications** related to ESD.

**Education of referring physicians**
- ESD success depends on the degree of **fibrosis**; fibrotic lesions have **lower en bloc resection rates and higher bleeding and perforation rates**.
- Practices to avoid: **ink/tattoo injection immediately under or close by a lesion**, **extensive biopsies**, and **partial snare polypectomy**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Barrett's first-line modality.** This update states "EMR is considered the first-line therapy for BE" while also giving a list of BE features that favor ESD (BPA 4). [[asge-2023-esd|ASGE 2023]] later frames the choice by lesion size (ESD over EMR for EAC >20 mm). Per source priority, the newer ASGE 2023 guideline governs the size-based technique choice; this update contributes the **lesion-feature** indications (nodularity, equivocal histology, recurrent dysplasia, positive-margin EMR specimen), which ASGE 2023 does not enumerate.
- **Barrett's size threshold.** Table 2 here suggests ESD for BE T1a lesions **≥15 mm** not amenable to en bloc EMR; [[asge-2023-esd|ASGE 2023]] uses **>20 mm** for EAC. Both are stated on their own pages; the newer guideline's threshold is what the entity pages assert.
- **Gastric expanded criteria and poorly differentiated lesions.** BPA 3 includes "poorly differentiated superficial cancers ≤2 cm"; [[asge-2023-esd|ASGE 2023]] directs **poorly differentiated gastric cancer of any size toward surgical evaluation**. Newer guideline governs; the discrepancy is noted on [[endoscopic-submucosal-dissection]].
- The update gives **no numeric adverse-event rates** for duodenal ESD; [[asge-2015-ampullary-duodenal-adenomas|ASGE 2015]] supplies the 23%–35% perforation figure already on [[nonampullary-duodenal-adenoma]].
