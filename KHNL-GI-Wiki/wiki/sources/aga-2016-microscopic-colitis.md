## Summary

Official AGA Institute recommendations on the **medical management** of microscopic colitis, developed with GRADE and accompanied by a separate technical review (Pardi et al., *Gastroenterology* 2016;150:247–274). Nine recommendations cover induction of clinical remission and maintenance therapy.

Scope is deliberately narrow: the guideline addresses **medical treatment only** and **does not address diagnosis, surgical management, or the appropriateness of screening for associated autoimmune disorders**. It therefore gives **no histologic diagnostic criteria** (no collagen band thickness, no intraepithelial lymphocyte counts). Because the technical review found outcomes did not differ between lymphocytic and collagenous colitis, **the recommendations do not distinguish between the two subtypes**.

The therapeutic frame is symptomatic: microscopic colitis is not associated with increased mortality, and there is no evidence that persistent histologic inflammation portends long-term unfavorable outcomes such as colorectal cancer or need for surgery. The goal of medical therapy is therefore to **relieve symptoms and improve quality of life while minimizing drug-related adverse effects**. Consistent with this, it is **generally not necessary to perform colonoscopy to assess histologic response**.

Budesonide is first line for induction (and, in relapsers, for maintenance). Mesalamine, bismuth salicylate and prednisolone/prednisone are conditional alternatives **only when budesonide is not feasible**. The guideline recommends **against** cholestyramine-plus-mesalamine combination therapy, *Boswellia serrata*, and probiotics. Because of the absence of clinical trial data, it **did not address medical treatment of corticosteroid-refractory microscopic colitis**; it notes only that very limited case-series evidence suggests azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine and anti-TNF agents may benefit such patients.

### Dosing, durations, and supporting data

- **Budesonide induction — 9 mg daily.** Meta-analysis of 6 RCTs: clear benefit in inducing clinical response, 5 studies also showing histologic response; 2 showed QoL improvement (difference not statistically significant). Patients on budesonide 9 mg daily were **more than twice as likely to achieve clinical remission over an average of 7 to 13 days** vs no treatment (**RR 2.52; 95% CI 1.45–4.4**). Risk of serious adverse events is low.
- **Stopping induction — cessation can be considered after 8 weeks of therapy.** **One-third of patients will remain symptom-free thereafter** and not require maintenance therapy.
- **Budesonide 9 mg daily vs mesalamine 3 g daily** (high-quality trial): budesonide patients were **nearly twice as likely** to achieve clinical **and** histologic remission; **no statistically significant difference in adverse events**.
- **Mesalamine vs no treatment:** single RCT, moderate quality — mesalamine associated with a **lower** likelihood of clinical response vs no treatment (**OR 0.74; 95% CI 0.44–1.24**), not statistically significant; serious imprecision, so benefit is uncertain. In 2 other trials with mesalamine in the control arm the clinical response rate was **84% and 87%**; in a third it was **44%**.
- **Bismuth salicylate:** small RCT — **all 7 patients** in the intervention arm responded vs **none of 7** controls; 3-fold higher likelihood of histologic response (not statistically significant). No adverse events reported, but long-term salicylate/bismuth toxicity is unknown, and **8 to 9 tablets divided 3 times daily** is a significant pill burden in an older population.
- **Prednisolone (or prednisone):** very small RCT — **22% clinical response among 9 patients** in the intervention arm vs **none of 3** controls. Serious imprecision and risk of bias.
- ***Boswellia serrata*:** single RCT — **44% of 16 patients** improved clinically vs **27% of 15** on placebo; no QoL difference; **adverse events were more frequent**; all outcomes not statistically significant. Panel also concerned about access to a standardized formulation.
- **Probiotics:** small RCT of *Lactobacillus acidophilus*, *Bifidobacterium animalis*, and *lactis* strains vs no treatment — uncertain benefit for clinical remission, histologic response and QoL due to serious imprecision.
- **Cholestyramine + mesalamine:** single RCT (low quality) failed to show incremental clinical benefit from adding cholestyramine to mesalamine. Feasibility also weighed against it (interferes with absorption of other medications, especially with polypharmacy in older patients). **The panel explicitly does not comment on cholestyramine monotherapy**, which has not been evaluated in trials.
- **Budesonide maintenance — 6 mg daily for 6 months:** 2 RCTs, moderate quality — **66% lower relative risk of clinical relapse (RR 0.34; 95% CI 0.19–0.6)**; also maintained histologic response and QoL. **A lower dose (3 mg daily alternating with 6 mg daily) over 12 months** showed similar efficacy in maintaining clinical response.
- **Who gets maintenance:** only patients who have had a **clinical relapse after cessation of induction therapy**. Maintenance dosing may start at 6 mg but **in clinical practice is commonly tapered to the lowest effective dose**; **cessation of maintenance can be considered after 6 to 12 months**.
- **Bone health:** although systemic bioavailability of budesonide is low, **prolonged use may predispose to bone loss** — osteoporosis prevention and screening should be considered in patients requiring maintenance therapy.

### Cost and sequencing considerations

- Budesonide is **expensive**; alternative therapies may be considered if cost is a determining factor. Because **costs are similar between mesalamine and budesonide**, cost is **not** likely to be a determining factor in choosing mesalamine.
- A trial of mesalamine may be appropriate for patients with a **contraindication to**, **poor response to**, or **strong preference against** budesonide.
- Bismuth salicylate is positioned as a **second-line alternative** for select patients with contraindications to corticosteroids or for whom cost is a determining factor.
- **Prednisolone is considerably less expensive than budesonide** and may be an alternative when budesonide cost is prohibitive; it may be considered in patients with **refractory symptoms after budesonide**, once other coexisting etiologies such as celiac disease have been excluded. In most cases it should not be used first line.

### Epidemiology, scope, and diagnostic context stated by the guideline

- Chronic watery diarrhea caused by inflammation in the colon, **diagnosed by colonic biopsy**; **predilection for those 60 years of age or older**; two subtypes (lymphocytic and collagenous) with a **female predominance in collagenous colitis**.
- **Reported prevalence 48 to 219 per 100,000.**
- **Occurs in 7.5% of patients undergoing evaluation for chronic diarrhea** — so it would be prudent to perform colonoscopy **with biopsies of multiple segments of the colon**. If flexible sigmoidoscopy is performed instead, **obtain biopsy specimens from the descending colon in addition to the rectosigmoid**, because rectosigmoid specimens may not reveal the disease in some cases.
- **Not associated with increased mortality**; no evidence that persistence of histologic inflammation portends colorectal cancer or need for surgery.
- **Ongoing symptoms despite medical therapy** → consider **coexisting causes of chronic diarrhea such as celiac disease**; residual bowel symptoms may reflect coexisting or postinflammatory **functional bowel disorders**. For patients with residual symptoms after budesonide, **normal colonic biopsy specimens may be suggestive of coexisting irritable bowel syndrome or celiac disease**.
- **Refractory patients** → evaluate potential **medication triggers: nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, proton pump inhibitors, and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors**.
- **Corticosteroid-refractory disease was not addressed** (no clinical trial data). Very limited case-series evidence suggests **azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine, and anti–tumor necrosis factor agents** may benefit these patients; the AGA encourages prospective trials.
- The guideline also calls for trials of **lower-cost alternatives such as antidiarrheal agents (e.g. loperamide) and cholestyramine monotherapy** with cost-effectiveness analyses; the role of combination therapies has yet to be fully explored.
