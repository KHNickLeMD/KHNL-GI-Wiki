## Summary

The **canonical endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) quality-indicator set** for US practice, and the **third iteration** from the ACG/ASGE Task Force on Quality in Endoscopy — superseding the task force's **2006** and **2015** ERCP documents. Literature search ran January 2014 (the date of the last update) through **February 2025**. It defines **13 numbered indicators**, each carrying a **strength of recommendation**, a **measure type** (process or outcome) and a **numeric performance target**, split across preprocedure, intraprocedure and postprocedure phases. **Five are named priority indicators** — #1 (appropriate, documented indication), #4 (deep cannulation of the native papilla), #6 (extrahepatic bile duct stone extraction), #7 (rectal nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug [NSAID]) and #9 (unplanned hospital visit within 30 days) — and the task force advises units to start there before adding the rest.

The document names **four changes from the 2015 version** as the important ones: a **more restrictive approach to ERCP indications**; a **clear endorsement of rectal NSAIDs in average-risk patients with an intact ampullary sphincter**, reversing the prior restriction to high-risk cases; a **new indicator tracking the use or nonuse of prophylactic pancreatic stent placement (PSP)** in high-risk cases; and a deliberate shift of emphasis onto **unplanned events** — early repeat biliary intervention and unexpected hospitalization — as the postprocedure outcome measures, on the grounds that they are more reliably defined than the alternatives.

One denominator change is easy to miss and changes who is being measured. The **stone-extraction indicator is no longer restricted to stones smaller than 1 cm**, as it was in 2015: the denominator is now **all stones in the extrahepatic bile duct after successful cannulation**, because high-quality ERCP is expected to include proficiency with at least some advanced extraction techniques. A provider's rate computed the old way is not comparable to the >90% target.

Grades are the task force's **own 8-level scheme (1A, 1B, 1C+, 1C, 2A, 2B, 2C, 3)**, adapted from Guyatt, **not GRADE** — the same scheme the companion colonoscopy document uses, and the same vocabulary trap. A **"3" means expert opinion only**, not a third-strength GRADE rating. The strength of evidence for each indicator was set **by author consensus**.

The document also reprints, as its **Table 2**, the 19 quality indicators **common to all gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopic procedures**, which are issued and discussed in a **separate companion article** (Elmunzer et al., *Am J Gastroenterol* 2026;121:1781–91 / *Gastrointest Endosc* 2024;100:382–94). Those indicators are cited here to that companion document, not to this one.

## Key Findings / Claims

### The 13 ERCP quality indicators (Table 1)

Strength and target exactly as the document gives them. **Priority indicators marked ★.**

| # | Indicator | Strength | Measure | Target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Preprocedure** | | | | |
| 1 ★ | ERCP performed for an accepted indication, and that indication documented | 1C+ | Process | >98% |
| 2 | Informed consent obtained, **including a specific discussion of the risks of ERCP**, and documented | 3 | Process | >98% |
| 3 | Prophylactic antibiotics given for appropriate indications | 2B | Process | >98% |
| **Intraprocedure** | | | | |
| 4 ★ | Deep cannulation of the duct of interest achieved in patients with **native papillae and standard foregut anatomy** | 1C | Outcome | >90% |
| 5 | Radiation exposure documented and measures used to reduce exposure to patients and staff | 1C | Process | >98% |
| 6 ★ | **Extrahepatic bile duct stones in patients with normal biliary anatomy extracted successfully** and documented | 1B | Outcome | >90% |
| 7 ★ | **Rectal indomethacin or diclofenac given to patients with an intact papilla** | 1A | Process | >90% |
| 8 | **Use or nonuse of prophylactic pancreatic stent placement** for post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP) prevention in high-risk cases documented and tracked | 1A | Process | >98% |
| **Postprocedure** | | | | |
| 9 ★ | Unplanned hospital visit within **30 days** of ERCP | 1C | Outcome | **<15%** |
| 10 | Unplanned biliary intervention (e.g., salvage percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage or repeat ERCP) within **30 days** | 1C | Outcome | **<15%** |
| 11 | PEP rate documented and tracked | 3 | Process | >95% |
| 12 | Clinically significant hemorrhage after sphincterotomy or sphincteroplasty documented and tracked | 3 | Process | >95% |
| 13 | Cholangitis and cholecystitis within 30 days documented and tracked | 3 | Process | >95% |

> **Note the measure type on #11–13.** These are **process** indicators — the target is the frequency with which the event is *documented and tracked*, not the event rate itself. The task force says so explicitly: a fair single benchmark for the PEP rate "may not be feasible or justified", so it chose complete tracking instead, with the expectation that the rates are then benchmarked **within groups having similar case mixes**.

### Strength-of-recommendation scheme (Table 3)

| Grade | Clarity of benefit | Supporting evidence | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1A | Clear | RCTs without important limitations | Strong; applies to most clinical settings |
| 1B | Clear | RCTs with important limitations | Strong; likely applies to most practice settings |
| 1C+ | Clear | Overwhelming observational evidence | Strong; applies to most practice settings in most situations |
| 1C | Clear | Observational studies | Intermediate strength; may change when stronger evidence is available |
| 2A | Unclear | RCTs without important limitations | Intermediate; best action may differ by circumstance or patient/societal values |
| 2B | Unclear | RCTs with important limitations | Weak; alternatives may be better in some circumstances |
| 2C | Unclear | Observational studies | Very weak; alternatives are likely better in some circumstances |
| 3 | Unclear | **Expert opinion only** | Weak; likely to change as data become available |

### Accepted indications for ERCP (Table 4)

Explicitly **not an inclusive list** — ERCP may be performed for other indications when the risk–benefit ratio is favourable.

- **Biliary:** choledocholithiasis · cholangitis · bile leak · biliary stricture (malignant or benign) · obstructive jaundice · placement of a transpapillary gallbladder stent · ampullary mass · biliary obstruction due to papillary stenosis · exchange or removal of a stent placed for an accepted biliary indication.
- **Pancreatic:** therapy of a symptomatic pancreatic duct stone · pancreatic duct leak · recurrent acute pancreatitis with **suspected papillary stenosis** · adjunctive management of pancreatic fluid collections · investigation of intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm · exchange or removal of a stent placed for an accepted pancreatic indication.

### What counts as a successful cannulation, and what does not (indicator 4)

The denominator rules are the decision content here, because they decide whether a difficult case counts against the operator:

- **Advanced techniques count as success** — double-guidewire, cannulation over a pancreas stent, needle-knife sphincterotomy, fistulotomy. The task force recommends endoscopists performing ERCP be familiar with one or more of them.
- **Endoscopic ultrasound-guided biliary drainage (EUS-BD) after a failed attempt is an unsuccessful ERCP.** Such patients are in the **denominator but not the numerator**.
- **Duodenal obstruction preventing access to the papilla counts as a failure** — the task force acknowledges this does not reflect the endoscopist's technical skill, but kept the rigid definition to standardize measurement.
- **Modified upper GI anatomy is excluded** (e.g., Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, Billroth II), since ERCP is significantly more challenging there.

> Benchmark data behind the >90% target: a US registry of 85 high-volume endoscopists reported an overall cannulation rate **>80%**, with **83.5% of participants above 90%**; 22 recently graduated advanced-endoscopy trainees achieved a cohort native-papilla rate of **93.1%**, but only **77.3% of the independent endoscopists individually exceeded 90%**. The Netherlands' mandatory registry gives an overall native-papilla rate of **90.7%**, with many endoscopists below 90% and several below 85%, clustering among lower-volume providers. **There is no agreed definition of a low-volume ERCP operator** — a meta-analysis of 13 studies used thresholds ranging from <25 to <156 ERCPs annually.

> [!pitfall] Persisting to hit the cannulation target can hurt the patient
> The task force states plainly that >90% is desirable but that **overambitious persistence may unacceptably increase pancreatitis, perforation and sedation-related risk**, and that returning on a different day after papillary oedema has resolved is associated with a high success rate. When to pivot to an alternative strategy, and how long to persist, **require further study** — the document sets no time limit.

### Stone extraction (indicator 6)

- Most stones are **<10 mm** and come out with conventional sphincterotomy and balloon extraction. Harder: stones **above the biliary confluence**, retained in the **cystic duct**, **impacted**, **multiple stacked**, or in a **nondilated or tapered distal duct**, and any case needing needle-knife sphincterotomy for access.
- **Complete clearance at the index ERCP is achieved in about 75% of cases**; large stones are less common, occurring in **<20% of ERCPs**.
- **Large (>10 mm) stones:** sphincterotomy **plus transpapillary large balloon dilation** clears **85%** at the first ERCP. Across 19 RCTs (2,752 patients) comparing 5 approaches, clearance of common duct stones >10 mm ranged from **100% with single-operator cholangioscopy** down to **57% with mechanical lithotripsy**.
- Real-world shortfall: in independently verified hospital episode statistics on **37,468 patients** undergoing a first ERCP for biliary stones, **only two-thirds had complete clearance**. Nationwide registry data from the Netherlands put ERCP success at **85.8% when reported versus 76.0% when not reported** (*P* < 0.001).
- **Document the size and location of the stones, any strictures and any postsurgical anatomy**, so that later benchmarking compares like with like.

### PEP prophylaxis — the two indicators that changed (7 and 8)

**Rectal NSAIDs (indicator 7, grade 1A, >90%).**

- The 2015 document restricted the recommendation to **high-risk** cases; this one **endorses rectal indomethacin or diclofenac in unselected patients undergoing ERCP**, on the body of evidence showing benefit in all patients.
- Effect size: meta-analyses put the risk reduction around **50%**; a large RCT in >2,000 average-risk participants showed a **54% relative risk reduction**.
- The document names the contrary data rather than burying it: a 2012 trial of 602 elevated-risk patients created the perception that NSAIDs work only in high-risk cases, and an RCT of largely average-risk patients at a single centre showed **no benefit**. Two real-world effectiveness studies affirm benefit in all comers.
- **Timing: before or after the ERCP both work**, but the large RCT showing benefit gave the drug **systematically before** the procedure.
- **Denominator = patients with an intact papilla.** Patients having a repeat biliary intervention through a prior sphincterotomy (e.g., routine stent exchange) are at **negligible** PEP risk and are excluded. Do **not** withhold NSAIDs from a patient who has a prior *biliary* sphincterotomy but is undergoing **pancreatic endotherapy** — they remain at risk.
- **The target is 90%, not higher, deliberately** — to allow for contraindications (NSAID allergy, renal dysfunction) and very-low-risk patients. The gap being measured is large: existing evidence suggests NSAIDs are given to **well under 50%** of patients undergoing ERCP.

**Prophylactic pancreatic stent placement (indicator 8, grade 1A, >98% documented).**

- The indicator measures **documentation of use or nonuse** in high-risk cases, not a placement rate. **Denominator = high-risk ERCPs; numerator = those where use or nonuse is clearly documented.**
- Supporting RCT: a multicentre trial across **20 centres in the US and Canada** found **rectal indomethacin plus a prophylactic stent more effective than indomethacin alone**, reducing PEP by **more than 30%** and producing numerically fewer cases of severe pancreatitis and pancreatitis-related death. The benefit was **greatest in those at greatest risk**.
- **Risk factors that define "high risk"** as the document lists them: clinical suspicion of biliary or pancreatic sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, ampullectomy, difficult cannulation, double-wire cannulation technique, pancreatic sphincterotomy, and pancreatic endotherapy — explicitly *not limited to* these.
- **Why documentation rather than a rate:** PSP is technically demanding, each step can itself injure the pancreas, and **an unsuccessful attempt at stent placement may raise PEP risk above baseline**. A placement-rate target could drive overambitious stenting and more harm.
- Usage has collapsed — **PSP is used in under 5% of high-risk cases** in recent data. Those without training or experience **should refer anticipated high-risk cases** rather than attempt it, and should not feel compelled by this indicator to attempt a difficult stent.
- Decision inputs the document gives for whether to place one: **PSP is clearly advisable when pancreatic duct access is already achieved** (e.g., inadvertent guidewire passage). It is **less clear-cut when PEP risk is only modestly elevated** and placement is anticipated to be difficult — suboptimal cannulation alignment, a pancreatic ansa loop. Also weigh the patient's ability to undergo follow-up abdominal radiography (**mandatory**, to confirm an unflanged stent has passed) and the endoscopist's own comfort.

### Antibiotic prophylaxis (indicator 3)

Published literature most strongly supports prophylaxis in **three scenarios**, and these three are the indicator's denominator:

1. Patients in whom **complete relief of biliary obstruction is not anticipated** (e.g., primary sclerosing cholangitis [PSC], biliary cyst) **or is not achieved**.
2. Patients undergoing **cholangioscopy**.
3. Patients who have undergone **orthotopic liver transplantation**.

- **No data support routine antibiotic prophylaxis for pancreatoscopy** — individualize.
- ASGE has **moved post-liver-transplant prophylaxis from universal to conditional**, giving antibiotics for anticipated incomplete drainage (ischemic cholangiopathy, multiple intrahepatic strictures, contrast injection without stent placement), on newer data showing low ERCP-associated infection rates in that group.
- Antibiotics may also be worthwhile when **extraluminal contamination is suspected** — concern for intraprocedural perforation or bile duct injury.
- The document is equally explicit that **avoiding antibiotics where no benefit is likely is part of this indicator**; inappropriate use drives resistance and drug adverse events.

### Radiation (indicator 5)

- Principle is **as low as reasonably achievable (ALARA)**; the indicator is the first step in a comprehensive dose-minimization plan.
- **Measure dose directly where possible** — "dose area product" and "dose at reference point" are the precise measurements. **Fluoroscopy time is a surrogate and may correlate poorly** with actual dose, especially in prolonged and complex cases. Primary dose measurement is encouraged; if unavailable, **track fluoroscopy time at minimum**.
- Because case mix varies more by centre than by provider, **comparing radiation exposure among providers within one institution is more useful than absolute values**.
- Dose-reduction best practices named: **reduce pulse rate (frames per second)**, use a **"save last image"** function instead of static image acquisition, keep the **source distant from the patient and the detector close**, **collimate routinely**, uniform **personal protective equipment including leaded eye protection**, and **proper shielding**. All health care professionals in the ERCP practice — physicians and radiology technicians — **should undergo radiation safety training**.
- Fluoroscopy carries risk to patients and staff: **excess cancer through DNA damage** and, in extreme situations, **cutaneous injury**.

### Adverse-event rates the document prints

| Event | Rate |
|---|---|
| PEP, average-risk patients | **1%–7%** |
| PEP, high-risk populations (suspected sphincter of Oddi disorder, recurrent idiopathic acute pancreatitis, prior PEP) | **up to 30%**, with severe pancreatitis more common |
| Cholangitis after ERCP | **<1%** |
| Cholecystitis | **0.2%–0.5%** |
| Hemorrhage (most often after sphincterotomy) | **0.8%–2%** of cases |
| Perforation (guidewire-, sphincterotomy- or endoscope-induced) | **0.1%–0.6%** |
| Cholangitis/cholecystitis rates across reported cohorts | **1%–8%**, with **PSC the highest-risk indication** |
| Post-sphincterotomy bleeding — the rate to strive for | **<1%**, and in all cases **<1%–2%** of all procedures |

- **Informed consent (indicator 2) should cover at least 5 adverse outcomes:** pancreatitis, hemorrhage, **infection (both procedure-related and duodenoscope-related)**, perforation, and cardiopulmonary events — plus the possibility that the ERCP may not succeed and that an adverse event could be severe.
- **Carbapenem-resistant *Escherichia coli* infections are rare** but have been reported in the context of previous duodenoscope outbreaks. The task force **deliberately did not make duodenoscope contamination a quality indicator**: the measurable event rate is low and the clinical significance of contamination identified by endoscope culture remains unclear.
- **Clinically significant hemorrhage** has variable definitions; most require a notable hemoglobin decrease (**at least 2–3 g**) with melena, hematemesis or other signs of GI hemorrhage.
- **Precut sphincterotomy** is a probable technical risk factor for post-ERCP bleeding and would be expected to produce a substantially greater rate.

### The consensus definition of PEP (used for indicator 11)

**All three** of the following:

1. Abdominal pain consistent with pancreatitis;
2. Serum amylase or lipase **greater than 3× the upper limit of normal, measured more than 24 hours after** ERCP;
3. **Either** need for admission **or** prolongation of an admission of **at least 2 days**.

> The task force criticizes this definition in its own document: the interpretation of post-ERCP pain and the decision to hospitalize are **nonobjective and vary by practice style**, so a practitioner with a low admission threshold will record a higher PEP rate than one without. Patients with pre-existing chronic pain may partially satisfy the criteria for reasons unrelated to the procedure. This — plus case-mix effects — is why **between-provider and between-centre PEP comparisons are not reliable**, and why the indicator measures tracking rather than rate. **Longitudinal comparison against one's own past performance is the current mainstay.**

### Unplanned events (indicators 9 and 10)

- **Unplanned hospital visit <15%**, counting ERCP-specific adverse events, additional physician-defined adverse events, and other morbidity such as postprocedure pain or nausea. Reported rates in the US and other Western countries, across the commonest indications, are **typically <15%**.
- Greater-than-expected rates reflect one or more of: **suboptimal preprocedure evaluation (case selection or optimization)**, **technical performance factors**, or **inadequate ambulatory infrastructure to support patients in the recovery period**.
- **Unplanned biliary intervention <15%** within 30 days — repeat ERCP, salvage percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography, or a pivot to EUS-guided drainage at a *subsequent* procedure.
- **What does not count as unplanned:** an intervention anticipated before the index ERCP (e.g., placing a stent in acute suppurative cholangitis in a patient not safe for stone extraction at that sitting — the later procedure is a *planned* future event), and a **rescue intervention performed at the time of the index ERCP** (e.g., EUS-BD for failed cannulation during the same session). The same rescue **done a few days later is an unplanned intervention**.
- ERCP success rates across studies range **76% to 99%** with varying definitions and methodology.
- The task force **disagreed internally** on this indicator: counting repeat ERCP at any time would capture unsuccessful procedures and excess use, but certain indications — benign bile duct stricture — **legitimately require at least 2 ERCPs per patient**. The <15% target accounts for intentional delays judged to be in the patient's best interest.

### Controversial indications the document rules on

- **Suspected sphincter of Oddi disorder.** ERCP is **not indicated for biliary-type pain with no objective evidence of obstruction** (no dilated bile duct, normal liver enzymes). The **EPISOD** sham-controlled trial randomized **214 patients** with biliary-type pain after cholecystectomy, normal liver enzymes and a nondilated duct (type III) to sphincterotomy or sham: the **sham group reported greater relief of pain and disability at 1 year (37% vs 23%, *P* = 0.01)**, results were the same at 5 years, and **manometry was neither reproducible nor predictive of any outcome**. Type III is now considered a **disorder of gut–brain interaction** (previously a functional GI disorder). **Papillary stenosis** obstructing the bile duct outlet **may** be an indication for biliary sphincterotomy in patients with biliary-type pain **and** elevated liver enzymes or a dilated duct — evaluate judiciously, since the bile duct also dilates without obstructive pathology with **increasing age, cholecystectomy and opioid use**, and there are many other reasons for elevated liver tests.
- **Recurrent acute pancreatitis with normal pancreatic ductal anatomy** — remains controversial, results conflicting. ERCP here should be considered **only after other evaluations are negative and after a thorough discussion of the potential lack of benefit and the risk of severe adverse events**.
- **Pancreas divisum / minor papilla intervention.** Robust efficacy data are lacking. Systematic reviews of observational data suggest **>70% benefit** but are limited by heterogeneity; one small RCT (19 patients) found reduced future pancreatitis episodes with minor papilla stenting (**10% vs 67%, *P* < 0.05**). But a **recent large-scale multicentre randomized sham-controlled trial, presented in abstract form at Digestive Disease Week 2025**, concluded that **minor papilla sphincterotomy did not significantly reduce recurrence of acute pancreatitis** versus sham — challenging the longstanding practice of empiric minor papilla sphincterotomy in this population.
- **Preoperative drainage of malignant biliary obstruction.** Trials and meta-analyses show preoperative endobiliary stent placement in obstructive jaundice from **resectable** pancreatic cancer or extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma carries **greater risk of adverse events (mostly cholangitis)** than going straight to surgery. However, **neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy** is now standard in many settings and bilirubin normalization via stenting is a prerequisite for chemotherapy. In the neoadjuvant context, **a metal stent at the index ERCP reduces stent-related adverse events and unplanned reinterventions compared with plastic**. If an **uncovered** metal stent is placed for drainage, it is **imperative to confirm malignancy before placement** — it cannot be reliably removed if the stricture turns out to be benign.
- **Malignant hilar obstruction:** ERCP with stenting improves surgical outcomes in patients in whom the remnant liver volume will be **<50% of the original**. Regardless of the level of obstruction, **ERCP should be performed for a clear clinical purpose, not simply because obstructive jaundice exists**.
- **PSC.** **Magnetic resonance imaging with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) has high sensitivity (86%) and specificity (94%)** and similar diagnostic accuracy to ERCP (**83% vs 85%, not significant**), so MRCP is the guideline-recommended diagnostic approach. **ERCP should be reserved as a primarily therapeutic modality** in symptomatic patients, or for evaluating malignancy in patients with cholestasis or indeterminate-appearing strictures on MRCP. Diagnostic ERCP is justifiable only rarely — high clinical suspicion with a negative or equivocal MRCP and an unrevealing liver biopsy.
- **Minimizing diagnostic-only ERCP for suspected choledocholithiasis.** The original **2010** ASGE probability criteria had a positive predictive value of just **62%**, producing unnecessary ERCP in up to **30%** of cases; one retrospective study found the high-risk category held a stone only **73.6%** of the time. The **2019** revision is more stringent and achieved **sensitivity 96.87% (95% CI 95.37–97.98)** and **positive predictive value 89.57% (95% CI 85.20–92.75)**. Even so, **definitive visualization of a stone** on transabdominal ultrasound, intraoperative cholangiogram, MRCP or EUS is the way to drive diagnostic-only ERCP toward zero; **antecedent EUS before any ERCP for which a stone has not been confidently identified** is likely to reduce diagnostic ERCP, limited by local EUS availability.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Rectal NSAIDs: this document widens the population, [[asge-2023-post-ercp-pancreatitis|ASGE 2023]] had already done so.** No conflict in direction; this is the quality-indicator set catching up to the PEP guideline, and it makes administration a **measured 1A indicator** rather than a recommendation. The **>90%, not >98%**, target is deliberate.
- **Prophylactic pancreatic stents: a measurement paradox the task force states openly.** It grades the intervention 1A and simultaneously warns that making placement a measured target could cause **overambitious stenting and net harm**, so it measures documentation instead. A later pass should not "fix" this into a placement-rate target.
- **No performance target for the PEP rate itself**, by design. Any future wiki claim of an acceptable PEP rate must come from elsewhere; this document declines to set one.
- **Duodenoscope contamination is deliberately absent** from the indicator set — not an oversight. Relevant where [[endoscope-reprocessing]] discusses measurement.
- **Minor papilla sphincterotomy for pancreas divisum** is a live reversal: observational data suggesting >70% benefit versus a negative multicentre sham-controlled RCT available **only as a 2025 abstract**. The wiki carries both, and does not yet have the full paper.
- **Cannulation persistence has no stated time limit** — the document explicitly calls the optimal pivot point an open research question. Do not supply one.
