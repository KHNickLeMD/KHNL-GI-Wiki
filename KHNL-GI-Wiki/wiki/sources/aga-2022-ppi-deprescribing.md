## Summary

Proton pump inhibitors are among the most-used medications in the world: **7%–15%** of patients use one at any given time, rising to **40% in patients 70 years or older**, and roughly **one-quarter** of everyone started on a PPI continues for at least a year. In a large observational study of ambulatory PPI users, **nearly two-thirds had no clear indication**. Against that, the trials establishing PPI efficacy tested durations of **4–12 weeks**, or no more than **6–12 months** for maintenance. The gap between how PPIs were studied and how they are used is the problem this update addresses.

The update's guiding principle is deliberately symmetric, and it cuts against the prevailing direction of travel. Patients should not take a medication without a reasonable expectation of benefit — **but the trigger for stopping is the absence of an indication, not fear of harm.** The authors are emphatic that PPI-associated adverse events (PAAEs), from chronic kidney disease to fracture to dementia, come entirely from observational data, that randomized trials comparing PPIs with placebo have shown **no higher rate of any adverse event**, and that many proposed associations lack a plausible mechanism. **BPA 10 therefore states that neither a PAAE, nor a history of one, nor the presence of risk factors for one, is an independent indication to withdraw a PPI.**

The counterweight is bleeding risk. Because risk factors for upper GI bleeding — concomitant over-the-counter aspirin, for instance — are often "hidden," patients must be formally assessed for UGIB risk *before* de-prescribing (BPA 6), and those at high risk must not be de-prescribed at all (BPA 7). The update also carves out specific populations who should generally not be trialed off: complicated GERD (severe erosive esophagitis, esophageal ulcer, peptic stricture — BPA 4) and Barrett's esophagus, eosinophilic esophagitis, or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (BPA 5).

Where de-prescribing *is* appropriate, two practical points: most patients on twice-daily dosing should be stepped down to once daily (BPA 3) — double-dose PPI has never been studied in any RCT and is not FDA-approved, yet up to 15% of users are on it — and either tapering or abrupt discontinuation is acceptable (BPA 9), since the one trial comparing them found no significant difference. Patients should be warned about **rebound acid hypersecretion** (BPA 8), a real physiologic phenomenon that produces transient upper GI symptoms and can be managed with as-needed H2RAs or antacids rather than immediate PPI resumption.

## Best Practice Advice Statements (all 10, verbatim)

**Best Practice Advice 1:** All patients taking a PPI should have a regular review of the ongoing indications for use and documentation of that indication. This review should be the responsibility of the patient's primary care provider.

**Best Practice Advice 2:** All patients without a definitive indication for chronic PPI should be considered for trial of de-prescribing.

**Best Practice Advice 3:** Most patients with an indication for chronic PPI use who take twice-daily dosing should be considered for step down to once-daily PPI.

**Best Practice Advice 4:** Patients with complicated gastroesophageal reflux disease, such as those with a history of severe erosive esophagitis, esophageal ulcer, or peptic stricture, should generally not be considered for PPI discontinuation.

**Best Practice Advice 5:** Patients with known Barrett's esophagus, eosinophilic esophagitis, or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis should generally not be considered for a trial of de-prescribing.

**Best Practice Advice 6:** PPI users should be assessed for upper gastrointestinal bleeding risk using an evidence-based strategy before de-prescribing.

**Best Practice Advice 7:** Patients at high risk for upper gastrointestinal bleeding should not be considered for PPI de-prescribing.

**Best Practice Advice 8:** Patients who discontinue long-term PPI therapy should be advised that they may develop transient upper gastrointestinal symptoms due to rebound acid hypersecretion.

**Best Practice Advice 9:** When de-prescribing PPIs, either dose tapering or abrupt discontinuation can be considered.

**Best Practice Advice 10:** The decision to discontinue PPIs should be based solely on the lack of an indication for PPI use, and not because of concern for PAAEs. The presence of a PAAE or a history of a PAAE in a current PPI user is not an independent indication for PPI withdrawal. Similarly, the presence of underlying risk factors for the development of an adverse event associated with PPI use should also not be an independent indication for PPI withdrawal.

### Who is "high risk for upper GI bleeding" (BPA 6/7)

The update states plainly that **"precisely which patients are at sufficiently increased risk for upper GI bleeding to warrant PPI co-therapy remains uncertain,"** and that published guidance statements differ (Table 2 below). Synthesizing across them, patients are at high risk if they have **any** of:

- **A history of upper GI bleeding**
- **Multiple antithrombotics** (including both anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents)
- **Aspirin or an NSAID plus an additional risk factor for upper GI bleeding** — namely: older than 60 years, severe medical comorbidity, use of a second NSAID or aspirin, taking an antithrombotic, or taking an oral corticosteroid

In such patients the PPI **should not** be de-prescribed. Separately, patients with **hypersecretory states such as Zollinger-Ellison syndrome** should clearly not be de-prescribed.

### Dose step-down (BPA 3)
- Double-dose PPI (standard dose twice daily, or double-strength once daily) has **not been studied in any RCT** and is **not FDA-approved** — yet up to **15%** of PPI users are on a higher-than-standard dose.
- Higher-dose PPIs increase cost and have been more strongly associated with community-acquired pneumonia, hip fracture, and *C. difficile* infection — **though there is no direct evidence of a causal relationship**.
- Higher oral doses **are** supported **acutely** to prevent rebleeding from peptic ulcer disease. There is **minimal** evidence for higher doses for maintenance of healed esophagitis or prevention of PUD complications.
- **No study of Barrett's esophagus** shows double-dose is superior to standard-dose for preventing extension or progression to dysplasia or cancer.
- PPIs at **any** dose are **ineffective** for laryngopharyngeal symptoms.
- High-dose PPI **is** indicated for Zollinger-Ellison syndrome — prevalence ~1/1,000,000.
- Trial evidence: of 117 patients on higher-than-standard doses for GERD, **80% successfully stepped down** to standard dose without significant symptom recurrence or need to re-escalate.

### Populations not to de-prescribe (BPA 4, 5)
- **~20%** of patients with untreated GERD have erosive esophagitis; most GERD is nonerosive.
- Recurrence of esophagitis after withdrawal is common, particularly in **symptomatic LA grade C or D**.
- Because many GERD patients are treated empirically, it **may not be known** whether a given PPI user has had severe erosive esophagitis. In those patients de-prescribing can be considered, but the PPI should be **quickly re-initiated** if symptoms/signs of complicated GERD emerge, with consideration of upper endoscopy to confirm severe erosive esophagitis.
- **Barrett's esophagus:** observational studies and RCTs indicate PPIs reduce the risk of esophageal adenocarcinoma → do not trial off.
- **Eosinophilic esophagitis:** clinical response **61%** and histologic remission **51%** with PPI therapy in symptomatic esophageal eosinophilia. Not every EoE patient requires a PPI (those responding to topical steroids or non-pharmacologic therapy), but de-prescribing in a previous responder may cause a high rate of symptomatic and histologic recurrence.
- **Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis:** low-quality evidence that PPIs reduce progression; the authors judge it reasonable not to target these patients until evidence definitively shows PPIs are ineffective.

### Rebound acid hypersecretion (BPA 8)
- **Mechanism:** PPI raises intragastric pH → serum gastrin rises (acid secretion inhibition normally requires the presence of gastric acid) → hypergastrinemia promotes parietal cell and enterochromaffin-like cell proliferation → increased acid-producing capacity. On stopping the PPI, that increased parietal cell mass is unleashed.
- **Duration:** not well characterized. Physiologic studies suggest ECL and parietal cell mass can still be present **8 weeks** after withdrawal, usually regressed by **6 months**.
- RCT evidence (Reimer et al): in persons **without** a history of upper GI symptoms, PPI withdrawal after an 8-week course led to a **higher incidence of upper GI symptoms** than continuing.
- **~One-half** of patients with uncomplicated GERD who discontinued PPI remained off at 6 months — but **three-quarters of those who succeeded** were using H2RAs or over-the-counter antacids for symptom control.
- **Management of withdrawal symptoms:** as-needed H2-receptor antagonists and/or contact antacids are reasonable; on-demand PPI in place of continuous PPI is effective symptom control and can serve as a **partial de-prescribing strategy**.
- **Red flag:** severe persistent symptoms lasting **more than 2 months** after discontinuation may indicate a continuing indication for PPI, or a non–acid-mediated cause of symptoms.
- An RCT found that persons **without abnormal esophageal acid exposure (acid exposure time >4.0%)** in the first **7 days** after PPI withdrawal on ambulatory pH testing are much more likely not to require PPI re-initiation — though the feasibility of pH testing before every discontinuation attempt is unclear.

### Taper vs abrupt (BPA 9)
- One published trial compared abrupt discontinuation with a **3-week taper** (PPI daily → PPI every other day for 3 weeks → stop): **no significant difference** in remaining off PPI and symptom-free at 6 months — **31% (tapered) vs 22% (abrupt)**.
- The authors caution that since parietal/ECL hyperplasia may take **2–6 months** to regress, this taper may simply have been too rapid to work.

### Why BPA 10 exists — the behavioral data
- Concern about PAAEs is common: nearly **40%** of patients have attempted to discontinue PPIs, **83% of them without physician advice**. Patients reporting "extreme concern" about PPI adverse effects were **20 times** more likely to have tried to stop.
- **Three-quarters of physicians** report altering treatment plans because of PPI concerns, and **~80%** say they would discontinue PPIs when presented with a clinical scenario of a patient **at high risk of upper GI bleeding**.
- All studies reporting PAAE associations are **observational** and cannot establish causality; **RCTs comparing PPIs with placebo have not shown a higher rate of any adverse event**; **no RCT has demonstrated increased incidence of any PAAE**.

### Other context
- Most PPIs are prescribed by primary care providers; PPIs have been available over the counter in the US since 2003, so physicians may not be involved in the decision to start.
- Chronic use is often **unintended** — e.g. a PPI started in hospital for stress ulcer prophylaxis and continued at discharge without review.
- PPIs are frequently initiated without a definite indication, notably empirically for **cough, throat clearing, globus, and voice hoarseness** — an RCT has definitively shown no benefit for these, so such patients should be considered for de-prescribing.
- Where infrastructure exists, a **pharmacist- or nurse-specialist–centered multidisciplinary approach** may help systematically identify inappropriate PPI use.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Age threshold, within the same document.** The synthesized high-risk criteria use **>60 years**; the ACG 2009 row of Table 2 uses **>65 years**. The source prints both without reconciling them. The wiki should carry both and name the discordance rather than picking one.
- **The high-risk definition is explicitly unsettled.** The source states that which patients warrant PPI co-therapy "remains uncertain" and that guidance statements "offer varying recommendations." Table 2 is a display of disagreement (ACG, ACP, ACCF/ACG/AHA, ESC/EACTS, ACCP, ACC), not a consensus.
- **De-prescribing pushes against PAAE literature.** BPA 10 directly opposes the common practice of stopping PPIs because of observational adverse-event signals. Any wiki content citing PPI-associated adverse events (e.g. the *C. difficile* risk data on [[proton-pump-inhibitors]] from [[aga-2026-cdiff-adults]]) must be read alongside BPA 10 — note that the 2026 C. diff CPU independently reaches the same conclusion (continue if indicated, never dose-reduce), so these are **concordant, not conflicting**.
- **Taper vs abrupt is underpowered, not settled.** The single trial showed 31% vs 22% — the source explicitly raises the possibility that the 3-week taper was too rapid given a 2–6 month regression timeline.
- **Ambulatory pH testing to predict success** (acid exposure time >4.0% in the first 7 days post-withdrawal) is described as promising but of unclear feasibility.
- **Duration of rebound acid hypersecretion is not well characterized.**
