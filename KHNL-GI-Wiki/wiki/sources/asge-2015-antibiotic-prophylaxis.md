## Summary
American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) Standards of Practice Committee guideline on periprocedural antibiotics for gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy, addressing both prevention of infective endocarditis (IE) and prevention of local/remote infections. Endoscopy can cause transient bacteremia (highest with esophageal dilation 12–22%, variceal sclerotherapy up to 52%, and instrumentation of obstructed bile ducts), but clinically significant infection is extremely rare — among millions of procedures only ~25 temporally associated IE cases have been reported, and there is no causal link between endoscopy and IE nor evidence that prophylaxis prevents it. Transient bacteremia from routine daily activities (brushing, flossing, chewing) often exceeds endoscopy-associated rates.

**Antibiotic prophylaxis is NOT indicated solely to prevent IE for any GI endoscopic procedure**, regardless of cardiac risk lesion, consistent with the American Heart Association's (AHA) shift away from procedure-based IE prophylaxis. The exception: patients with high-risk cardiac conditions who also have an established GI infection where enterococci may be part of the flora may receive coverage (directed at the infection, not the procedure).

Prophylaxis IS warranted in specific scenarios to prevent local infection: endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with anticipated **incomplete biliary drainage** (and after liver transplant or known/suspected obstruction with possible incomplete drainage); **percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy/jejunostomy (PEG/PEJ)** placement (parenteral cefazolin); **endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)–guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA; EUS-FNA) of cystic lesions** (mediastinal, pancreatic/peripancreatic); and **all cirrhotic patients admitted with GI bleeding** (antibiotics started at admission regardless of endoscopy — ceftriaxone intravenous (IV), or oral norfloxacin if intolerant). Prophylaxis is NOT recommended for ERCP with complete drainage and no obstruction, EUS-FNA of solid lesions, synthetic vascular grafts/nonvalvular cardiovascular devices, or orthopedic prostheses; it is suggested for lower-GI endoscopy in continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis patients.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Bacteremia risk by procedure
- **High:** esophageal dilation (bougienage 12–22%; higher with malignant strictures and multiple dilators), variceal sclerotherapy (up to 52%, mean ~14.6%), instrumentation of obstructed bile ducts.
- **Low:** diagnostic esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) ± biopsy, colonoscopy ± biopsy/polypectomy, EUS-FNA of solid lesions (within range of diagnostic EGD), endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL).
- Routine daily activity (brushing/flossing/chewing) causes transient bacteremia often exceeding endoscopy rates — a key rationale against procedure-based IE prophylaxis.

### Recommendations (GRADE)
1. We recommend against the routine administration of antibiotic prophylaxis solely for prevention of IE. **(Moderate quality)**
2. We suggest that patients with high-risk cardiac conditions (Table 2) and established GI tract infections in which enterococci may be part of the infecting bacterial flora should receive antibiotic coverage. **(Low quality)**
3. We recommend against antibiotic prophylaxis before ERCP when obstructive biliary tract disease is not suspected or complete biliary drainage is anticipated. **(High quality)**
4. We recommend that antibiotic prophylaxis be administered before ERCP in patients who have had liver transplantation or who have known or suspected biliary obstruction, where there is a possibility of incomplete biliary drainage. Antibiotics that cover biliary flora such as enteric gram-negative organisms and enterococci should be used and continued after the procedure if biliary drainage is incomplete. **(Moderate quality)**
5. We recommend against antibiotic prophylaxis before diagnostic EUS or EUS-FNA of solid lesions of the GI tract. **(Moderate quality)**
6. We suggest antibiotic administration prior to EUS-FNA of mediastinal cysts. **(Low quality)**
7. We suggest administration of prophylactic antibiotics before EUS-FNA of pancreatic or peripancreatic cysts. **(Low quality)**
8. We recommend administration of parenteral cefazolin (or an antibiotic with equivalent microbial coverage) to all patients before PEG/PEJ tube placement. **(High quality)**
9. We recommend that all patients with cirrhosis admitted with GI bleeding should have antibiotic therapy instituted at admission with intravenous ceftriaxone (or an antibiotic with equivalent microbial coverage [eg, oral norfloxacin] in patients allergic to or intolerant of ceftriaxone). **(High quality)**
10. We recommend against administration of antibiotic prophylaxis before GI endoscopic procedures for patients with synthetic vascular grafts or other nonvalvular cardiovascular devices (eg, implantable electronic devices). **(High quality)**
11. We recommend against antibiotic prophylaxis for patients with orthopedic prosthesis undergoing any GI endoscopic procedure. **(Moderate quality)**
12. We suggest administration of antibiotic prophylaxis before endoscopy of the lower GI tract in patients undergoing continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis. **(Very low quality)**

### Additional points
- IE prophylaxis: high-risk cardiac conditions (Table 2) include prosthetic cardiac valve/prosthetic repair material, prior IE, unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease (CHD) (incl. palliative shunts/conduits), completely repaired CHD with prosthetic material/device within 6 months, repaired CHD with residual defects at/adjacent to a prosthetic patch/device, and cardiac transplant recipients with valvulopathy. Even for these, prophylaxis is not given solely for the endoscopic procedure.
- **PEG/PEJ:** cefazolin ~30 min before placement reduces peristomal infection; methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA) decolonization where endemic.
- **EUS-FNA cysts:** antibiotics (eg, fluoroquinolone) before and often 3–5 days after FNA of cystic lesions; rationale is cyst-infection prevention.
- **Cirrhosis + GI bleed:** Cochrane meta-analysis (12 randomized controlled trials [RCTs], 1241 patients) — antibiotics reduce infections, rebleeding, and mortality; start at admission regardless of whether/when endoscopy occurs.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- No causal link between endoscopy and IE and no evidence prophylaxis prevents it — basis for recommending against procedure-based IE prophylaxis (aligns with AHA).
- American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) 2009 statement recommending prophylaxis for all joint-replacement patients before invasive procedures was later withdrawn for lack of evidence; ASGE recommends against prophylaxis for orthopedic prostheses.
- Evidence for EUS-FNA cyst prophylaxis and peritoneal-dialysis prophylaxis is low/very-low quality.
