## Summary

- Preface by the two issue editors introducing a 12-article issue on the endoscopic oncologist (p xvi).
- An editorial: no graded recommendations, no trial data of its own. Everything below is the authors' perspective.
- Argues that over three decades the gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopic oncologist's role has emerged and expanded — from diagnosis through staging, resection, antitumor delivery, palliation and nutritional support (p xvi; Table 1).

## Key Findings / Claims

### A Historical Perspective (p xv)
- First-person account: recruited in 1993 as Head of Gastrointestinal Oncology at the University of California, Irvine, within a new "NCI-designated Comprehensive Cancer Center".
- Saw the space where GI intersects with oncology as an area of clinically unmet needs.
- The era as described:
  - Gastroenterologist made the diagnosis of digestive cancers, then quickly sent the patient to the medical oncologist without much further involvement.
  - Medical oncologist gave chemotherapy with or without radiation but might not have full command of GI issues arising during and after treatment.
- Started with an endoscope and the then relatively new endoscopic ultrasound (EUS); embedded in a cancer center beside medical, radiation, gynecologic and surgical oncologists.
- Started an advanced fellowship in EUS and GI Oncology; later received the first endowed chair with this title ("The Vincent and Anna Kong Chair of Gastrointestinal Endoscopic Oncology").

### The Role, Area by Area (p xvi)
- **Diagnosis of superficial cancer:** endoscopic detection and assessment of submucosal invasion with image-enhanced and magnifying endoscopy "have improved dramatically".
- **Diagnosis of extraluminal cancer:** EUS for tumors adjacent to the GI tract (posterior mediastinum, lung, peritoneum, liver); EUS, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography and cholangioscopy complementary for biliary cancer — described as standard of care.
- **Staging:** EUS more complementary to computed tomography (CT)/MRI as imaging has improved; definitive N staging with EUS–fine needle aspiration (FNA)/biopsy "has unique clinical value".
- **Endoscopic resection of early cancer:** in its infancy in the early 1990s; now mainstream and indispensable — endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), endoscopic full thickness resection (EFTR), submucosal tunneling endoscopic resection (STER).
- **EUS-guided antitumor delivery:** first reported in 2000; injection, ablation and radioactive seed implantation for solid and cystic pancreatic tumors. Definitive role in neuroendocrine tumors and adjunctive/immunomodulatory role in pancreatic cancer "still await confirmatory clinical trials".
- **Palliation of luminal obstruction** (esophageal, gastric outlet, biliary, colonic): self-expanding stents, lumen apposing stents and ablation, to bridge or completely bypass the malignant stricture.
- **Tumor bleeding:** palliative cessation of bleeding.
- **Pain:** EUS-guided celiac plexus and celiac ganglion neurolysis.
- **Nutrition:** enteral feeding when oral intake is compromised.

### Toolbox, 1993 vs 2023 (Table 1, p xvii)

| Role | 1993 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosing luminal cancers | Low-quality imaging, biopsy with forceps, histology only | High-quality imaging, image enhancement, magnifying endoscopy, high-yield sampling, biological markers |
| Diagnosing extraluminal cancers | CT in its infancy; MRI not yet available; EUS emerged to image and biopsy pancreatic, liver, biliary, adrenal and lung cancers | EUS, especially with FNA/fine needle biopsy, complementary to CT/MRI; EUS, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography and cholangioscopy complementary to imaging for biliary cancer |
| Staging GI and other cancers | EUS more accurate than CT for staging esophageal, gastric, pancreatic and rectal cancers | CT/MRI improved, so less need for EUS staging, although complementary; EUS/FNA for nodal staging is valuable |
| Endoscopic resection of early cancer | EMR started in Japan, just making its way to the West | EMR, ESD, EFTR and STER mainstream in the West and indispensable for early cancer treatment |
| Endoscopic delivery of antitumor agents | Early case reports of delivering agents via EUS-guided fine needle injection | Injection, ablation and radioactive seed implantation for solid and cystic pancreatic tumors |
| Luminal (esophageal, gastric, colonic, biliary) obstruction | Dilation, alcohol injection, ND:YAG laser, early stiff stents, venting G-tube | Softer, fully and partially covered stents, lumen-opposing stents, gastrojejunostomy, hepaticojejunostomy, choledochojejunostomy, cholecystojejunostomy, and similar |
| Tumor bleeding | ND:YAG laser, alcohol injection | Argon plasma coagulation, topical hemostatic agents |
| Pain management | EUS-guided celiac neurolysis | EUS-guided celiac ganglion lysis; pancreatic duct stenting |
| Nutritional support | G-tube | G-J tube, direct J-tube, luminal stenting and bypass |
