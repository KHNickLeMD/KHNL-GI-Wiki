## Summary

ESGE's management guideline for biliary strictures — **how to drain them, with what, for how long**. It replaces the drainage half of the 2018 ESGE biliary stenting guideline and harmonises it with the 2022 ESGE therapeutic endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) guideline. It is deliberately **independent of diagnosis**: the work-up that separates benign from malignant strictures (sampling, cholangioscopy, imaging) belongs to the separate ESGE 2025 biliary stricture diagnosis guideline and is **not** restated here. There are therefore **no tissue-acquisition recommendations and no sampling-yield figures in this document**.

The headline change is that **benign stricture stenting is now prescribed by etiology rather than as one rule**. The 2018 guideline gave a single statement — multiple plastic stents (MPSs) or a fully covered self-expandable metal stent (FCSEMS) for any benign biliary stricture (BBS). The 2026 version splits it four ways: MPSs for postcholecystectomy strictures, either option for post-liver-transplant anastomotic strictures, FCSEMS for chronic pancreatitis, and balloon dilation with **no stent** for primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC).

On the malignant side the document moves **away from percutaneous drainage at every decision point**. Endoscopy beats surgery and percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTBD) for primary decompression; endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) beats PTBD for preoperative hilar drainage; EUS-guided biliary drainage (EUS-BD) beats PTBD after failed ERCP (the only **strong** recommendation in the whole failed-ERCP/altered-anatomy block); and for palliative Bismuth III–IV hilar disease, ERCP ± EUS-BD is preferred over primary PTBD, reversing the 2018 position that named PTBD first. The hilar drainage target also moves up: **>50% and ideally ≥70% of viable nonatrophic liver volume**, where 2018 said ≥50%.

Most recommendations rest on low or very low quality evidence; the task force says so explicitly and lists the trials it wants (preoperative SEMS vs upfront pancreaticoduodenectomy, antimigration FCSEMS for BBS, MPSs vs FCSEMS in postcholecystectomy strictures, prospective liver-volumetry studies in hilar palliation, partially covered vs uncovered SEMS in distal malignant biliary obstruction).

## Key Findings / Claims

### Practical stenting parameters for benign strictures (ungraded, stated in §3.1)
- **Start endotherapy** for a BBS with a **persistent rise in liver function tests for more than a month** and/or **cholangitis**.
- Choice between MPSs and FCSEMS depends on **etiology, stricture location, common bile duct (CBD) diameter, and endoscopist experience**.
- **MPSs:** insert the maximum number of stents possible, **exchange every 3–4 months**, **total duration 12 months**.
- **FCSEMS:** **8–10 mm diameter**, **indwell 6–12 months**.
- **Endoscopic sphincterotomy is needed** to run an MPS protocol, to place and remove an intraductal FCSEMS, and to reduce the risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP) from transpapillary FCSEMS insertion.

### Periprocedural and technical points (ungraded, §3.5)
- **Antibiotics before ERCP** for: predicted **incomplete biliary drainage**, **hilar stricture** management, **immunocompromised** patients, **PSC**, and **cholangioscopy** — details deferred to the ESGE ERCP adverse-events guideline.
- **PEP prophylaxis:** **rectal diclofenac or indomethacin 100 mg in every patient without a contraindication**, plus hyperhydration and a prophylactic pancreatic stent, per existing ESGE guidance.
- **Keep a stent registry and recall patients for stent removal/exchange**, especially in BBS — forgotten stents can be extremely difficult to remove, and scheduled exchange reduces septic complications. Carried unchanged from 2017; no new data.
- **Stent dysfunction in MBO (§3.2.5):** the 2026 update makes **no recommendation**, where 2018 had one. Distal MBO with a blocked SEMS — an uncovered or a covered SEMS inside it appear **equally effective** for patency (one randomized controlled trial [RCT] plus a retrospective series). Proximal MBO — data conflict between plastic and metal inside the original SEMS. **EUS-BD lumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS) dysfunction** is ~10% with a 12% reintervention rate in meta-analysis, and is **mitigated by placing an FCSEMS or a coaxial double-pigtail plastic stent through the LAMS**.

### Supporting data behind the decision points

**Benign — postcholecystectomy.** Incidence <1% of cholecystectomies; involves the main hepatic confluence in almost half of cases, which is why MPSs (adaptable to a confluence stricture) are first-line. 154 patients treated with MPSs: 9.4% stricture recurrence at a mean of 11 years after stent removal, 7.4% needing unscheduled exchange for AEs. 18 patients with strictures >2 cm from the confluence treated with FCSEMS for 10–12 months: only 61% free of further treatment at 5 years, 16.7% migration, 38.9% AEs. Endoscopic retreatment was always possible after either strategy failed, and neither precluded later surgery.

**Benign — post-transplant anastomotic.** Biliary strictures complicate 4%–20% of orthotopic liver transplants (up to 40% of living-donor transplants). FCSEMS is usually **10 mm** (8 mm per anatomy) for **6 months**; MPSs are added/exchanged every 3–4 months for 1 year. Meta-analyses: equal resolution and AEs, fewer ERCPs and lower cost with FCSEMS. At 5 years the trade reverses — one RCT found **36% recurrence with transpapillary FCSEMS vs 7% with MPSs**, and a single-arm series 51%; MPS recurrence is 3%–6% beyond 5 years. An intraductal antimigration FCSEMS narrowed the gap (33.3% vs 28.6%). A **sequential stent-addition MPS protocol** (plastic stents added at each ERCP without removing the previous ones) matched conventional exchange on resolution, recurrence and AEs — including no excess cholangitis — while cutting procedure time, fluoroscopy time and cost.

**Benign — post-transplant nonanastomotic.** Arises from ischemic cholangiopathy; meta-analysis risk factors are donation after circulatory death, PSC as the transplant indication, Roux-en-Y reconstruction, hepatic artery thrombosis, longer cold and warm ischemia times, and longer operative time. Four cholangiographic patterns: **confluence-dominant** (manageable with MPSs), **minor form** (rarely needs stenting), and **diffuse necrosis** and **multifocal progressive** (largely stent-dependent; retransplantation almost always required). Technique: selective cannulation of intrahepatic radicles, **balloon dilation commonly to 4–6 mm**, then MPSs; exchange every 3–4 months over 1 year, or continue until resolution without a fixed timeframe. Resolution 41%–60% short-term, falling to **21% at 1–8 years**; cholangitis during the stent program in up to 74%.

**Benign — chronic pancreatitis.** Long intrapancreatic strictures; 60%–70% long-term resolution after endotherapy. Intervention indicated for **jaundice, cholangitis, or prolonged cholestasis (alkaline phosphatase elevated for over a month)**, to prevent secondary biliary cirrhosis (seen in 7.3% of untreated chronic-pancreatitis BBS). Three RCTs put FCSEMS and MPSs level on resolution (75.8% vs 77.1%; 92% vs 90%; 92.6% vs 85.4%) with **significantly fewer ERCPs for FCSEMS** (2.6 vs 3.9) and far fewer stents used. FCSEMS migration 7%–25%; tissue ingrowth/embedding 3%–4%; **position the stent to avoid occluding the cystic duct**. Five years after FCSEMS removal, 61.6% remained stent-free (77.4% of those with initial resolution). No head-to-head comparison of 6-month vs 10–12-month indwell exists.

**Benign — PSC.** **High grade (dominant) stricture, new definition: a stricture causing 75% reduction in duct diameter in the CBD or hepatic ducts on magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP).** These carry increased cholangiocarcinoma risk and require brush cytology or biopsy at ERCP, ± cholangioscopy; assess the anatomy with MRCP before dilating. Balloon dilation alone vs dilation + stenting: technical success 96.8% vs 91.9%, clinical success 86.5% vs 70.8%, AEs 11.2% vs 26.9% (meta-analysis, 458 patients); a second meta-analysis (660 patients) found equal efficacy with fewer interventions (2.7 vs 6.5) and fewer AEs (15% vs 44%), cholangitis 3.2% vs 16.8%. The multicenter RCT found equal recurrence-free rates but **severe procedure-related AEs 6.7% with dilation vs 45.4% with stenting**. Repeated dilation gave 81% transplant-free survival at 5 years and 52% at 10 years. Recurrence-free interval generally 9–12 months. **When a stent is unavoidable in PSC, use plastic and remove it within 1–2 weeks** to avoid cholangitis from occlusion by debris.
- **Immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4)-related cholangitis** is handled separately: diagnosis rests on the HISORt criteria (histology, imaging [MRCP], serology [serum IgG4], organ involvement, response to steroids); **immunosuppression is the key treatment**, and endoscopic drainage is reserved for septic cholangitis or long-standing fibrotic strictures — **balloon dilation preferred, short-course stenting only if refractory to dilation**.

**Malignant — distal.** 5%–10% of patients resected for presumed pancreatic cancer have benign disease, which is the argument against a USEMS before the etiology is proven. The landmark PBD RCT (upfront surgery within 1 week vs plastic-stent drainage then surgery at 4–6 weeks) found more severe AEs with drainage (74% vs 39%) but **excluded patients with bilirubin above 250 µmol/L (14 mg/dL)** and used plastic stents, now known to dysfunction more than SEMS. SEMS vs plastic for PBD: longer patency and fewer reinterventions, but more post-ERCP AEs (PEP 10.7% vs 4.7%). A neoadjuvant RCT found less stent dysfunction with FCSEMS than plastic (18.2% vs 72.8%); FCSEMS and USEMS performed equally in another, where **4-cm stents predicted reintervention — a 6-cm SEMS may be preferable**. Covered vs uncovered SEMS: no difference in dysfunction, reintervention or survival; a 62-study meta-analysis favored **partially covered SEMS over FCSEMS on time to recurrent obstruction (369 vs 238 days)**. Antimigration tricks: a **coaxial double-pigtail stent through an FCSEMS cut migration from 40% to 15% and extended patency from 173 to 237 days**.
- **EUS-BD as primary drainage:** meta-analyses of RCTs show EUS-BD and ERCP equal on technical success, clinical success, safety and patency, but real-world data report 7% technical failure and 30-day mortality up to 12%. **CBD diameter ≤15 mm and low operator experience (≤10 LAMS placements) predict misdeployment and failure.** The task force positions primary EUS-BD as an alternative only in expert hands and **when the CBD is ≥12 mm**; at one high-volume center it was needed in only 6.6% of distal MBO cases.

**Malignant — hilar.** Preoperative drainage indications include severe jaundice, cholangitis, and a **predicted future liver remnant ≤30%**. The RCT of ERCP vs PTBD for potentially resectable perihilar cholangiocarcinoma was **stopped early for higher mortality with PTBD (41% vs 11%)**; meta-analysis also links PTBD to more implantation metastases. Palliative bicenter data: equal technical and therapeutic success, but **procedure-related death 6.0% (ERCP) vs 15.8% (PTBD)**, more pancreatitis with ERCP (9.4% vs 0%). EUS-HGS for hilar MBO: technical success 91%–100%, clinical success 65%–100%, AEs 8%–35%; versus PTBD, better clinical success (100% vs 75%) and fewer AEs (13% vs 58%).
- **Volume target.** Bilateral SEMS beat unilateral on clinical success (95.3% vs 84.9%), reintervention (42.6% vs 60.3%) and cumulative patency (252 vs 139 days) in an RCT. Older data set the bar at >50% drained; newer series found **≥80% drained improved survival (8.5 vs 3.5 months; 450 vs 224 days)** and a **receiver-operating-characteristic threshold of 71% drained** predicted being able to give chemotherapy (92% vs 44%). Drainage of >50% and of >2 liver sectors each predicted clinical success independently of unilateral vs bilateral.
- **How to compute it:** divide the segments drained by the nonatrophic/nonresected segments present. Sectors to recognise on preprocedural imaging are **right anterior (V, VIII), right posterior (VI, VII), and left (II, III)** — noting for each whether tumor infiltration separates them. MRCP defines the strategy; three-dimensional computed tomography volumetry is an alternative. **Do not intubate atrophic segments** — it raises post-ERCP cholangitis.
- **Stent type.** No RCT compares USEMS with FCSEMS in unresectable hilar MBO. A plastic-stent vs 6-mm FCSEMS RCT (50 patients) found more migration with FCSEMS (48% vs 16%) but longer time to recurrent obstruction (190 vs 96 days) and fewer reinterventions (16% vs 40%). The task force's caution is that a **USEMS is a definitive intervention** — suboptimal initial drainage cannot easily be optimised afterwards.

**Altered anatomy and failed ERCP.** ERCP fails in 10%–20% of patients overall (ESGE performance measures set the expert-center ceiling at 5%); a **repeat attempt after waiting at least 2 days succeeds in up to 82%**. EA-ERCP procedural success is 61.7%–75.8% with single-balloon and up to 93% with double-balloon enteroscopy (AEs 4%–6.6%); **short double-balloon scopes (<200 cm) do better than long ones** because standard ERCP accessories fit. EUS-BD in altered anatomy: technical success 97.8%, clinical success 94.9%, AEs 12.8%. EDGE: 94%–98% ERCP success, AEs 14%–18%; **a 20-mm LAMS dislodges less than a 15-mm**, and routine 20-mm use plus anchoring has made single-session EDGE feasible — the LAMS must still be removed later to avoid weight regain. EUS-rendezvous technical success 77%–86.1% (92% in recent benign-stricture series) with a 25% failure rate in one two-center report; it is **not** demonstrably better than percutaneous rendezvous (technical success 89.2% vs 95.8%; AEs 9.8% vs 13.4%, with more pancreatitis and bile leak after EUS-rendezvous). In double biliary + gastric outlet obstruction, **EUS-CDS carries a 5.04-fold relative risk of recurrent biliary obstruction versus EUS-HGS** (food impaction above the duodenal stricture), with similar AEs.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

**Versus [[acg-2023-biliary-strictures|ACG 2023]] (both tier-1; ESGE 2026 is newer by publication date and takes the page):**
- **Benign extrahepatic stricture, stent choice.** ACG 2023 prefers an FCSEMS over MPSs for benign extrahepatic strictures generally (conditional, low). ESGE 2026 makes the answer etiology-specific and **puts MPSs first for postcholecystectomy strictures**, reserving FCSEMS for strictures >2 cm from the confluence or a patient who will not return for repeated ERCPs. Both agree FCSEMS costs fewer procedures; they disagree on which default a postcholecystectomy stricture gets.
- **Hilar drainage target.** ACG 2023 says >50% of nonatrophic liver volume. ESGE 2026 raises it to **>50% and ideally ≥70%** of *viable* nonatrophic volume, for proven malignant Bismuth II–IV strictures.
- **Hilar ERCP vs PTBD.** ACG 2023 found insufficient evidence to prefer either. ESGE 2026 issues a **strong** recommendation for ERCP over PTBD in the preoperative setting, and prefers ERCP ± EUS-BD over primary PTBD for palliation.
- **Preoperative-drainage threshold.** ACG 2023 names serum bilirubin >14.6 mg/dL as a drainage indication, derived from the exclusion threshold of the same landmark RCT that ESGE quotes as **250 µmol/L (14 mg/dL)**. ESGE 2026 does not print a bilirubin cutoff at all; it uses "severe symptomatic jaundice (e.g. intense pruritus)" plus the surgical-timing window.
- **Hilar stent choice.** ACG 2023 found insufficient evidence to prefer plastic or USEMS. ESGE 2026 keeps both on the table but adds a **decision rule** — plastic while the drainage strategy is undefined or outside an expert center; USEMS once the disease is biopsy-proven and unresectable and complete drainage is predicted.

**Versus [[asge-2023-post-transplant-biliary-strictures|ASGE 2023]] (tier-1; ESGE 2026 newer):**
- ASGE 2023 suggests a **covered SEMS instead of MPSs** for extrahepatic anastomotic strictures after liver transplantation. ESGE 2026 suggests **either**, and attaches an anatomic condition to the FCSEMS (>2 cm distal to the confluence) that ASGE expresses qualitatively ("not just below the bifurcation"). ESGE also surfaces the long-term trade ASGE does not: equal resolution at 1–2 years, but **36% vs 7% recurrence at 5 years** favoring MPSs in the one RCT with that follow-up.
- Dwell time: ASGE 2023 removes a covered SEMS after a fixed 3–6 months; ESGE 2026 says the FCSEMS is **usually left 6 months** and sets MPS therapy at **3–4-month exchanges for 12 months**.

**Versus [[asge-2024-therapeutic-eus-biliary|ASGE 2024]] (tier-1; ESGE 2026 newer):**
- **Strength.** ASGE 2024's EUS-BD-over-PTBD statement is conditional/low. ESGE 2026 makes it **strong, moderate quality** for malignant stricture after failed or unfeasible ERCP.
- **"Malignancy suspected" as a reason to pick PTBD.** ASGE 2024 lists suspected malignancy among the situations where PTBD is preferred over EUS-BD. ESGE 2026 is the opposite — malignant stricture after failed ERCP is precisely where it makes EUS-BD a strong recommendation. ESGE 2026 wins the page.
- **Route choice.** ASGE 2024 says either EUS-HGS or EUS-CDS for distal MBO after failed ERCP. ESGE 2026 adds a hard branch: **a duodenal stricture forces EUS-HGS**, because EUS-CDS obstructs from food impaction (5.04-fold relative risk of recurrent biliary obstruction).
- **Non-bypass altered anatomy.** ASGE 2024 makes EA-ERCP the initial approach for all non-RYGB altered anatomy. ESGE 2026 splits it by etiology — EA-ERCP first for **benign** strictures, but **EUS-BD (or EA-ERCP, by anatomy and expertise) first-line for malignant** ones, with PTBD second.
- **RYGB.** ASGE 2024 prefers EDGE over EA-ERCP and laparoscopy-assisted ERCP across the board. ESGE 2026 makes EDGE first-line only for **benign** strictures and only when there is no specific EA-ERCP expertise, and prefers EUS-BD for malignant strictures.

**Versus [[asge-2021-malignant-hilar-obstruction|ASGE 2021]] and [[asge-2015-ercp-benign-biliary|ASGE 2015]]:** both predate ESGE 2026 and are superseded wherever they conflict on stent choice, drainage route, or volume target; ESGE 2026 adds the explicit ≥70% target and the Bismuth-stratified route that neither prints.
