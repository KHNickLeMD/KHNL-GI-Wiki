## Summary

AGA expert commentary on the diagnosis and management of **cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS)** in adults — a chronic disorder of gut–brain interaction (DGBI) that is common (~2% US prevalence, more common in women) yet markedly under-diagnosed and under-treated, with most patients enduring years of diagnostic delay, futile investigation, and sometimes unnecessary surgery. Half of patients visit the ED at least annually and 1 in 3 become disabled.

CVS is diagnosed clinically by **Rome IV criteria** and unfolds in **4 phases** — inter-episodic, prodromal, emetic, recovery — each with a distinct treatment goal (see Figure 1 of source). The commentary stratifies patients into **mild** vs **moderate–severe** CVS (ANMS-CVSA criteria), which drives whether prophylaxis is offered. It details a limited diagnostic workup to exclude mimics, the CVS-vs-CHS distinction (cannabis timing/duration), and a full menu of prophylactic and abortive pharmacotherapy (Table 1), all necessarily based on case series and expert opinion given the absence of RCTs.

## Key Findings / Claims

*This CPU is a narrative expert commentary; it does not carry numbered Best Practice Advice statements. Decision-critical content is the diagnostic criteria (Box 1) and the medication table (Table 1).*

**Diagnosis — Rome IV criteria for CVS in adults (Box 1, top):**
- Stereotypical episodes of acute-onset vomiting lasting **<7 days**
- **≥3 discrete episodes in a year**, of which **2 in the prior 6 months**; episodes separated by **≥1 week of baseline health**
- Absence of vomiting between episodes (milder symptoms — nausea, occasional vomiting, dyspepsia — may persist)
- Supportive criterion: personal or family history of **migraine**

**Severity (ANMS-CVSA, Box 1 bottom):**
- **Mild:** <4 episodes/yr, each <2 days, no ED visits/hospitalizations → **abortive therapy only**
- **Moderate–severe:** ≥4 episodes/yr, each >2 days, ≥1 ED visit/hospitalization → **prophylactic + abortive therapy**
- **Coalescent CVS:** worsening trajectory → loss of well periods, may meet chronic nausea vomiting syndrome criteria; offer prophylaxis akin to moderate–severe.

**Clinical clues:** ~65% have a prodrome (median 1 h); abdominal pain present in most episodes (does not exclude CVS); episodes often early-morning; hot-water bathing gives relief in ~48% of non-cannabis users (not specific to CHS). Common triggers: stress (70–80%), sleep deprivation, menses, travel/motion, infection/surgery.

**Comorbidities:** mood/anxiety/panic (50–60%), migraine (20–30%), seizure (~3%), POTS/autonomic imbalance — treating these improves CVS outcomes.

**Workup (to exclude mimics):** CBC, electrolytes, glucose, LFTs, lipase, UA; one-time EGD or upper-GI imaging to exclude obstruction (recognize post-retching epiphenomena as non-causal — don't repeat). **Do not routinely order gastric emptying scans.** Consider Addison's, hypothyroidism, and **acute hepatic porphyria** as mimics; brain imaging + neurology if localizing neuro symptoms.

**CVS vs CHS:** CHS proposed as a CVS subset when heavy (>4×/wk, often daily), prolonged (>1 yr) cannabis use **precedes** symptom onset; retrospective CHS diagnosis requires cannabis cessation for **6 months or ≥3 typical cycle lengths** with symptom resolution. Offer abortive/prophylactic therapy regardless of cannabis use.

**Prophylaxis (moderate–severe only) — Table 1:**
- **First-line: TCAs** (amitriptyline/nortriptyline/doxepin). Start 25 mg qhs → goal 75–150 mg or 1–1.5 mg/kg qhs; slow titration; nortriptyline less anticholinergic than amitriptyline.
- **Second-line:** topiramate (start 25 mg → 100–150 mg/d; kidney stones, avoid in pregnancy, teratogenic), zonisamide (100 → 200–400 mg/d; kidney stones), levetiracetam (500 mg bid → 1000–2000 mg/d; safer in pregnancy), aprepitant (NK-1 antagonist; 125 mg 2–3×/wk if >60 kg), CoQ10/riboflavin supplements.

**Abortive (mild or moderate–severe) — Table 1:** triptans (**sumatriptan** 20 mg intranasal or 6 mg SC, may repeat ×1 at 2 h, max 2/24 h; avoid in ischemic heart disease/pregnancy) + antiemetic (**ondansetron** 8 mg SL q4–6h; promethazine/prochlorperazine; both available PR) ± sedatives (diphenhydramine, benzodiazepines — alprazolam available SL/PR). Nearly all patients need **2-agent combinations**.

**ED management:** treat all regardless of CHS suspicion; IV dextrose-containing fluids + IV antiemetics; **IV ketorolac as first-line non-narcotic analgesic**; benzodiazepines for sedation in a quiet, dark room; narcotics only in most severe refractory cases.
