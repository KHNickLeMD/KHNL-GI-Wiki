## Summary

The AASLD **Expert Practice Opinion (EXPO)** that **updates the 2009 AASLD position paper on liver biopsy** ([[aasld-2009-liver-biopsy]]). It covers current indications, techniques, benefits, risks, and limitations. Explicitly **outside its scope**: targeted biopsy of focal lesions, biopsy for clinical trials, and laparoscopic/surgical approaches.

**Methodology matters for how much weight these statements carry.** The document states plainly that **for EXPO documents, unlike AASLD Guidelines, no systematic review and no GRADE methodology was used**. A writing group appointed by the AASLD Practice Guidelines Committee performed a *targeted* literature review and created expert opinion statements that were **unanimously agreed upon**. So the 16 statements are **ungraded expert opinion** — there is no evidence grade or strength rating attached to any of them, and none should be reported as if there were.

The substantive shift from 2009 is that **biopsy is now used selectively**, mostly where diagnostic uncertainty persists after noninvasive work-up, and that **endoscopic ultrasound–guided liver biopsy (EUS-LB) has been promoted to a co-equal third technique** alongside percutaneous and transjugular liver biopsy (TJLB), with its own place in the approach algorithm. The document also **reverses the traditional right-lateral-decubitus recovery position** and **lowers the minimum acceptable specimen length** relative to the older ≥2–3 cm target.

On safety it is deliberately non-prescriptive: there is **no prothrombin time/international normalized ratio (INR) or platelet threshold** that defines high risk, high-quality data are scarce, and decisions about approach and prophylactic blood product transfusion in severely deranged patients **should be individualized in consultation with a hematologist**.

## Key Findings / Claims

### The 16 EXPO statements

Ungraded expert opinion, unanimously agreed; **numbering is the document's own**. *(As published, the statement boxes print statements 4, 5 and 6 a second time, renumbered 8, 9 and 10 — the wording is identical. They are listed here as printed; it appears to be a typesetting repetition rather than three additional statements.)*

**Indications**
1. Liver biopsy should be considered when evaluating **unexplained abnormal liver test results**.
2. Liver biopsy should be considered to **assess liver fibrosis when noninvasive testing is uncertain and/or discordant**.
3. Liver biopsy should be considered when **histologic assessment will inform clinical management decisions**.

**Technique selection**
4. *(repeated as 8)* The **selection of liver biopsy technique should depend on local expertise**, as well as patient-specific factors, including the **presence of ascites or coagulopathy**, **concurrent indications** (such as need for hepatic venous pressure gradient [HVPG]), and **patient preference**.
5. *(repeated as 9)* **Imaging localization** of the liver and adjacent structures **should be used either immediately before or in real time during percutaneous liver biopsy in adults**.
6. *(repeated as 10)* **TJLB can be considered** in the setting of **difficult anatomy** (ie, lack of an ideal anatomical window to perform the biopsy percutaneously), **coagulopathy**, **ascites**, and/or **when HVPG measurements are desired**.
7. **EUS-LB can be considered** in the setting of **difficult anatomy**, **when HVPG measurements are desired**, and/or **when endoscopy is also warranted**.

**Specimen adequacy**
11. To ensure an adequate specimen when performing **percutaneous** liver biopsy, a needle size of **at least 16-gauge or greater is preferred**. For **TJLB and EUS-LB the minimum needle size is 19-gauge**. **For all biopsy types, a specimen length >1.5 cm should be obtained.**
12. **Specimen adequacy should be assessed and reported on** in biopsy interpretation, **including whether there are greater than 11 portal tracts** in the specimen.

**Pathology communication**
13. Communication to the pathologist about **clinical suspicion and biopsy indication should be standardized and clear**.
14. Liver histology **should be interpreted by an expert pathologist** when the initial diagnosis is **ambiguous**, when the diagnosis is **discordant with the clinical presentation**, when the patient has a **complex (or rare) condition**, or when the findings **will dictate high-stakes treatments such as transplantation**.

**Children**
15. Liver biopsy in **infants and children** should be considered for evaluation of **unexplained abnormal liver tests**, for **assessment of fibrosis stage when noninvasive testing is uncertain and/or discordant**, or **when histologic assessment is required for therapeutic management**.
16. The **technical approach** for liver biopsy in infants and children (eg, wedge, percutaneous, transjugular, EUS-guided) **should be selected based on**: quantity of tissue required (number of passes vs wedge), **age/size of patient**, need for sedation, presence of **abnormal hepatic anatomy**, presence of **ascites and/or coagulopathy**, and **local expertise**.

### Approach algorithm (Figure 1)

Patient with abnormal liver tests / suspicion of liver disease → **full noninvasive evaluation** (serologies, imaging, noninvasive liver disease assessment [NILDA]) → diagnosis established → liver disease management. **No diagnosis** → plan for liver biopsy → **special considerations or contraindications?**

- **Yes → TJLB** for: coagulopathy · ascites · altered anatomy · desire to know HVPG.
- **Yes → EUS-LB** for: indication for endoscopy · altered anatomy · desire to know portal pressures.
- **No → percutaneous liver biopsy.**

### Technique comparison (Figure 2)

| | Percutaneous | TJLB | EUS-LB |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Operator(s)** | Hepatology, Radiology | Hepatology, Interventional radiology | Advanced endoscopist |
| **Needle size** | **16–18-gauge** | **18–19-gauge** | **19-gauge preferred** |
| **Major bleeding risk** | **0.5%–2%** | **0.6%–0.7%** | **0.6%–0.9%** (fine-needle technique may have higher bleeding risk) |
| **Pain** | +++ | ++ | + |
| **Advantages** | Fast, widely available; good specimen length/quality; low cost; minimally invasive | **Safest in coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, or significant ascites**; can measure HVPG; lower risk of capsular bleeding | Can measure **direct portal pressures**; useful when completing upper endoscopy for other reasons |
| **Disadvantages** | Suitable when anatomy is typical, **higher risk if anatomy atypical**; limited in ascites or severe coagulopathy | Requires more equipment and specialized expertise; **may obtain smaller, sometimes fragmented specimens**; limited availability; high cost | Requires advanced endoscopy expertise; **highly resource intensive** (requires anesthesia); limited availability; high cost |
| **Typical use cases** | Most common technique | Bleeding risk; ascites or obesity; HVPG wanted | Patients needing endoscopic evaluation; difficult anatomy or obesity |

- Percutaneous needles named: Menghini, Jamshidi, Klatskin, Tru-Cut, Vim-Silverman (not common). **Ultrasound is the preferred imaging modality** — no ionizing radiation (unlike computed tomography), allows real-time visualization and immediate adjustment, more economical and readily available.
- TJLB specimens are taken with devices obtaining cores **15–20 mm in length**. **Perivenular specimens that primarily include a longitudinal piece of vein wall are inadequate** — care must be taken to ensure liver parenchyma is included.
- EUS-LB uses preferably a **19-gauge EUS needle** with the **"wet heparin suction" technique**. A prospective randomized trial showed **19-gauge fine-needle biopsy yielded longer cores and aggregate specimen lengths than a 22-gauge** fine-needle biopsy, with less fragmentation during tissue processing. EUS-LB is **performed under sedation, which must be considered in the risk profile**.

### Complications by approach (Table 2)

| | Percutaneous | TJLB | EUS-LB |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pain (severe)** | 20%–30%, immediate–hours | 10%–20% (neck or local needle site), immediate–hours | 10%–15% (epigastric or right upper quadrant), immediate–hours |
| **Major haemorrhage** | Intra-abdominal/intraparenchymal **0.3%–0.6%**, within 6–24 h | Intraperitoneal **<0.1%–0.3%**, within 24 h | Intra-abdominal/intraparenchymal **0.50%**, within 6–24 h |
| **Other minor** | Vasovagal 1%–3%; minor bleeding (subclinical hematoma) 1%–2%; hypotension (nonvasovagal) <1% | Transient arrhythmias 1%–5%; neck hematoma 1%–2%; **capsular perforation without bleeding 0.1–0.3** | Sore throat/dysphagia <5%; transient hypotension <1% |
| **Other major** | Hemobilia <0.1% (days, up to ~5 d); bile peritonitis <0.1%; pneumothorax/hemothorax <0.1% | Major neck hematoma <0.5%; **carotid artery puncture <0.5%** | Perforation (GI tract) <0.1%; bile leak/bile peritonitis very rare |
| **Death** | **~0.01%** | **~0.01%** | Very rare |

- Overall: major adverse event rate of percutaneous biopsy is low, **major complication being bleeding in ~0.3%–0.6%**; **mortality <0.1%**.
- **Pain is the most common complication, in up to 80%**, typically minor, mild and transient — right upper quadrant or **right shoulder** (referred from stretching of the liver capsule). **Severe pain occurs in <10%.** In a large Italian multicenter study of **1838 biopsies, severe pain was the sole significant independent predictor of major complications (hazard ratio 30.6, p<0.05)**. Severe pain needing hospitalization usually warrants radiological evaluation — contrast-enhanced computed tomography or computed tomography angiography.
- **Major complications of TJLB occur in <1%** and are similar to percutaneous, including perforation of other organs (especially lung and gallbladder), intrahepatic arteriovenous fistula, vena cava injury, hemoperitoneum, and capsular perforation and bleeding.

### Safety in specific conditions

- **Hepatic amyloidosis:** a long-standing concern about bleeding risk that **larger studies have failed to confirm**. In 98 patients with biopsy-proven hepatic amyloidosis (1975–1997), **no hepatic rupture or death** due to liver biopsy; **4 patients (4%) bled**. In another study, bleeding complications occurred in **5.6% with amyloidosis vs 4.2% without**.
- **Sickle cell disease:** percutaneous biopsy **during acute intrahepatic sickle crisis has been considered a contraindication**. In 14 patients, **5 of 14 (36%) suffered serious hemorrhage, 4 of whom died (28% overall mortality)**; **4 of the 5 with complications were in acute sickle crisis**, whereas **none of the 9 without complications were in crisis**. **Timing matters — acute vs stable disease state.**
- Histopathologic features strongly associated with complications: chronic venous outflow obstruction, marked hepatic sequestration of erythrocytes, sinusoidal dilatation.

### Coagulation, antithrombotics and sedation

- **No specific prothrombin time/INR threshold defines high risk**, and in general studies suggest **no clear difference in bleeding risk between patients with and without cirrhosis**. The current **AASLD Guidance on Procedural Bleeding classifies both percutaneous biopsy and TJLB as high-risk-of-bleeding procedures**, but makes **no recommendation regarding specific INR or platelet thresholds that require correction**.
- For **thrombocytopenia**, bleeding risk appears to increase with decreasing platelet levels, but **cutoffs at which platelet transfusion is warranted are unclear**. In 497 patients with cirrhosis and thrombocytopenia undergoing ultrasound-guided biopsy, of whom 42 received prophylactic platelet transfusion, **there was no effect on rates of any bleeding (odds ratio 1.318; p=0.449) or major bleeding (OR 1.754; p=0.420)**.
- A recent study of elective percutaneous biopsies (2018–2021) did identify **thrombocytopenia as an independent risk factor for post-biopsy bleeding**, but did not specify whether patients had cirrhosis.
- **Propensity-matched data favour TJLB on hematoma only:** in 1467 patients matched for coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, ascites and other factors, TJLB had a **significantly lower rate of hematoma formation (0.2% vs 1.2%, p=0.049)**, with **no differences in other complications, readmission, or mortality**. In another study of 1300 patients, bleeding requiring transfusion was rare in both groups (n=2 TJLB, n=1 percutaneous) with 1 death in the TJLB group.
- **The document's own suggestion:** in patients with **severe derangements in coagulation or platelet levels** undergoing percutaneous biopsy, decisions about the best approach and whether prophylactic blood product transfusion is warranted **should be individualized and take place in consultation with a hematologist**. In patients with derangements in coagulation or platelets, **or who are anticoagulated or require antiplatelet therapy, the TJLB approach is usually preferred**.
- **Aspirin:** in 3636 patients, bleeding risk after percutaneous biopsy in those **taking aspirin within 10 days (n=585) was the same as in those not taking aspirin (n=3051)**. In aggregate, **insufficient data exist about aspirin or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs** to make robust recommendations.
- **Hold intervals for percutaneous biopsy, as stated:** **warfarin** typically held **~5 days with a target INR ≤1.5**; **direct oral anticoagulants** (eg apixaban, rivaroxaban) held **2–3 days (longer with renal impairment)**; **clopidogrel** usually stopped **5–7 days** before biopsy.
- **Desmopressin (DDAVP)** may be considered in **uremia**, on the premise that uremia affects platelet function and DDAVP shortens bleeding time; however **high-quality evidence is lacking and its effect on clinically significant bleeding complications remains uncertain**. Reasonable in high-risk patients with prolonged bleeding time, but **routine premedication is not supported by current evidence**.
- **Sedation:** none (other than local analgesia) to general anesthesia. Percutaneous typically **moderate sedation** (most often a benzodiazepine with or without an opioid analgesic). **EUS-LB sedation is almost always deep**, typically monitored anesthesia care with propofol. For **TJLB combined with HVPG**, sedating medications may **alter portal pressure and thus affect HVPG values**; available data suggest **propofol, but not fentanyl alone, may impact HVPG interpretation**, so **TJLB with HVPG may be performed with little or no sedation**. Patients with advanced liver disease may have **altered clearance of sedation medications**.

### Post-procedure care — the positioning reversal

- **The traditional practice of placing patients in the right lateral decubitus position after percutaneous liver biopsy is not supported by evidence and may actually cause more discomfort.** The rationale (liver compressing against the lateral abdominal wall to limit bleeding) is **longstanding convention rather than evidence**.
- In 90 patients randomized to right-side, supine, or combined (right-side then supine) positions, the **right lateral decubitus group experienced significantly more pain immediately after biopsy** — mean visual analog scale **26.5/100 vs 14.2 combined and 12.1 supine** (p=0.026 and p=0.009) — **with no difference in complication rates**. A 2019 study randomizing 90 patients to right-side, dorsal, or combined found **no differences in major adverse events or pain levels**; notably the **right-side position group rated the procedure as significantly less acceptable** than the dorsal or combined groups (p=0.001 and p=0.002).
- **Monitoring:** all patients monitored per local protocol; **no evidence supports a particular duration**, but monitoring should include **both vital signs and patient symptoms**. After percutaneous biopsy, patients are typically monitored in a recovery area with **bed rest for 2–4 hours**, with regular assessment of vital signs, pain, and the biopsy site, **as most bleeding complications occur within the first few hours**. Observe for **increasing abdominal pain, hypotension, tachycardia, or shoulder pain**.
- **After TJLB**, monitoring focuses on hemodynamics, neck access site inspection, **cardiac rhythm (due to right atrial/hepatic vein instrumentation)**, and signs of capsular or venous bleeding, **usually 2–4 hours** and longer if moderate sedation, coagulopathy, or cardiopulmonary comorbidity is present.
- **After EUS-LB**, patients are monitored as for other advanced endoscopic procedures; **most adverse events present early and same-day discharge is appropriate if the patient remains stable and asymptomatic**.

### Specimen adequacy — the evidence behind statements 11 and 12

- **Length and width are proxies for the yield of sufficient portal tracts.** The shorter and/or narrower the specimen, the less likely it is to accurately assess disease activity/severity.
- In 161 specimens, biopsies **≥3 cm in length and 1.4 mm in width** compared with biopsies **1.5 cm** in length — the **larger biopsies yielded improved detection of both inflammation and advanced fibrosis**.
- Interobserver variation: specimens **≥20 mm** had **kappa 0.98 for fibrosis score and 0.94 for inflammatory activity**, much greater than specimens of 15 mm (**0.92 and 0.77**). **Specimens of 15 mm in length had >1 fibrosis stage difference between pathologists in 20% of specimens.**
- **Current guidance: an acceptable specimen has no fewer than 11 portal tracts**, and **the presence of fewer than 11 complete portal tracts should be noted in the formal pathology report**, with the understanding that **diagnosis, grading and staging may be erroneous** due to insufficient sample size.
- **Interobserver concordance for fibrosis staging is only 78% even among expert pathologists.** Fibrosis is variable and staging varies among samples: in laparoscopic samples taken simultaneously from right and left lobes in 124 patients with HCV, biopsy findings in **15%** were interpreted as cirrhosis in one lobe but F3 fibrosis in the other. In 51 patients with metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease with specimens from each lobe, **35% were noted to have F3 fibrosis in one sample and F0 or F1 in the other**.
- **A liver biopsy specimen is only 1/50,000th of the liver volume**, and many conditions do not affect the liver uniformly.
- **Diagnostic yield:** diagnoses can be made in **74%–94%** of patients undergoing liver biopsy for unexplained elevation in liver tests; **normal histology is still identified in as frequently as 15%** of patients with unexplained liver test elevations.

- **Electron microscopy may be required** in storage disorders — a second pass and special processing may be necessary.
- **Histopathology requisitions should include a clinical question** providing a differential diagnosis or a specific query, plus age, sex, key comorbidities, liver enzyme levels (**at a minimum alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and bilirubin**), results of pertinent serological testing and liver-directed imaging, and any known exposures.

### Indications in detail (Table 1)

| Indication | Noninvasive assessment | Noninvasive alternative to biopsy | Clinical scenarios to proceed with biopsy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unexplained elevation in liver tests** | History, physical exam, directed serological testing, liver imaging | Assessment of response to empiric interventions | Elevated alanine aminotransferase **despite weight loss and alcohol cessation or discontinuation of herbal supplements**; elevated alkaline phosphatase with **normal anti-mitochondrial antibody and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography without known risks for drug-induced liver injury**; suspected autoimmune hepatitis **with consideration of immunosuppressive therapy**; **ruling out drug-induced autoimmune-like hepatitis when drug-induced liver injury is suspected** |
| **Fibrosis assessment** | Blood and/or imaging-based noninvasive liver disease assessment | Additional noninvasive testing, including more advanced imaging | **Discrepant results** — eg high Fibrosis-4 index, nodular liver, and low liver stiffness; **high-stakes clinical decisions**, such as patients with heart failure and nodular liver undergoing heart transplant evaluation; **payer requires biopsy confirmation of fibrosis stage before initiation of therapy** |
| **Clinical management decisions** | Monitoring of liver tests and noninvasive liver disease assessment | Serial liver enzymes, noninvasive testing, clinical assessment | Patients with **autoimmune hepatitis considering discontinuation of immunosuppression**; **liver transplant recipients with abnormal liver tests who may need adjustment in immunosuppression** |

- Histology remains **integral in the management of abnormal liver tests after liver transplantation**, and is useful in **dual diagnoses or "overlap syndrome"** (features of both autoimmune hepatitis and primary biliary cholangitis), and in **diffuse infiltrative disease** (eg amyloidosis, granulomatous hepatitis, malignancies).

### Liver biopsy in children (Table 4)

| Technique | Sedation/anesthesia | Tissue collection |
|---|---|---|
| Percutaneous (**15–16G needle**) | **Infant:** minimal sedation, **transabdominal approach**<br/>**Toddler:** moderate sedation<br/>**Child/teenager:** moderate sedation | **1 cm if <2.5 kg**<br/>**2 cm if >2.5–15 kg**<br/>**3 cm if >15 kg** |
| Ultrasound-guided percutaneous | As percutaneous, but may require general anesthesia | Can also sample focal lesions |
| Computed tomography–guided percutaneous | As percutaneous, but may require general anesthesia | Can also sample focal lesions |
| Transjugular (**18–19G needle**) | **Lower size limit 8–10 kg**; moderate sedation to general anesthesia | **Multiple passes can be collected more safely** than percutaneous |
| Laparoscopic | All ages: general anesthesia | **Wedge biopsy useful for enzyme analysis** |
| EUS-guided | All ages: general anesthesia | **Longer tissue core** |

- Genetic testing (gene panels or whole exome sequencing) **has largely but not completely replaced** liver biopsy for suspected metabolic liver disease in infants and young children.
- **Percutaneous biopsy in infants requires a transabdominal rather than transthoracic approach** in most cases, due to the **lower position of the liver in the abdomen**. **Urgent assessment for biliary obstruction in cholestasis is the most common indication** in infants; the differential of neonatal cholestasis is broader than in older children and **electron microscopy has greater potential impact**.
- Infant percutaneous biopsy can be done **at the bedside with minimal sedation** (such as high-concentration sucrose solution by mouth and local analgesia). It **can be combined with gallbladder cholangiogram** in interventional radiology; if suspicion for biliary atresia is low, this combination is **most efficient when the cholangiogram is normal** (bile ducts filling into the liver and draining into the duodenum) and open procedures are avoided.
- In 275 liver biopsies in 190 children, **major bleeding was rare**. Children treated with **aspirin within 5 days of biopsy did not have significantly increased risk for bleeding**; however, **low-molecular-weight heparin within 24 hours before a biopsy may increase risk**.
- Data on larger needle size and number of passes **do not demonstrate an increased risk of bleeding**; in practice clinicians generally strive to limit the number of needle passes.
- **Throw length of an automated needle should be adjusted for the size of the patient** — eg **1 cm for <3 kg neonates, 2 cm for older infants/toddlers (3–20 kg), 3 cm for children 2 years (20 kg) and older**.
- In younger children, **a needle of smaller gauge produces smaller-caliber specimens that may fragment on removal** and **may lack an adequate number of portal tracts** for high-quality pathologic review.
- **EUS-LB has been used safely in children** with a high technical success rate and adequate specimen retrieval.

### Future directions
- Liver biopsy has undergone substantial change since its inception ~7 decades ago; its future "will likely be characterized by integration with advanced technologies rather than replacement," including artificial intelligence and linkage to multi-omics (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics).
- **Noninvasive liver disease assessment is expected to remain an important complement to histopathological evaluation, rather than eliminate it.**
- **EUS-LB is named as a major new technical advance**, combining advantages of percutaneous and transjugular approaches by facilitating bilateral lobe sampling, simultaneous portal pressure measurement, and concurrent endoscopic indications; "its role in liver disease management is expected to continue to evolve."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Supersedes [[aasld-2009-liver-biopsy]]** (same society, newer; the document states it is an update of that guidance). Where they differ, **2026 governs the page**:
  - **Minimum specimen length:** 2009 recommended **≥2–3 cm** (ideal ~3 cm after fixation). 2026 statement 11 sets **>1.5 cm for all biopsy types**. These are different standards, not a restatement — 2026 is the lower, operative floor, while the evidence it cites still shows ≥20 mm gives materially better interobserver concordance. Both belong on the page: >1.5 cm is the minimum to accept, longer is better.
  - **Image guidance:** 2009 Rec 13 made ultrasound marking *preferred, not mandatory* (Class I/B). 2026 statement 5 says imaging localization **should be used** immediately before or in real time for percutaneous biopsy in adults, and the text says it **is currently recommended for all percutaneous liver biopsies**. Stronger.
  - **Recovery position:** 2009 described right lateral decubitus as conventional with questionable benefit. 2026 states it is **not supported by evidence and may cause more discomfort**, citing randomized data. 2026 governs.
  - **Needle gauge:** 2009 recommended ~16-gauge cutting needles and warned that thin (<18-gauge) needles cause diagnostic error. 2026 keeps ≥16-gauge as preferred for percutaneous but sets **19-gauge as the minimum for TJLB and EUS-LB** — a route-specific standard 2009 did not give.
  - **EUS-LB:** 2009 did not address it. No conflict; 2026 fills the gap.
- **Not a contradiction but a scope note:** EXPO statements are **ungraded expert opinion with no systematic review and no GRADE**, whereas [[aasld-2009-liver-biopsy]] carried Class/Level ratings. Newer and same society, so 2026 governs on substance, but the page should not present its statements as carrying an evidence grade — they carry none.
- **Specimen adequacy vs [[acg-2025-eus-quality|ACG/ASGE 2025]]:** ACG/ASGE's EUS-LB adequacy definition (**>15 mm with >6 complete portal triads**) is close to AASLD 2026's >1.5 cm but uses a **lower portal-tract count** than AASLD's **>11**. AASLD 2026 is newer by a year and is the liver society; ACG/ASGE's figure is explicitly described as the definition "used in EUS-LB studies," against a stricter society standard. Both are reported, neither is silently dropped.
- **Statement numbering:** the published statement boxes **print statements 4, 5 and 6 a second time as 8, 9 and 10**, with identical wording. Recorded as printed; no attempt made to renumber.
- **Open, by the document's own admission:** no INR or platelet threshold defining high risk; cutoffs for platelet transfusion unclear; no prospective or randomized head-to-head comparison of biopsy technique in coagulopathy; insufficient data on aspirin/non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs; no evidence supporting a particular duration of post-procedure monitoring.
