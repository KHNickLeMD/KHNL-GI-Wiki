## Summary

Fourteen best practice advice (BPA) statements on the **medical** management of colonic diverticulitis, commissioned and approved by the AGA Institute Clinical Practice Updates Committee. The document is an Expert Review: the BPA are **numbered but carry no GRADE or evidence rating**, and the authors state in their conclusion that the advice rests on **low- to moderate-quality evidence**.

The update reframes several long-standing habits. Computed tomography (CT) is positioned as the diagnostic test because clinical suspicion alone is right only 40%–65% of the time. Antibiotics move from routine to **selective** in immunocompetent patients with mild uncomplicated disease, and BPA 7 supplies the explicit criteria that push a patient back into the antibiotic group. Post-episode colonoscopy becomes conditional on history, recency of the last examination, and disease course rather than automatic. Most consequentially for surgical referral, **episode count is removed as an indication for elective resection** (BPA 13), replaced by a personalised discussion (BPA 14).

The natural-history data behind those decisions are the page-level payload: complicated disease is usually the **first** presentation, not a late one, and the risk of complication *falls* with recurrences — the inverse of what patients (and many clinicians) assume. Roughly 40%–50% of diverticulitis risk is genetic, which the update asks clinicians to tell patients explicitly so that dietary self-blame is defused.

The update is also notable for what it removes: mesalamine, probiotics, and rifaximin are all named as ineffective for preventing recurrence, and the nut/corn/popcorn and small-seed restrictions are contradicted outright.

## Key Findings / Claims

### The 14 Best Practice Advice statements (verbatim; the document numbers them and assigns no evidence grades)

1. **Best Practice Advice 1:** "Computed tomography should be considered to confirm the diagnosis of diverticulitis in patients without a prior imaging-confirmed diagnosis and to evaluate for potential complications in patients with severe presentations. Imaging should also be considered in those who fail to improve with therapy, are immunocompromised, or who have multiple recurrences and are contemplating prophylactic surgery in order to confirm the diagnosis and location(s) of disease."
2. **Best Practice Advice 2:** "Whether patients should have a colonoscopy after an episode of diverticulitis depends on the patient's history, most recent colonoscopy, and disease severity and course. Colonoscopy is advised after an episode of complicated diverticulitis and after a first episode of uncomplicated diverticulitis, but can be deferred if a recent (within 1 year) high-quality colonoscopy was performed."
3. **Best Practice Advice 3:** "After an acute episode of diverticulitis, colonoscopy should be delayed by 6–8 weeks or until complete resolution of the acute symptoms, whichever is longer. Colonoscopy should be considered sooner if alarm symptoms are present."
4. **Best Practice Advice 4:** "In patients with a history of diverticulitis and chronic symptoms, ongoing inflammation should be excluded with both imaging and lower endoscopy. If there is no evidence of diverticulitis, visceral hypersensitivity should be considered and managed accordingly."
5. **Best Practice Advice 5:** "A clear liquid diet is advised during the acute phase of uncomplicated diverticulitis. Diet should advance as symptoms improve."
6. **Best Practice Advice 6:** "Antibiotic treatment can be used selectively, rather than routinely, in immunocompetent patients with mild uncomplicated diverticulitis."
7. **Best Practice Advice 7:** "Antibiotic treatment is advised in patients with uncomplicated diverticulitis who have comorbidities or are frail, who present with refractory symptoms or vomiting, or who have a C-reactive protein >140 mg/L or baseline white blood cell count >15 × 10⁹ cells/L. Antibiotic treatment is advised in patients with complicated diverticulitis or uncomplicated diverticulitis with a fluid collection or longer segment of inflammation on CT scan."
8. **Best Practice Advice 8:** "Immunocompromised patients are more likely to present with severe or complicated disease. For these patients there should be a low threshold for cross-sectional imaging, antibiotic treatment, and consultation with a colorectal surgeon."
9. **Best Practice Advice 9:** "To reduce the risk of recurrence, patients with a history of diverticulitis should consume a high-quality diet, achieve or maintain a normal body mass index, be routinely physically active, and not smoke. Additionally, patients with a history of diverticulitis should avoid regular use (2 or more times per week) of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs except aspirin prescribed for secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease."
10. **Best Practice Advice 10:** "Patients should understand that approximately 50% of the risk for diverticulitis is attributable to genetic factors."
11. **Best Practice Advice 11:** "Patients with a history of diverticulitis should not be treated with mesalamine, probiotics, or rifaximin to prevent recurrent diverticulitis."
12. **Best Practice Advice 12:** "Patients should be educated that complicated diverticulitis is most often the first presentation of diverticulitis. The risk of complicated diverticulitis decreases with recurrences."
13. **Best Practice Advice 13:** "An elective segmental resection should not be advised based on the number of diverticulitis episodes."
14. **Best Practice Advice 14:** "A discussion of elective segmental resection for patients with a history of diverticulitis should be personalized to consider severity of disease, patient preferences and values, as well as risks and benefits, including quality of life. Patients should understand that surgery reduces, but does not eliminate, diverticulitis risk, and that chronic gastrointestinal symptoms do not always improve with surgery."

### Definitions and disease spectrum
- **Uncomplicated** — thickening of the colon wall + peri-colonic inflammatory changes.
- **Complicated** — additionally abscess, peritonitis, obstruction, stricture, and/or fistula. Only **12%** of patients present with complicated disease; most common complication is phlegmon or abscess, then peritonitis, obstruction, stricture, fistula.
- **Smoldering diverticulitis** — ~**5%** of patients; abdominal pain + continued inflammation on CT. A **distinct diagnosis** from segmental colitis associated with diverticular disease.
- **Segmental colitis associated with diverticular disease** — rare; diverticular colitis that **spares the rectum**; likely on the inflammatory bowel disease spectrum.

### Diagnostic performance
- Clinical suspicion alone correct in only **40%–65%**.
- CT abdomen/pelvis with oral and intravenous contrast — **sensitivity/specificity 95%**.
- Abdominal ultrasonography — avoids contrast and radiation, operator-dependent, used more in Europe.
- Magnetic resonance imaging — very sensitive but **less specific than CT**; generally not used acutely.
- Plain radiographs — cannot confirm the diagnosis; useful for perforation or obstruction.

### Colon cancer risk after an episode (drives BPA 2)
- Meta-analysis, 31 studies / 50,445 patients: pooled colon cancer prevalence **1.9%**.
- **Complicated 7.9% vs uncomplicated 1.3%.**
- Advanced adenomas **4.4%**; adenomas **14.2%** among those undergoing lower endoscopy.
- In a randomised trial of observation vs antibiotics, **1.2%** were diagnosed with colon cancer within 3 months of randomisation.
- **Alarm symptoms** (bring colonoscopy forward): change in stool caliber, iron-deficiency anemia, blood in stool, weight loss, abdominal pain.
- Recurrent uncomplicated disease → routine colorectal cancer screening/surveillance intervals **unless** alarm symptoms.

### Risk factors for progression to complicated disease (the BPA 7 decision inputs)
- Overall risk of progression in acute uncomplicated diverticulitis: **5%**.
- Baseline American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification **III or IV**.
- Symptom duration **>5 days** before presentation.
- Presence of **vomiting**.
- **C-reactive protein >140 mg/L.**
- **Baseline white blood cell count >15 × 10⁹ cells/L.**
- **Fluid collection or longer segment of inflammation on baseline CT — 86 mm vs 65 mm.**
- Any one of these → treat as high risk, give a course of antibiotics.

### Antibiotic regimens and duration
- **Outpatient, mild uncomplicated:** oral fluoroquinolone + metronidazole, **or** monotherapy with oral amoxicillin-clavulanate. Duration **usually 4–7 days**, can be longer.
- Duration individualised on general health status, immune status, severity of presentation, CT findings, patient expectations.
- **Immunosuppressed:** broad-spectrum gram-negative + anaerobic coverage, **longer duration 10–14 days**.
- Evidence for withholding antibiotics: systematic review/meta-analysis of 9 studies, 2505 patients — no difference in time to resolution, readmission, progression to complication, or need for surgery. **Limited to immunocompetent patients without sepsis.**

### Immunosuppression
- Corticosteroid use is a risk factor for diverticulitis and contributes to perforation and death; same for chemotherapy and transplant regimens (risks less well defined).
- Immunosuppressed patients can present with **milder** signs and symptoms → low threshold for CT.
- After an episode managed without surgery, the chronically immunosuppressed patient **should consult a colorectal surgeon** to discuss elective resection.

### Recurrence natural history (the BPA 12–14 decision inputs)
- ~**20%** have ≥1 recurrence within 10 years; ~**8%** within the first year.
- After a **second** episode: **18% at 1 year, 55% at 10 years**. After a **third**: **40% at 3 years**.
- Recurrent diverticulitis is associated with a **reduced** risk of complicated disease — odds ratio **0.78** (95% confidence interval 0.62–0.98).
- Among 386 patients with complicated diverticulitis, **286 (74%) had no prior history** of diverticulitis.
- After an abscess managed without surgery: recurrence **25% within 5 years**.

### Elective resection — what the numbers actually show
- At 5-year follow-up, recurrent diverticulitis **15% after elective surgery vs 61% managed nonoperatively**.
- In 2 studies, **22%–25%** continued to have ongoing abdominal pain **after** surgery.
- After complicated diverticulitis managed without surgery, long-term emergency surgery and/or death was **low (5%)**; elective resection was **not** associated with reduced emergency surgery or death.
- Rates of elective colectomy have continued to **increase** in the United States despite guidelines moving to a case-by-case approach.

### Chronic symptoms after an episode (BPA 4)
- Periodic abdominal pain in ~**45%** at 1-year follow-up.
- Exclude ongoing inflammation, diverticular stricture or fistula, ischemic colitis, constipation, and inflammatory bowel disease with **both** CT abdomen/pelvis with oral and intravenous contrast **and** lower endoscopy.
- If the workup is negative → visceral hypersensitivity; the authors reassure the patient and, if needed, treat with a **low to modest dose of a tricyclic antidepressant**.

### Risk-factor detail behind BPA 9 and 10
- **Decrease risk:** prudent dietary pattern (high fibre from fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes; low red meat and sweets), vegetarian diet, physical activity (particularly vigorous).
- **A fibre supplement is not a replacement for a high-quality diet.**
- **Not associated with risk:** nut, corn, and popcorn consumption; fruits with small seeds (strawberries, blueberries).
- **Increase risk:** obesity (particularly central), weight gain, smoking, alcoholism (but not alcohol consumption by itself), regular nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug use (greater for non-aspirin than aspirin), opiate analgesics, corticosteroids, menopausal hormone therapy (risk not dose- or duration-dependent).
- **Genetics:** twin and sibling studies attribute **40%–50%** of risk to genetic effects; risk **3×** higher for siblings of cases; higher in monozygotic than dizygotic twins. >30 susceptibility loci identified; 4 with stronger effects for diverticulitis than for diverticulosis — *PHGR1*, *FAM155A*, *CALCB*, *S100A10*.

### Epidemiology
- United States: >1.9 million outpatient visits and 208,000 inpatient admissions annually, cost **$5.5 billion**.
- Incidence **180/100,000 persons per year**; incidence in those **40–49 years old rose 132%** from 1980 through 2007.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Genetic attribution is stated two ways within the document.** BPA 10 says "approximately 50%"; the body text says twin and sibling studies attribute "40%–50%". The page carries the range with the BPA figure noted.
- **vs [[acg-2026-diverticulitis]] (newer, same tier).** Where the two overlap, ACG 2026 keeps the page under source priority (within a tier, newer publication date wins). This CPU remains the source for the areas ACG 2026 does not cover — in particular the *first*-episode elective-resection question and the progression-risk cut-points.
- **vs [[aga-2015-acute-diverticulitis]].** Same society, same topic, six years apart. The 2015 guideline is superseded by this update on every overlapping claim; retain 2015 only to document what changed.
- **The document is an Expert Review and carries no evidence grades.** Its own conclusion describes the evidence base as low to moderate quality. Any statement presented elsewhere as a graded AGA recommendation is a mis-citation.
- **Segmental colitis associated with diverticular disease has no page.** It is defined here (rectum-sparing diverticular colitis, likely on the inflammatory bowel disease spectrum) but the definition is all this source gives; a dedicated source would be needed for a full page.
