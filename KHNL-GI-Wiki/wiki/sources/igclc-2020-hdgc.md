## Summary

Updated clinical practice guidelines from the **International Gastric Cancer Linkage Consortium (IGCLC)**, replacing the consortium's 2015 guidelines. Produced by a group of 19 researchers, seven pathologists, ten gastroenterologists, seven breast and gastric surgeons, seven clinical geneticists and genetic counsellors, a pharmacist, and 13 hereditary diffuse gastric cancer (HDGC) advocates or family members, meeting at Wānaka, New Zealand, 16–18 March 2019, with focus groups and specialty-level consensus within writing groups.

HDGC is characterised by a high prevalence of **diffuse gastric cancer (DGC)** and **lobular breast cancer (LBC)**, with a worldwide incidence estimated at **5–10 per 100 000 births**. It is caused largely by inactivating germline variants in ***CDH1*** (E-cadherin); pathogenic variants in ***CTNNA1*** (α-catenin) account for a minority of families.

Randomised trial evidence specific to HDGC is scarce, so the recommendations rest on consensus expert opinion, anecdotal evidence and observational studies rather than formal systematic review; the document itself states that the **strength of evidence is low to moderate** on GRADE, meaning further research is likely or very likely to change the estimates of effect.

The update makes two structural moves. First, the **genetic testing criteria are deliberately relaxed**, mainly by loosening age limits, to redress the balance between the accessibility, cost and acceptability of testing and the increased identification of carriers through multigene panels. Second, **confidence in endoscopic surveillance in expert centres has increased** — it can now be safely offered to those who wish to postpone prophylactic total gastrectomy or whose cancer risk is not well defined — although **prophylactic total gastrectomy remains the recommended option** for gastric cancer risk management in pathogenic *CDH1* variant carriers.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definitions

In this document, **"pathogenic variant" covers both likely pathogenic and pathogenic variants**. HDGC is now defined **genetically, not clinically**:

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **HDGC** | Pathogenic germline *CDH1* or *CTNNA1* variant in an **isolated individual with DGC**, or in a **family with one or more DGC cases in first- or second-degree relatives** |
| **HLBC** (hereditary lobular breast cancer) | Pathogenic *CDH1* variant in an **isolated individual with LBC**, or in a family with one or more LBC cases in first- or second-degree relatives, with **no known DGC** in either situation |
| **HDGC-like** | Family fulfils HDGC genetic testing **family criterion 1 or 2** but has **no identified pathogenic *CDH1* or *CTNNA1* variant**. Requires at least one confirmed DGC case plus another gastric cancer or LBC in a first- or second-degree relative |

- Families with HLBC are **recategorised as having HDGC** if DGC (or an HDGC precursor lesion) is identified in a family member at a later date.
- The HDGC/HLBC distinction acknowledges that **not all families with pathogenic *CDH1* variants are equally at risk of DGC**.

### Genetic testing criteria (Panel 1) — the 2020 revision

*CDH1* testing is recommended when **any one** of the following is met **and cancer diagnoses have been confirmed**. Where a criterion involves two or more cancers, **at least one cancer should have confirmed histology**; where possible, other relevant cancers should also be confirmed. **Histologically confirmed intestinal-type gastric cancer and non-lobular breast cancer must not be used to fulfil testing criteria** — those cancers are not part of HDGC.

**Family criteria** (family members must be **first- or second-degree blood relatives of each other**):

| No. | Criterion |
|---|---|
| 1 | **≥2 cases of gastric cancer in the family regardless of age, with at least one DGC** |
| 2 | **≥1 case of DGC at any age, and ≥1 case of lobular breast cancer at age <70 years, in different family members** |
| 3 | **≥2 cases of lobular breast cancer in family members <50 years of age** |

**Individual criteria:**

| No. | Criterion |
|---|---|
| 4 | **DGC at age <50 years** |
| 5 | **DGC at any age in individuals of Māori ethnicity** |
| 6 | **DGC at any age in individuals with a personal or family history (first-degree relative) of cleft lip or cleft palate** |
| 7 | **History of DGC *and* lobular breast cancer, both diagnosed at age <70 years** |
| 8 | **Bilateral lobular breast cancer, diagnosed at age <70 years** |
| 9 | **Gastric in-situ signet ring cells or pagetoid spread of signet ring cells in individuals <50 years of age** |

- **Testing order:** individuals meeting criteria should have ***CDH1* analysed first**; if no variant is identified, ***CTNNA1*** should be considered.
- **Who to test, when no living affected relative exists:** test an affected person where possible; otherwise consider tissue testing (tumour or healthy tissue) from an affected deceased relative; if neither is possible, consider indirect testing in unaffected family members.
- **What changed from 2015, explicitly:** the **age threshold for isolated DGC rose from <40 to <50 years**; the **age threshold for women with bilateral LBC rose from <50 to <70 years**, at an expected yield of pathogenic *CDH1* variants of approximately **7%**. The 2015 criterion of testing individuals with a personal or family history of cleft lip/palate and DGC, or with HDGC precursor lesions, **remains**.
- **Māori:** approximately **13% of the New Zealand Māori population with advanced DGC** carry a pathogenic germline *CDH1* variant, so **all Māori with confirmed DGC are now recommended to undergo *CDH1* genetic testing**.
- **Japan and South Korea:** the **Japanese Gastric Cancer Association classification** is recommended for DGC instead of the Lauren classification.
- Families with newly identified HDGC presenting with **advanced** gastric cancer can display features of the **non-solid type poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma** subclass. Patients with **multiple signet ring cell carcinoma lesions**, identified either endoscopically or in the gastrectomy specimen, are also recommended to be offered *CDH1* testing.

### Genetic counselling and laboratory standards

- Testing should be offered from the **legal age of consent (generally 16–18 years)**; testing of younger family members can be considered based on family history.
- Testing must be performed in **certified molecular diagnostic laboratories** — CLIA-approved, ISO 15189 accredited, or equivalent.
- Analysis should include **sequencing of the entire open reading frame and intron–exon boundaries, plus copy number analysis of individual exons** to detect deletions or duplications. **Large *CDH1* deletions (including whole exons) are rare — fewer than 5% of pathogenic variants.**
- **Direct-to-consumer results must be validated in a certified laboratory.**
- Variant interpretation should use the **ACMG/AMP** guidelines. A **"likely pathogenic" variant has a 90% likelihood of pathogenicity**, so a 10% chance remains that the variant will later be reclassified as benign.
- **No indication for presymptomatic testing** in families carrying a variant of unknown significance, or a benign or likely benign variant.
- **In-vitro and in-silico functional assays must not be used for *CDH1* variant classification** until clinically validated — the currently published assays cannot be used to predict pathogenicity of *CDH1* missense variants.
- ***CDH1* testing for breast cancer should only be contemplated in women with confirmed LBC** — pathogenic *CDH1* variants appear to be associated only with LBC, not with invasive breast carcinoma of no special type (formerly ductal breast cancer) or other breast cancer types.
- Individuals undergoing **panel tests** that include *CDH1* and *CTNNA1* should receive genetic counselling, with added emphasis on the uncertain risks in families with no history of DGC.
- ***PALB2*** testing could be considered in families where the increased risk remains unexplained, alongside other genes associated with increased gastric cancer risk — ***ATM*, *BRCA2*, the [[lynch-syndrome|Lynch syndrome]] genes, *APC* and *TP53***.
- **Preimplantation genetic diagnosis and prenatal diagnosis** should be discussed during counselling and made available to pathogenic variant carriers; adults of childbearing age should be offered **reproductive genetic advice**.

### Cancer risk in pathogenic *CDH1* variant carriers

Penetrance estimates are **strongly influenced by the clinical criteria used to ascertain the families**, and the document presents them as a range rather than a single figure:

| Estimate | Gastric cancer risk, males | Gastric cancer risk, females | Ascertainment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hansford et al. | **70% by age 80** | **56% by age 80** | Families all meeting the **2010** HDGC clinical criteria |
| Roberts et al. | **42%** | **33%** | Only **37%** of families met the less stringent **2015** criteria |

- **Female breast cancer risk estimates have ranged from 39% to 55%** and have become more consistent between studies.
- Lower gastric cancer risk was also observed in a study in which **39% of families met the 2015 HDGC criteria**.
- Because historical risk is strongly influenced by individual genetic background and lifestyle, **it should not be assumed that the historical risk will equal the risk faced by younger generations**.
- Whether penetrance from **pathogenic missense *CDH1* variants** is substantially lower than from truncating variants **remains unknown**.
- **No strong evidence** that the risk of other cancer types is significantly increased in individuals with a pathogenic *CDH1* variant. In particular, there is **insufficient evidence to recommend additional colorectal cancer screening beyond adherence to national population screening guidelines**.
- Other than *CTNNA1*, **no additional genes predisposing specifically to DGC but not intestinal-type gastric cancer have been identified**, despite panel and whole-exome sequencing efforts.

### Clinical practice recommendations, by genetic result (Figure 1)

The document's management algorithm splits on the test result. *(The four groups below are the document's own; the wiki has not renumbered anything — Figure 1 and the prose are unnumbered.)*

**1. Pathogenic *CDH1* variant carrier, with DGC in the family → HDGC**

- Should be advised to **consider prophylactic total gastrectomy, irrespective of endoscopic findings**.
- Where possible, surgery in **early adulthood, generally between 20 and 30 years of age**.
- **Not recommended in patients older than 70 years** unless there are noteworthy mitigating circumstances — the reason given is increased perioperative risk and prolonged recovery with older age.
- For those **declining or wishing to postpone** gastrectomy: **yearly endoscopy by experienced endoscopists with knowledge of HDGC**.
- ***H. pylori* should be eradicated if present.**
- Breast: **annual breast surveillance**; consider bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy with or without reconstruction.
- **Total gastrectomy if a biopsy is positive.**

**2. Pathogenic *CDH1* variant carrier, no family history of DGC → HLBC**

- Management is **not straightforward**; DGC penetrance is probably significantly lower, though more data are needed. Signet ring cell carcinomas have nonetheless been reported in prophylactic total gastrectomy specimens from carriers with no family history of DGC.
- **Yearly endoscopic surveillance should be offered**, and **prophylactic total gastrectomy should also be considered**, with careful attention to the uncertain gastric cancer risk.
- LBC risk managed with **either yearly surveillance or bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy**.
- **Yearly breast cancer surveillance is recommended in pathogenic *CDH1* variant carriers without a family history of DGC or breast cancer.**

**3. *CDH1* variant of unknown significance**

- Annual endoscopic surveillance **for at least 2 years** may be considered for those families that are **HDGC-like**; a paucity of data means there is a lack of consensus on the clinical utility of surveillance in these groups, and surveillance endoscopy should **ideally be done as part of a research study**.
- **Prophylactic total gastrectomy is not advised** for variant-of-unknown-significance carriers **when endoscopies are negative**.
- Individualised breast cancer risk assessment and surveillance are recommended.

**4. HDGC-like (criteria met, no pathogenic variant)**

- **Consider yearly endoscopic surveillance for at least 2 years**; after 2 years the interval **may be increased**, at the discretion of the endoscopist, based on individual findings in earlier endoscopies and on the family history. A positive biopsy is most likely during the first endoscopy.
- **Start at age 40 years, or 10 years before the earliest case of gastric cancer within the family, with a minimum age of 18 years.**
- **Prophylactic total gastrectomy is not advised** when endoscopies are negative, because of the uncertainty surrounding the level of individual risk.
- **Breast management based on individualised assessment.**
- There are **few data** to support surveillance endoscopy in **first-degree relatives of young individuals with DGC** in the absence of any family history or pathogenic *CDH1*/*CTNNA1* variant.

**5. Pathogenic *CTNNA1* variant carriers**

- Asymptomatic carriers are recommended to undergo **yearly endoscopic surveillance in an expert centre**.
- **Prophylactic total gastrectomy can be considered** depending on the results of the biopsies **and the penetrance of DGC in the family history**.
- **Breast surveillance can be considered on a case-by-case basis** — little is known about *CTNNA1* penetrance.
- Intramucosal DGC foci have been observed in prophylactic total gastrectomy specimens from young asymptomatic *CTNNA1* carriers, suggesting *CDH1* and *CTNNA1* could have similar implications for DGC risk.

### Endoscopic surveillance (Panel 3 — endoscopy key recommendations)

*Condensed from the document's own bulleted panel; it numbers nothing.*

- Surveillance should be **conducted in expert centres familiar with HDGC**.
- **Surveillance instead of prophylactic total gastrectomy can be considered** depending on the individual circumstances and wishes of pathogenic variant carriers.
- **Surveillance instead of prophylactic total gastrectomy should be considered in pathogenic variant carriers with an unclear risk for DGC** — those who have not met HDGC genetic testing criteria, or who carry pathogenic *CTNNA1* variants.
- **Surveillance may be considered** for individuals with a family or personal history of DGC and a *CDH1* variant of unknown significance, and for affected individuals from HDGC-like families and their first-degree relatives; **after two negative endoscopies, intervals can be prolonged** at the discretion of the endoscopist, based on individual findings in earlier endoscopies and on the family history.
- **Surveillance endoscopies should include both targeted and random biopsies.**
- **The number of recommended random biopsies is 28–30** — **three to five cardia, five fundus, ten body, five transition zone, five antrum**.
- **Gastric inlet patches in the oesophagus are registered, inspected and biopsied.**
- **All patients undergoing surveillance should be fully informed about the limitations of endoscopy.**

**Supporting detail:**

- Limitations to be discussed: **DGC can be difficult to visualise on endoscopy, and it is unknown whether surveillance in this context positively affects life expectancy.**
- The **upper age limit** for surveillance endoscopy depends on fitness for gastrectomy, but in general **surveillance in those older than 70 years is probably not purposeful**.
- Signet ring cell carcinoma lesions are detected in gastric biopsies in **40–61%** of *CDH1* variant carriers in expert centres, most often at the **baseline** endoscopy; **older studies report a lower detection rate of 9–16%**. High-definition endoscopes, image-enhancing techniques (e.g. narrow band imaging) and the experience of the endoscopist and pathologist are all likely contributors.
- The **a priori chance of having at least one signet ring cell carcinoma lesion in the total gastrectomy specimen** of a pathogenic *CDH1* carrier is **95%**. Consequently, the clinical relevance of a few superficial (stage T1a) signet ring cell lesions in endoscopic biopsies is questionable — superficial lesions can be very indolent.
- **The goal of surveillance is therefore not to detect every single superficial lesion.** For patients wishing to postpone surgery, the goals are: exclude deeper infiltrating lesions; detect large or numerous signet ring carcinoma T1a lesions (these patients probably have a higher chance of developing higher-stage lesions); and assess changing histology and endoscopic appearance.
- **Staging investigations are advised if** erosive lesions, lesions with a disturbed vascular or pit pattern, or histopathological signs of invasion into or beyond the muscularis mucosae are identified.
- If a signet ring cell carcinoma lesion has **none of these risk indicators**, individual circumstances such as age and comorbidity might mean **postponement of prophylactic total gastrectomy after multidisciplinary team review** — but in that situation **intensified endoscopic monitoring every 6 months** is advised.
- All surveillance programmes should be **audited and ideally included in a prospective clinical trial**.

### Prophylactic total gastrectomy

**Patient selection and preparation**

- The decision should be **careful and deliberate**; patient, family and care coordinators involved early.
- Patients should be offered **preoperative psychological counselling**.
- **Untreated addictions** (food, drug, alcohol, tobacco) complicate recovery and should be addressed preoperatively.
- **Avoid surgery in patients with eating disorders** (anorexia, bulimia) or other psychiatric diagnoses refractory to treatment that impair daily life (e.g. bipolar disorder, severe depression) and could interfere with the decision and recovery.
- A **baseline endoscopy before surgery** should be performed, to ensure there is no endoscopically evident cancer (which would require staging investigations) and to identify coincidental pathology such as [[barretts-esophagus|Barrett's oesophagus]] that might alter the proximal extent of the resection.

**Surgery**

- Should **only be offered by surgeons working in facilities with transparent outcome data** and demonstrable capability in preventing, recognising and managing the complications of total gastrectomy.
- **The surgical approach is not as important as the experience of the surgeon** — minimally invasive (laparoscopic and robotic) approaches affect short-term rather than long-term outcomes versus open surgery.
- Gastrectomy should be **total**, with **intraoperative confirmation of oesophageal squamous mucosa in the proximal margin and duodenal mucosa in the distal margin**.
- **Extended D2 lymphadenectomy is not required and is generally discouraged**, to minimise postoperative morbidity — perigastric lymph node metastases are exceedingly uncommon in the absence of biopsy-proven DGC. A **perigastric D1 lymph node dissection** is a reasonable compromise to avoid understaging the rare patient with a previously unappreciated T2 tumour.

### Breast cancer surveillance and risk-reducing mastectomy (Panel 2)

*Recommendations are more specifically tailored to LBC than general breast guidelines and are consistent with eviQ, NICE, ESMO and NCCN.*

**Surveillance**

- **Begin at age 30 years**, and include **yearly clinical breast examination**.
- **Breast awareness** should be explained, with education about the clinical features of LBC — indrawn nipple or skin tethering, thickening in breast tissue, or subtle change in breast contour, shape or size.
- **Modifiable risk factors** (alcohol intake, exercise, weight) should be discussed.
- **Yearly breast MRI with contrast from age 30 years.** The age at which it should cease is not clear; there might be benefit in continuing beyond 50 years, even in non-dense breasts, because of the greater sensitivity of MRI in detecting LBC.
  - Should ideally be performed **mid-cycle (days 10–14)**, when background breast enhancement is lowest.
  - **No evidence to support use of abbreviated MRI.**
- **Yearly mammography from age 40 years is recommended**, but can be considered from **35–40 years on a case-by-case basis**.
  - **Mammography alone is inadequate for screening in HDGC.**
  - **Not recommended in those younger than 35 years** unless there are clinically suspicious findings.
  - The extra benefit of mammography at the time of MRI is likely to be low, and the option to omit it can be considered case by case.
- **Ultrasound** has a role in women unable to have MRI or with no access to MRI; should be **combined with yearly mammography**, and has a role in investigating symptoms between screening intervals.

**Bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy**

- **Can be considered in HLBC and HDGC.**
- **Not usually recommended in those younger than 30 years, nor generally in those older than 60 years.**
- Skin-sparing and nipple-sparing mastectomy are acceptable provided adequate surgical margins are achievable.
- When **LBC is detected, treatment should follow standard practice**. A woman with a pathogenic *CDH1* variant could choose breast-conserving surgery, but bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy should also be considered, as for any woman at high risk.
- **Lobular carcinoma in situ** is typically a coincidental finding on biopsies done for another reason and **does not mandate risk-reducing mastectomy**; the option should be discussed alongside ongoing surveillance and chemoprevention.
- In a woman with **invasive breast carcinoma of no special type and no family history of LBC or DGC** found on a panel test to carry a pathogenic *CDH1* variant, this is likely to be a sporadic cancer (if pathological review excludes misclassification) and **breast-conserving surgery is acceptable**, with ongoing surveillance.

**Chemoprevention**

- In women at elevated breast cancer risk, chemoprevention with **selective oestrogen receptor modulators (pre-menopausal) or aromatase inhibitors (post-menopausal)** shows about a **50% risk reduction**; benefit is higher in some lobular carcinoma in situ studies, though **no chemoprevention studies are specific to LBC**.
- **Therapeutic levels of chemopreventive agents could be compromised after total gastrectomy.**
- Side-effects of endocrine therapy on quality of life affect uptake and compliance; discussion with a breast specialist is necessary.

### Histopathology

- **Two preinvasive/precursor lesions are recognised exclusively in *CDH1* pathogenic variant carriers** and are important clues to the diagnosis of HDGC:
  1. **In-situ signet ring cell carcinoma** — signet cells with hyperchromatic and depolarised nuclei within the basal membrane of a gland, replacing the normal cells of the gland.
  2. **Pagetoid spread** of a row of signet cells below the preserved epithelium of glands and foveolae, and within the basal membrane.
- The **predominant lesions in HDGC are tiny foci of typical signet cells, usually confined to the superficial lamina propria**, without infiltration beneath the muscularis mucosae. Neoplastic cells are usually small in the deep level at the neck gland zone and enlarge towards the surface.
- Biopsy specimens can also show **non-signet ring cell poorly cohesive (diffuse) gastric cancer with a so-called aggressive phenotype** — pleomorphic or bizarre, diffusely infiltrative cells. These features are **highly suggestive of disease progression and should be described in the pathology report**, to prompt staging and clinical intervention.
- **Advanced HDGC** resembles sporadic DGC: **linitis plastica** with infiltration of the gastric wall by atypical cells with diffuse growth, plus cords, microglands and small mucin lakes.
- **Prophylactic gastrectomy specimens:** macroscopic examination should follow a specific protocol; both the **WHO (2019)** and **Lauren** classifications should be used; **surgical margin analysis is mandatory** to confirm no residual gastric mucosa or tumour at the margins. A **three-level histopathology protocol** is proposed, from the minimum necessary for patient care up to total gastric embedding and mapping.
- The risk of developing signet ring cell carcinoma in **oesophageal cardiac-type glands is unknown**, and the risk in **heterotopic gastric mucosa in the duodenum** is very low.
- **Breast:** no unique histopathological or immunohistochemical findings distinguish *CDH1*-related LBC from sporadic LBC. β-catenin and p120-catenin can be used to confirm lobular phenotype — **p120-catenin shows cytoplasmic staining** (membranous in invasive breast carcinoma of no special type and ductal carcinoma in situ) and **β-catenin is negative in lobular neoplasia**.

### Long-term sequelae after total gastrectomy

Patients should receive **lifelong follow-up with an experienced multidisciplinary team** monitoring nutritional, hormonal, immune, neurocognitive, pharmacokinetic and psychological effects. Patients should be educated about symptoms of **late internal herniation — an urgent, potentially life-threatening complication that can occur at any time after total gastrectomy**.

| Complication | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Early dumping (15–30 min postprandial)** | Smaller meals, chewed well and eaten slowly; avoid drinking with meals |
| **Late dumping (90 min to 3 h postprandial)** | Meals low in sugar and high in protein; multiple small portions (six to eight a day); avoid drinking with meals |
| **Lactose intolerance** | Milk alternatives, lactase supplements |
| **Fat malabsorption** | Low-fat diet; consider pancreatic enzymes; monitor blood levels of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K); start vitamin D supplementation |
| **Small bowel bacterial overgrowth or blind loop syndrome** | Antibiotics, with or without surgery |
| **Dysphagia and anastomotic strictures** | Smaller bites with deliberate mastication; upper endoscopy with balloon dilatation |
| **Altered response to solid oral medication** | Use alternate dosage forms; see Panel 4 |
| **Increased effects of alcohol** | Caution; avoid CNS depressants; do not drive or operate heavy machinery; regular assessment of drinking patterns; screening for alcohol use disorders |
| **Nutritional deficiencies** | High-potency multivitamin with additional **vitamin B12, iron and calcium citrate** (iron and calcium separated by 4–5 h); correct dosing of vitamin B12 essential; for iron-deficiency anaemia, **iron infusions** (cf. oral supplements) |
| **Osteopenia or osteoporosis** | Regular bone density scans (**baseline then every 2–5 years**); adequate calcium citrate (divided doses) and tailored vitamin D; weight-bearing exercise; if osteoporosis present, **intravenous bisphosphonate therapy** |
| **Gallstones** | Low-fat diet; active lifestyle; avoid medications known to cause gallstones (e.g. gemfibrozil) |
| **Bile reflux** | Appropriate length of Roux limb at surgery; elevate head of bed **>30 degrees**; **no oral intake 2–3 h before bed**; avoid dietary triggers (spicy, large, fatty, sugary meals; large amounts of liquid at a time); ingesting appropriate food (soft, dry cracker, Greek yoghurt) may help soothe and carry bile downwards; consider **bile acid sequestrants and sucralfate** |
| **Persistent nausea and vomiting** | Assess thiamine levels, replace (oral or intravenous) when needed; avoid dairy; easy to digest, non-offensive foods; consider **ondansetron wafers** |
| **Early satiety** | Several small meals throughout the day; set a timer to ensure meals are not skipped |
| **Weight loss** | Weight loss of **around 15–20%** is expected after total gastrectomy but **stabilises in 3–6 months**; eat at least **six to eight smaller meals per day and snack frequently**; include protein-fortified or high-calorie (but low-fat) foods |

### Drug absorption after total gastrectomy (Panel 4)

- **Inform all patients** about altered absorption of medicines after total gastrectomy.
- **Substitute solid oral medication with chewable, dispersible or liquid forms**; consider sublingual, topical, vaginal, rectal and parenteral routes.
- Patients may **crush tablets or open capsules and ingest contents separately** when no other dosage form exists and it is safe to do so. Caution with liquids — high sugar content can cause dumping; dispersible tablets might cause abdominal discomfort.
- **Use alternative contraception rather than the oral contraceptive pill** because of impaired absorption.
- Where possible, **avoid medicines that irritate the intestinal mucosa** — NSAIDs, corticosteroids, oral bisphosphonates, aspirin, specific antibiotics, potassium chloride. **Avoid doxycycline.**
- **Avoid medication likely to cause gallstones** (e.g. gemfibrozil).
- **Seek alternatives to drugs requiring an acidic environment for absorption** — carbamazepine, azole antifungals, phenytoin, selegiline. (The increased pH of the intestinal tract also increases exposure to weak acids, including NSAIDs.)
- **Avoid extended-release and other delayed-release formulations**, and delayed-release medication generally.
- **Assess drug–nutrient interactions** (e.g. iron and calcium) as patients supplement post-surgery.
- **Special attention to the quantity and effects of alcohol**; **caution with narrow-therapeutic-window medicines**; regular clinical assessment and review of medicines.

### Sexuality, fertility and pregnancy

- Both total gastrectomy and bilateral mastectomy can substantially affect sexuality — postprandial fullness, bloating, diarrhoea, dumping syndrome and altered alcohol tolerance all interfere with intimate relationships and self-confidence.
- Women not wishing to conceive can be offered an **intrauterine device or another form of contraception that does not require gastrointestinal absorption**.
- Those wishing to conceive should be counselled about **preimplantation genetic diagnosis** and provided with nutritional counselling before and during pregnancy.
- **An interval of at least 6–12 months after surgery is recommended** to allow weight stabilisation and nutritional recovery. Pregnancies after total gastrectomy appear to be normal, though caution is warranted because pregnancies after bariatric surgery show an increased risk of adverse perinatal outcomes (preterm birth, small-for-gestational-age babies, babies admitted to the intensive care unit).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Supersedes [[acg-2015-hereditary-gi-cancer]] on the testing criteria.** ACG 2015 required DGC <40 years for an isolated case; IGCLC 2020 raises this to **<50 years**. ACG 2015 required "≥2 cases of DGC in the family with at least one <50 years"; IGCLC 2020 family criterion 1 requires only **≥2 gastric cancers regardless of age, at least one of them diffuse**. Both newer and from a dedicated consortium, so IGCLC 2020 governs the page; the ACG 2015 criteria are retained and marked superseded, because readers will have met them.
- **Supersedes ACG 2015 on breast surveillance.** ACG 2015 extrapolated from [[brca-pathogenic-variants|BRCA]] protocols — mammography and MRI from age 35, clinical breast exam every 6 months. IGCLC 2020 gives LBC-specific ages: **MRI and clinical examination from 30, mammography from 40** (35–40 case by case), and states that **mammography alone is inadequate**.
- **Supersedes ACG 2015 on colorectal screening.** ACG 2015 advised colonoscopy from age 40 in families that include colon cancer. IGCLC 2020 states there is **insufficient evidence to recommend colorectal cancer screening beyond national population guidelines** for *CDH1* carriers, and finds no strong evidence of increased risk of other cancer types.
- **Penetrance figures conflict with ACG 2015's**, which reported 67–80% gastric cancer risk. IGCLC 2020 gives **70% male / 56% female** from families ascertained on the 2010 criteria and **42% / 33%** from a cohort where only 37% met the 2015 criteria, and explicitly attributes the spread to ascertainment. The page now carries the range with its ascertainment attached rather than a single number.
- **Unresolved in the document itself:** whether missense-variant penetrance is lower than truncating-variant penetrance; whether surveillance affects life expectancy; *CTNNA1* penetrance; the age at which breast MRI should stop.

The main text's clinical decisions are complete without it.
