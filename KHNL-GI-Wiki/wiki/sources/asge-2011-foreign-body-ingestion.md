## Summary

ASGE Standards of Practice guideline covering the evaluation and endoscopic management of ingested foreign bodies and esophageal food bolus impaction in adults and children. Ten GRADE-rated recommendations, plus two tables: the GRADE evidence-quality legend (Table 1) and the timing-of-endoscopy triage table (Table 2), which is the operative decision tool of the document.

Core framing: most ingestions pass spontaneously (≥80% in pre-endoscopic series), so the guideline is organised around identifying the minority that do not — objects that will not pass a fixed anatomic narrowing (>2.5 cm diameter at the pylorus; >6 cm length at the duodenum), objects that injure while they sit (disk batteries in the esophagus, magnets, sharp-pointed objects), and patients whose airway is at risk (unable to manage secretions). Everything else is triaged to urgent (within 24 h) or nonurgent/observational management with serial radiographs.

The esophagus is the pressure point. Esophageal foreign objects and food impactions must be removed **within 24 hours** — delay reduces the likelihood of successful removal and increases perforation risk. Once past the esophagus, most objects (including sharp ones) pass uneventfully in 4–6 days.

For food bolus impaction — the most common esophageal foreign body in Western adults — the 2011 guideline formally accepts the **gentle push technique** alongside en bloc and piecemeal extraction, a reversal of the prior ASGE position. It also fixes two hard rules: **papain and other proteolytic enzymes must never be used**, and **eosinophilic esophagitis must be looked for** (found in up to 33% of food bolus impactions) with mid- and distal-esophageal biopsies, deferring dilation pending pathology when EoE is suspected.

### Timing of endoscopy (Table 2, reproduced)

**Emergent endoscopy**
- Patients with esophageal obstruction (ie, unable to manage secretions)
- Disk batteries in the esophagus
- Sharp-pointed objects in the esophagus

**Urgent endoscopy**
- Esophageal foreign objects that are not sharp-pointed
- Esophageal food impaction in patients without complete obstruction
- Sharp-pointed objects in the stomach or duodenum
- Objects >6 cm in length at or above the proximal duodenum
- Magnets within endoscopic reach

**Nonurgent endoscopy**
- Coins in the esophagus may be observed for 12–24 hours before endoscopic removal in an asymptomatic patient
- Objects in the stomach with diameter >2.5 cm
- Disk batteries and cylindrical batteries that are in the stomach of patients without signs of GI injury may be observed for as long as 48 hours. Batteries remaining in the stomach longer than 48 hours should be removed.

*The guideline attaches no hour value to "emergent." "Urgent" is anchored in the text: esophageal foreign objects and food impactions should be removed within 24 hours.*

### Epidemiology and risk

- ≥80% of foreign objects pass without intervention (pre-endoscopic series).
- Intentional ingestion: endoscopic intervention rate 63%–76%; surgical intervention 12%–16%.
- Mortality extremely low — no deaths in 852 adults; 1 death in 2206 children across compiled series.
- Majority of ingestions are pediatric; peak incidence age 6 months–6 years.
- Adult true (nonfood) foreign body ingestion: psychiatric disorders, developmental delay, alcohol intoxication, incarcerated individuals seeking secondary gain. Multiple objects and repeat episodes are common.
- Edentulous adults at greater risk — obstructing food bolus or their dental prosthesis.
- Food bolus impaction patients often have underlying esophageal pathology directly causing the impaction.
- Impaction, perforation, or obstruction occurs at GI angulations or narrowing → prior GI tract surgery or congenital gut malformation increases risk.
- Once through the esophagus, most foreign bodies, including sharp objects, pass uneventfully.
- Increased perforation risk: sharp and pointed objects, animal or fish bones, bread bag clips, magnets, medication blister packs.

### Diagnosis

- Area of discomfort often does **not** correlate with the site of impaction; symptoms frequently occur well after ingestion.
- Presentations in young children, mentally impaired adults, psychiatric illness: choking, refusal to eat, vomiting, drooling, wheezing, blood-stained saliva, respiratory distress.
- Oropharyngeal or proximal esophageal perforation: neck swelling, erythema, tenderness, crepitus.
- Peritonitis or small-bowel obstruction require surgery — endoscopy should not delay surgical consultation.
- Biplane radiographs identify most true foreign objects, steak bones, and free mediastinal or peritoneal air; confirm location, size, shape, and number; help exclude aspirated objects.
- **Not readily seen radiographically:** fish or chicken bones, wood, plastic, glass, thin metal objects.
- Contrast examination generally should not be performed — aspiration risk, and contrast coating of the object and mucosa compromises subsequent endoscopy.
- CT may be useful but may not detect radiolucent objects; sensitivity improved with 3-dimensional reconstruction.
- Metal detectors localize most swallowed metal objects; especially helpful in pediatric patients.
- Persistent esophageal symptoms should be evaluated by endoscopy **even with a negative radiographic evaluation**.
- Suspected nonbony food bolus impaction without complications (no perforation, no respiratory distress) → endoscopy may proceed **without** radiographs.

### Airway and sedation

- Initial management includes assessment of ventilatory status and airway.
- Unable to manage secretions → high aspiration risk, requires urgent management.
- Endotracheal intubation (typically under general anesthesia) for: proximal esophageal foreign bodies needing airway protection, objects difficult to remove, multiple objects, and when rigid esophagoscopy is needed.
- Pediatric endoscopy often uses general anesthesia + endotracheal intubation — smaller, more compliant airways carry higher risk of airway obstruction during endoscopy.
- Most adult cases can be managed with conscious sedation.

### Equipment

- **Flexible endoscopy is first-line.** Retrospective comparison: 0 perforations in 76 flexible cases vs 2 of 63 (3.2%, P < .002) with rigid esophagoscopy.
- **Rigid esophagoscopy** may help for proximal foreign bodies impacted at the upper esophageal sphincter or hypopharyngeal region; allows airway protection without an overtube.
- Standard or therapeutic endoscopes preferred; small-caliber transnasal approach reported successful.
- **Retrieval devices:** rat-tooth and alligator forceps, polypectomy snares, polyp graspers, Dormia baskets, retrieval nets, magnetic probes, friction-fit adaptors or banding caps. Practicing grasping a similar object before endoscopy helps select the device and grasp.
- Retrieval net superior to other devices for smooth, round objects in a prospective in vivo study.
- **Overtubes** protect the airway, facilitate endoscope passage during removal of multiple objects or piecemeal clearance of a food impaction, and protect esophageal mucosa from laceration during sharp-object retrieval. Longer overtubes crossing the gastroesophageal junction should be used when available for sharp/pointed objects distal to the esophagus; a >45 cm overtube is used for long objects.
- Overtube use is less common in pediatric patients (esophageal injury risk on insertion); newer softer tubes may mitigate this in older children.
- **In the absence of an overtube, a foreign body protector hood is recommended** to protect the esophagus during removal of sharp or pointed objects.

### Food bolus impaction

- Most common esophageal foreign body in adults in the Western world = impacted meat or other food.
- Endoscopic options: extraction (en bloc with polypectomy snare, retrieval net, friction-fit adaptor or banding cap; or piecemeal) and advancement of the bolus into the stomach.
- Historically ASGE and others advocated against pushing the bolus without first passing the endoscope around it to examine the esophagus distal to the obstruction. Two large series of the push technique reported **no perforations in 375 patients** — gentle pressure applied to the center of the bolus; if advancement fails, reduce bolus size piecemeal then reapply gentle pressure. Perforation remains a risk if excessive force is applied.
- In most circumstances it is considered safe to **dilate after food bolus extraction when an esophageal stricture is present**, to reduce recurrence.
- Caution after prolonged impaction or if eosinophilic esophagitis is suspected.
- **Eosinophilic esophagitis reported in as many as 33% of patients with food bolus impaction.** If suspected → obtain biopsies of the **mid and distal esophagus**, and dilation may be deferred pending pathology results.
- **A proteolytic enzyme, like papain, should never be used** — hypernatremia, mucosal erosion, and esophageal perforation can result.
- **Glucagon 1.0 mg IV** advocated to relax the distal esophagus and allow spontaneous passage while endoscopic therapy is coordinated. Effectiveness questioned; a single small randomized study showed no significant improvement over placebo. Relatively safe and remains an acceptable option, but **its use should not delay definitive endoscopic removal**.

### Short, blunt objects (including coins)

- Removed with foreign body forceps (rat-tooth or alligator), snare, or retrieval net; smooth round objects best secured with a retrieval net or basket.
- With adequate visualization, objects not easily grasped in the esophagus may be advanced into the stomach where retrieval may be facilitated.
- Nonendoscopic techniques (fluoroscopically guided forceps, Foley catheter balloons, magnet-tipped nasogastric tubes) provide no airway protection, no direct visualization of the esophagus for underlying pathology or mucosal injury, and no control of the object → **endoscopic approaches are recommended**.
- **Objects wider than 2.5 cm** should be removed endoscopically — less likely to pass the pylorus (limited supporting data).
- **Objects that fail to pass beyond the stomach by 3 to 4 weeks should be removed endoscopically.**
- Clinical signs of peritonitis → immediate surgical evaluation.
- Surgical removal also considered for objects **distal to the duodenum, in the same location longer than 1 week**, that cannot be reached endoscopically.
- Coins: most commonly ingested by young children. Lodged in the esophagus → may be observed **12 to 24 hours** if asymptomatic. Marked symptoms (drooling, chest pain, stridor) → emergent removal. Distal esophageal coins pass spontaneously more often than proximal — randomized prospective trial: **56% distal vs 27% proximal**. Zinc toxicity reported with massive ingestion of pennies minted after 1982.

### Long objects

- **Objects longer than 6 cm** (toothbrushes, eating utensils) are likely to have difficulty passing the duodenum and should be removed.
- Supporting data: 112 of 139 objects larger than 6 cm remained proximal to the pylorus at endoscopy; 64% of those procedures occurred >48 hours after presentation.
- Technique: >45 cm overtube extending beyond the gastroesophageal junction; grasp with snare or basket, maneuver into the overtube, and withdraw foreign body + overtube + endoscope **in 1 motion** to avoid losing grasp of the object inside the overtube.

### Sharp-pointed objects

- Examples: chicken and fish bones, straightened paperclips, toothpicks, needles, bread bag clips, dental bridgework.
- Many are **not radiographically visible** — endoscopy should still follow a negative radiologic examination.
- **Sharp-pointed objects lodged in the esophagus are a medical emergency.**
- Direct laryngoscopy is an option at or above the cricopharyngeus; rigid or flexible endoscopy when laryngoscopy is unsuccessful or for objects below that level.
- Majority in the stomach pass without incident, but the **risk of complication from a sharp-pointed object is as high as 35%** → retrieve endoscopically from the stomach or proximal duodenum if it can be accomplished safely.
- Otherwise follow with **daily radiographs**; consider surgery for objects that **fail to progress after 3 days**.
- Instruct patients to immediately report abdominal pain, vomiting, persistent temperature elevations, hematemesis, or melena.
- Minimize mucosal injury: orient the object with its **point trailing** during extraction, use an overtube, or fit the endoscope with a protector hood.

### Disk (button) batteries and cylindrical batteries

- Children younger than 5 years are most likely to ingest a button battery; most come from hearing aids, watches, games, toys, and calculators.
- **Liquefaction necrosis and perforation can occur rapidly** when a disk battery is lodged in the esophagus → severe and potentially fatal complications. After radiographic documentation, **emergent removal**.
- Stone retrieval basket or retrieval net most often successful. Alternative: through-the-scope balloon passed through the working channel distal to the battery, inflated, and withdrawn to engage it — balloon, battery, and endoscope removed as a unit. **Use of an overtube or endotracheal tube is essential to protect the airway during this procedure.**
- If the battery cannot be retrieved from the esophagus, **push it into the stomach**, where it can often be retrieved with a basket or net.
- Once in the stomach, most disk batteries pass without complications. Batteries beyond the esophagus need not be retrieved **unless there are signs of GI tract injury**.
- **A large-diameter battery (>20 mm in diameter) remaining in the stomach longer than 48 hours** (repeat radiograph) should be removed.
- Once past the duodenum, **85% pass out of the body within 72 hours**; a radiograph every 3 to 4 days is adequate to assess progress.
- **Emetics are not beneficial** and have led to retrograde migration of batteries from the stomach into the esophagus. Cathartics and acid suppression have no proven role, although GI lavage may expedite passage.
- **Cylindrical batteries:** less common, limited data. Single retrospective analysis — no major life-threatening symptoms; only 18% had minor or moderate symptoms. **Batteries remaining in the stomach longer than 48 hours should be retrieved.** Signs of GI injury are an indication for removal; history should determine whether the battery had an **encasement defect** before ingestion.

### Magnets

- Ingestion of magnets can cause severe GI injury and death.
- Mechanism: attractive force between two magnets, or between a magnet and an ingested metal object, traps a portion of bowel wall between them → pressure necrosis of the bowel wall with fistula formation, perforation, obstruction, volvulus, or peritonitis.
- Some advocate removal, when possible, of **all magnets even if only 1 magnet is evident on radiograph or the history suggests only 1 was ingested** — additional undetected magnets, or metal pieces ingested with a magnet, can cause injury.

### Narcotic packets (body packing)

- Internal concealment of illegal drugs wrapped in plastic or contained in balloons or latex condoms; seen in regions of high drug trafficking; reported in both children and adults.
- Packets can usually be seen radiographically; CT may help, although false-negative scans have been reported.
- **Rupture and leakage of the contents can be fatal, so endoscopic removal should not be attempted.**
- Surgery is indicated when packets fail to progress or if signs of intestinal obstruction are present.
- If packet rupture is suspected → surgery **and** urgent medical consultation for drug toxicity.

### Small-bowel foreign objects

- Single- and double-balloon enteroscopy can access the small intestine and may have a role; case reports describe retrieval of retained video capsules and of retained objects with the potential to cause obstruction or perforation.
- Accessories (hoods, baskets, forceps) have been designed for enteroscopes; data remain limited.
- Decisions should consider patient stability, availability of accessories, potential length of the procedure, and whether an antegrade or retrograde approach is preferred.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Push technique — reversal of prior ASGE position.** Earlier ASGE guidance (1995/2002) and many authorities advocated against pushing a food bolus into the stomach without first passing the endoscope around it. This 2011 update lists the gentle push technique as an acceptable method (⊕⊕⊕◯) on the strength of 2 series with no perforations in 375 patients. Readers carrying the older rule should note the change.
- **Glucagon.** Advocated in older radiologic literature; a single small randomized trial showed no significant benefit over placebo. The guideline keeps it as "an acceptable option" because it is relatively safe — a weak, contested recommendation, not a standard of care.
- **>2.5 cm diameter threshold** rests on expert opinion (⊕◯◯◯) — "limited data exist to support this recommendation" in the guideline's own words.
- **"Emergent" is not given an hour value.** The guideline defines emergent by indication (complete obstruction / secretions, esophageal disk battery, esophageal sharp-pointed object), not by a clock. Only the 24-hour ceiling for esophageal objects and food impactions is numeric. Do not supply an hour cutoff from outside sources.
- **Age of the guideline (2011).** Button battery injury thresholds and pediatric magnet management have been active areas since publication; no newer tier-1 source on ingested foreign bodies is currently in `raw/`. Any update should be ingested before revising the battery/magnet sections.
- **No clinical algorithm figure** in the source — the document's decision content is carried entirely by Table 1 (GRADE legend) and Table 2 (timing), both recreated above as native tables. No figure capture was needed or possible.
