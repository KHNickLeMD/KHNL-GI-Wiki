## Summary

The US Multi-Society Task Force on Colorectal Cancer (USMSTF) — representing the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG), the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA), and the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) — issues 11 consensus recommendations on the identification, diagnosis, and management of serrated polyposis syndrome (SPS), the most common gastrointestinal polyposis syndrome and more common than all other gastrointestinal polyposis syndromes combined.

The document's central message is that SPS is common, underrecognized, and diagnosable only if endoscopists systematically record the size, number, and location of serrated polyps. Prevalence in screening colonoscopy cohorts is approximately 1 in 250, so a busy endoscopist could encounter and diagnose SPS several times per year. Diagnosis rests on the 2019 World Health Organization (WHO) clinical criteria, which count serrated polyps cumulatively across the patient's lifetime and across multiple colonoscopies.

Management is endoscopic, not surgical, in patients without concurrent colorectal cancer (CRC): a **clearance phase** of colonoscopies at 3–6-month intervals until all target lesions are resected, followed by a **surveillance phase** at 1–2 years, with 3-year intervals reserved for patients whose polyp burden has been greatly reduced. Resection of sessile serrated lesions (SSLs) and hyperplastic polyps is by cold snare, with or without submucosal injection for larger lesions, except where dysplasia is endoscopically evident. Surgery is reserved for cancer or for a polyp burden that cannot be controlled endoscopically — and expert referral for advanced endoscopic resection is advised before surgical referral.

SPS has no established genetic basis. Germline testing is indicated only when concurrent non-serrated polyps or family history independently meet criteria for a known hereditary syndrome. First-degree relatives (FDRs) are at heightened CRC risk and should undergo colonoscopic screening, although the supporting evidence is characterized as mixed.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Methodology and grading

- Evidence quality rated with the **Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine 2011 Levels of Evidence** (Level 1 strongest to Level 5 weakest).
- Strength of recommendation by **consensus**, using the terms **strong** and **weak**. Strong = most patients should be managed according to the recommendation. Weak = more latitude in application; phrased *"We suggest…"* as opposed to *"We recommend…"*. Weak recommendations are often made when the evidence is less robust, when clinical impact is smaller, or where patient perspective takes on greater importance.
- The document is explicitly a **consensus statement**, not a guideline — the USMSTF distinguishes its documents from those developed separately by the individual societies.
- Recommendation numbering is the document's own (Table 2).

### Diagnostic criteria

- **Recommendation 1** — We recommend the **2019 WHO criteria** be utilized for diagnosis of SPS. *(Strong recommendation, Oxford Level 5)*
  - Rated strong despite Level 5 evidence because the WHO criteria are widely accepted in clinical practice and research; using other criteria would create misunderstanding in clinical reports and new publications.

**2019 WHO criteria** (Table 3; SP = serrated polyp = hyperplastic polyp, sessile serrated lesion, or traditional serrated adenoma):

| Type | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Type 1** | **≥5 SPs proximal to the rectum**, all **≥5 mm** and **≥2 being ≥10 mm** |
| **Type 2** | **>20 SPs throughout the colon** with **≥5 being proximal to the rectum** |

- The required number of serrated lesions **can be reached by counting the total number of serrated lesions resected over all colonoscopies in the individual's lifetime** — Type 1, Type 2, or both types.
- WHO last modified the criteria in **2019**. The previously used criterion — *any serrated polyp proximal to the sigmoid colon in a first-degree relative of an SPS patient* — was **dropped**, contributed to by the lack of evidence for a genetic basis for SPS.
- The most recent criteria place greater emphasis on lesions in the **sigmoid colon** (counting proximal to the **rectum**), whereas previous WHO criteria placed emphasis on lesions proximal to the **sigmoid**. This followed recognition that in SPS approximately **half of CRCs occur in the rectosigmoid**.
- The criteria imply a sharp cutoff, but patients with multiple serrated polyps **approaching but not fully reaching** the WHO criteria are **also at increased risk of CRC** during surveillance.

### Genetics

- **Recommendation 2** — We recommend that germline genetic testing in patients with a personal diagnosis of SPS be performed **only when** there are concurrent non-serrated polyps that constitute an established indication for genetic testing (e.g., personal lifetime history of **>10 conventional adenomas**, **>2 hamartomatous polyps**) **or** a family history suggesting a hereditary gastrointestinal cancer syndrome. *(Strong recommendation, Oxford Level 3)*
- **No consistent genetic basis** has been identified for SPS; any definition of SPS is clinical and carries an arbitrary element.
  - A germline *RNF43* (Ring Finger Protein 43) variant was associated with one severely affected SPS kindred, but systematic testing revealed **no** *RNF43* germline pathogenic variants in SPS patients.
  - Germline testing for polyposis-related variants has generally been negative. One multigene panel study found variants in **9.6% of 73 patients**, with 6 different genes affected in the 7 individuals with positive tests.
  - In a recent analysis of **258 SPS patients** who had genetic testing, only **4** had germline variants — **3 in Lynch syndrome genes** and **1** *RNF43* variant.
  - Older age at diagnosis and absence of family history of CRC in most SPS patients favor SPS **not** being a hereditary syndrome.

### Prevalence and clinical features

- **SPS is the most common gastrointestinal polyposis syndrome** and is more common than all other gastrointestinal polyposis syndromes combined. Prevalence in screening colonoscopy cohorts is approximately **1 in 250**.
- European studies: SPS prevalence as high as **0.9% (1 case per 111 colonoscopies)** in patients with positive fecal occult blood tests, and **0.42% (1 per 238 colonoscopies)** in patients with the primary indication of screening.
- **Mean age at presentation is around 60 years**; approximately equal sex distribution, although some series are predominantly women.
- **Strong association with cigarette smoking**; smoking cessation is advisable, but there is currently no evidence that cessation alters the clinical course of SPS, and many SPS patients are nonsmokers.
- Within SPS, the overwhelming majority of serrated polyps are **hyperplastic polyps and SSLs rather than traditional serrated adenomas (TSAs)**; TSAs count toward the diagnosis but most patients reach the diagnosis via the number of SSLs and hyperplastic polyps. Hyperplastic polyps **≥10 mm** may be managed as SSLs.
- Both hyperplastic polyps and SSLs count toward an SPS diagnosis, in part because pathologic differentiation of SSL from hyperplastic polyp is subject to interobserver variation.
- **Type 1 and combined Type 1 + Type 2 SPS carry higher CRC risk than Type 2 alone.** Type 2 SPS has a **stronger association with smoking** than Type 1.
- The serrated pathway accounts for up to **30% of sporadic CRC**; it is considered most important in the proximal colon and increases progressively in importance from the rectum to the right colon.
- Synchronous conventional adenomas are relatively common in SPS, and **synchronous advanced conventional adenomas are a marker of increased CRC risk**.
- **Appendix:** high prevalence of serrated lesions (generally not visible endoscopically) in SPS patients undergoing right hemicolectomy, but the incidence of appendiceal cancer in SPS is low and **no consensus has emerged that prophylactic appendectomy is warranted**.

### CRC risk

- Meta-analysis of 36 studies including **2,788 patients**: CRC risk at the time of SPS diagnosis **14.7%**; risk of CRC **before** diagnosis **7.7%**. Most CRC diagnoses are made **at or before the index exam**.
- CRC incidence **during surveillance** is also elevated: meta-analysis CRC rate **2.8%** during surveillance, mean interval 28.9 months (range 4.3–80.4 months). CRC incidence rate has been **lower in recent studies and was zero in several** (Table 4: nine published studies with >60 patients, 5-year CRC risk 0%–7%).
- SSLs with dysplasia or concurrent advanced conventional adenomas are associated with higher CRC risk.

### Improving diagnosis

- **Recommendation 3** — When multiple serrated polyps are detected during colonoscopy, we recommend the **number, size, and location** of serrated polyps be described in the procedure report. This practice should be continued **at least until an SPS diagnosis is established**. *(Strong recommendation, Oxford Level 4)*
- **Recommendation 4** — We recommend colonoscopy in SPS be performed by endoscopists with **documented adequate detection of SSLs (SSL detection rate ≥6%)** during routine colonoscopy. *(Strong recommendation, Oxford Level 5)*
  - Current US recommendations are for all colonoscopists to measure their SSL detection rate, with recommended **minimum acceptable thresholds of 6%–7%** and **aspirational levels approaching 20%**.
- **Recommendation 5** — We recommend colonoscopy in SPS be performed with **high-definition white light colonoscopy, with or without virtual or dye spray chromoendoscopy**. *(Strong recommendation, Oxford Level 3)*
  - Narrow band imaging enhances detection of **sporadic** serrated polyps, particularly with excellent bowel preparation, but has produced **mixed results in SPS patients**.
  - In one trial of tandem colonoscopies in SPS patients, the **indigo carmine plus acetic acid** arm detected more additional SSLs than the indigo carmine or white light groups (**63.4% vs 39.1% vs 21.6%; P < 0.001**); nearly all additional serrated polyps were **<10 mm**.
  - Meta-analyses indicate **computer-aided detection has less benefit for serrated polyps than for adenomas**, and some programs perform **poorly in detection of large SSLs**.
- SPS is significantly underdiagnosed. A planned 1-year surveillance colonoscopy in fecal immunochemical test–positive patients with **1 or more serrated lesions ≥5 mm proximal to the sigmoid colon**, aimed at diagnosing SPS, **tripled the rate of SPS diagnosis**.
- Counting serrated polyps when numerous polyps are seen generally requires proficiency in **optical diagnosis** to differentiate conventional adenomas from serrated polyps. Placing every polyp resected in separate pathology containers would be costly and inefficient.
- **After** an SPS diagnosis is established, tracking numbers, sizes, and locations of serrated polyps vs conventional adenomas over multiple colonoscopies is no longer necessary; procedure reports should instead specify **total numbers of polyps resected and numbers of polyps ≥10 mm, 6–9 mm, and ≤5 mm**, to show whether polyp burden is increasing or decreasing.

### Management — surgery vs colonoscopy

- **Recommendation 6** — In patients **without concurrent CRC**, we recommend **endoscopic management as the preferred strategy over surgery** for primary management of SPS. *(Strong recommendation, Oxford Level 3)*
- **Recommendation 7** — We suggest individuals with SPS whose polyp burden is deemed **endoscopically unmanageable** be **referred to an expert in advanced endoscopic resection prior to surgical resection**. *(Weak recommendation, Oxford Level 5)*
- **Recommendation 8** — We recommend that **SSLs and hyperplastic polyps** in SPS patients be **resected by cold snare, with or without submucosal injection for larger lesions**, except when **dysplastic areas are endoscopically evident**. *(Strong recommendation, Oxford level 3)*
- Supporting detail:
  - Surgery is expected when cancer and SPS are diagnosed in the same colonoscopy. Surgical options include segmental colon resection or extended colectomy including subtotal colectomy.
  - Morbidity, mortality, and cost of surgery for benign colorectal polyps are significant and substantially greater than for endoscopic resection: in one nationwide study, postoperative **morbidity 13%**, **mortality 0.3%–0.4%**.
  - The overwhelming majority of SPS patients can be managed by colonoscopy with polypectomy — averaging **10–16 polyp resections per colonoscopy in the clearing phase** and **5–7 per colonoscopy in the surveillance phase**. In one study **5% of patients required >100 total lesion resections** during the clearing phase.
  - **Cold snare resection is effective for SSLs and hyperplastic polyps regardless of size.** In observational studies, lesions **≥10 mm** can be effectively resected piecemeal by cold snare with or without submucosal injection. No randomized trial has compared submucosal injection vs no injection for endoscopic resection of SSLs.
  - Cold resection has **fewer complications** than resection with electrocautery, and is efficient and cost-effective compared with endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) with electrocautery, because it **does not require clip closure** after resection.
  - For **lesions with overt dysplasia by endoscopic inspection**, resection with electrocautery is recommended by the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy, although supporting evidence is lacking. **Bulky SSLs** occur occasionally and can be challenging to remove by cold piecemeal resection, and may therefore warrant electrocautery resection.
  - **Minimum resection targets:** because up to half of cancers in SPS arise in the rectosigmoid, **all lesions ≥5 mm in the rectosigmoid** should be resected; in addition, **all conventional adenomas throughout the colon and all serrated lesions proximal to the sigmoid** should be resected. Some programs remove all serrated lesions **≥5 mm**, others **≥3 mm**, and some remove nearly all lesions as time permits.

### Surveillance intervals

- **Recommendation 9** — We recommend colonoscopy be performed in a **clearance phase at an interval of 3–6 months** followed by a **surveillance phase of 1–2 years**. *(Strong recommendation, Oxford Level 3)*
- **Recommendation 10** — We suggest patients with **greatly reduced polyp burdens** at surveillance **can be considered for 3-year intervals**. *(Weak recommendation, Oxford Level 5)*
- **Clearing phase** usually requires **≥2 colonoscopies**. Its endpoint is a colonoscopy in which **all visualized lesions ≥5 mm are resected**. Patients whose colons cannot be fully cleared during any single colonoscopy are in the clearing phase and should have colonoscopy repeated at **3–6-month intervals** until all lesions at or above the target size are removed.
- **Target lesions** for the clearing phase are usually defined as: **all conventional adenomas**, **all lesions proximal to the sigmoid**, and **all rectosigmoid serrated polyps either ≥5 mm or ≥3 mm** (Figure 2 footnote).
- Surveillance-interval assignment after the colon is cleared (Figure 2 — framework, "not meant to imply strict evidence-based recommendations"):

| Most recent colonoscopy findings | Surveillance interval |
|---|---|
| Any lesion **≥20 mm**; **≥3 lesions 10–19 mm**; any **SSL with dysplasia**; any **TSA**; or any **advanced conventional adenoma** | **≤1 year** |
| **≤2 lesions 10–19 mm**, good control of lesions ≥5 mm or ≥3 mm | **2 years** |
| **Only small lesions**; polyp burden greatly reduced | **Consider 3 years** |

- Figure 2 does not consider in detail the impact of concurrent conventional adenomas: because advanced conventional adenomas are a risk factor for CRC in SPS, **concurrent advanced conventional adenomas generally warrant surveillance at ≤1 year**. Multiple non-advanced conventional adenomas could reasonably be considered to influence interval selection.
- Recommendations for intervals assume colonoscopy is performed **to the cecum with adequate bowel preparation** and by endoscopists who measure and demonstrate **high detection skill**.
- Previous recommendations were often for **annual** colonoscopy in SPS; observational studies found selected patients can undergo colonoscopy at **2-year intervals without an increased CRC rate** and without increased incidence of advanced neoplasia. 1–2-year intervals may ultimately prove unnecessary for lower-burden patients scoped by highly effective detectors, but current USMSTF recommendations reflect the consistent use of 1-year and 2-year intervals in referral centers.
- There is currently **no substantial published evidence supporting interval extension beyond 3 years**.

### Relatives

- **Recommendation 11** — We suggest **first-degree relatives** of individuals with SPS undergo colonoscopy at **age 40**, or **10 years before the youngest age at CRC diagnosis**, or **5 years before the youngest age at SPS diagnosis in the family** — **whichever is earlier**. *(Weak recommendation, Oxford Level 4)*
- Supporting data:
  - Older study of 347 patients with an FDR with hyperplastic polyposis: **5-times elevated CRC risk (relative risk 5.4; 95% CI 3.7–7.8)** vs a population-based cancer registry.
  - Among **1,639 FDRs and second-degree relatives of 100 patients** with serrated polyposis: increased CRC risk for FDRs (**standardized incidence ratio 5.16; 95% CI 3.70–7.30; P < 0.001**) and second-degree relatives (**SIR 1.38; 95% CI 1.01–1.91; P = 0.04**).
  - A recent study observed a **5-fold increased CRC risk for FDRs of 37 patients with SPS** compared with FDRs of age-matched normal colonoscopy controls.
  - Two small studies of screening colonoscopy in FDRs demonstrated **mixed results** regarding increased yield of neoplasia and increased prevalence of SPS. **The evidence to support increased screening in FDRs of SPS patients is currently mixed.**

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Diagnostic criteria — supersedes what the wiki printed.** The page previously carried the **WHO 2010 (4th-edition)** criteria from [[acg-2015-hereditary-gi-cancer]] (≥5 serrated polyps proximal to the **sigmoid**, ≥2 >10 mm; or any serrated polyp proximal to the sigmoid in an FDR of an SPS patient; or >20 serrated polyps throughout the colon) alongside the [[usmstf-2020-followup-colonoscopy]] table-footnote thresholds. USMSTF 2026 resolves both: the landmark is the **rectum**, the Type 1 limb carries a **≥5 mm floor on every counted polyp**, and the **FDR-based criterion was dropped by WHO in 2019**. Newer, same-tier, same task force → USMSTF 2026 governs.
- **Surveillance intervals.** [[acg-2015-hereditary-gi-cancer]] Rec 19 recommends colonoscopy every **1–3 years** with removal of all polyps >5 mm. USMSTF 2026 separates management into a **3–6-month clearance phase** and a **1–2-year surveillance phase**, with 3 years only for greatly reduced burden. The newer consensus governs; the ACG interval is a single blended figure that does not distinguish the two phases.
- **Mean age at diagnosis.** USMSTF 2026 gives mean age at presentation **around 60 years**; [[acg-2015-hereditary-gi-cancer]] cites ~48 years. Newer source governs.
- **Resection technique.** No prior wiki source specified a resection modality for serrated lesions in SPS. USMSTF 2026 prefers **cold snare regardless of lesion size**, departing from the EMR-with-electrocautery default for large lesions; it notes the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy recommends electrocautery for endoscopically overt dysplasia and that **supporting evidence for that position is lacking**.
- **FDR screening.** [[acg-2015-hereditary-gi-cancer]] Rec 21 called family screening "unclear pending further data" and cited two non-endorsed schemes. USMSTF 2026 makes an actual (weak) recommendation with explicit ages, while itself characterizing the underlying evidence as mixed.
- **Prophylactic appendectomy** — serrated lesions are highly prevalent in the appendix in SPS and generally not endoscopically visible, but appendiceal cancer incidence is low and no consensus exists. Open question.
- **Whether smoking cessation alters the course of SPS or CRC risk in SPS remains uncertain.**
