## Summary

Joint ACG/CAG guideline on dyspepsia management, built on 14 numbered recommendations (Table 1 of the source). Searches ran from database inception to December 2015; consensus by modified Delphi with unanimous voting on every statement, including strength and quality.

Two practice-changing points. The age threshold for upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy in uninvestigated dyspepsia is raised to **60 years or over** (previous ACG guidance used 55); and **alarm features alone do not automatically precipitate endoscopy under 60**, because their positive predictive value (PPV) against a 0.3% pre-test probability of upper GI cancer is very low. For patients under 60 the initial strategy is non-invasive *Helicobacter pylori* testing with treatment if positive ([[helicobacter-pylori-infection|H. pylori]]), then empirical proton pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy for those who are negative or still symptomatic.

Patients whose endoscopy shows no pathology are defined as having functional dyspepsia (FD). FD therapy is sequential: eradication if infected, then PPI, then tricyclic antidepressant (TCA), then prokinetic, then psychological therapy. **Order differs between the two populations** — in FD the guideline explicitly puts TCA before prokinetic (Statement 9 commentary), whereas in uninvestigated dyspepsia (Statements 5 and 6) it states no preference between them.

The guideline covers only initial investigation and the drug classes above. It does **not** address management of organic pathology found at endoscopy (esophagitis, peptic ulcer disease — separate ACG guidelines), and it does **not** specify which *H. pylori* test or which eradication regimen to use.

## Scope and Definitions

- **Dyspepsia (guideline's working definition):** predominant epigastric pain lasting **at least 1 month**. May be associated with any other upper GI symptom (epigastric fullness, nausea, vomiting, heartburn) **provided epigastric pain is the patient's primary concern**. Chosen over Rome IV because Rome definitions, while useful for trials, are less relevant in practice given symptom overlap.
- **Functional dyspepsia:** dyspepsia in whom endoscopy (and other tests where relevant) has ruled out organic pathology explaining the symptoms.
- **Explicitly out of scope** (do not apply this guideline):
  - Alarm features **without** epigastric pain (e.g., progressive dysphagia and/or weight loss alone) — these patients do not meet the dyspepsia definition.
  - Epigastric pain suggesting a pancreatic or biliary source (e.g., pain radiating to the back) → appropriate imaging such as ultrasound or computed tomography.
  - Alarm features needing non-endoscopic tests (e.g., jaundice).
- **International caveat:** the viewpoint is US/Canadian; local disease incidence and drug availability may change the approach elsewhere.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Statement 1 — endoscopy at 60 or over

- Previous ACG guidance set routine endoscopy at **55 and over**; raised to **over 60** because endoscopy was only borderline cost-effective at 55 in economic analyses and age-specific gastric cancer incidence has fallen further in the US and Canada over the intervening 10 years.
- **Lower the threshold** for: childhood spent in a high-risk gastric cancer region (e.g., South East Asia, some South American countries), positive family history of upper GI malignancy.
- **In borderline cases, sex may be considered** — age-adjusted upper GI cancer risk is about **twice as high in men** as in women.
- Grade is very low quality because the data are national database/case-series and economic modelling, which are indirect and tend to overestimate endoscopy's benefit; clinicians may reasonably treat a minority of over-60s empirically if malignancy risk looks low.

### Statement 2 — alarm features do not mandate endoscopy under 60

- Systematic review of **7 studies, >46,000 dyspepsia patients**: alarm features had limited utility for upper GI malignancy and for any organic pathology.
- Individual alarm features (weight loss, anemia, dysphagia) — sensitivity and specificity each **~66%**, positive likelihood ratio (LR) **2.74 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.47–5.24)**. An alarm feature therefore confers only a **2–3-fold** relative increase; on a baseline risk that is very low under 60, **absolute risk remains well under 1%**.
- **Exceptions that still warrant endoscopy under 60** (clinical judgment, case-by-case): a particularly prominent feature — **weight loss >20 lb** or **rapidly progressive dysphagia** — or a **combination** of features (combinations have not been formally evaluated). Family history of upper GI malignancy also factors in.
- **Risk rises with age within the under-60 group** — the threshold to refer would be lower in a 58-year-old than a 28-year-old with dyspepsia and alarm features.
- **Pancreas:** routine pancreatic imaging is not recommended under 60 — in a systematic review of >57,000 dyspepsia patients, **<0.01%** had pancreatic cancer. Over 60 with new-onset dyspepsia, it is sensible to combine endoscopy with an imaging modality that evaluates the pancreas, such as abdominal ultrasound.

### Statement 3 — test-and-treat under 60

- **6 trials, 2,399 patients**, test-and-treat vs prompt endoscopy: no difference in global dyspepsia symptoms at ~1 year (74% vs 77% still symptomatic; relative risk (RR) of remaining dyspeptic **0.94, 95% CI 0.84–1.04**). Only **25%** of the test-and-treat arm had an endoscopy over 1 year vs nearly all of the prompt-endoscopy arm — the driver of a significant cost saving (**mean saving $402, 95% CI $329–$475**).
- *H. pylori* eradication vs placebo in infected dyspepsia patients (**2 trials, 563 patients**): RR remaining dyspeptic **0.81 (95% CI 0.70–0.94)**, **number needed to treat (NNT) 7 (95% CI 5–14)**.
- Test-and-treat vs empirical PPI (**4 trials, 1,608 patients**): 73% vs 78% still dyspeptic at 1 year, RR **0.89 (95% CI 0.77–1.04)** — not significant, but trend and costs both favored test-and-treat, so it is the preferred initial strategy with acid suppression reserved for the negative/non-responding.
- **Timing:** allow **at least 4 weeks** before reassessing symptomatic response to eradication therapy.
- **The old prevalence rule is dropped.** A previous guideline suggested PPI first when local *H. pylori* prevalence is **<15%**. This guideline rejects that: local prevalence is usually unknown, and even at very low infection rates test-and-treat is likely the most cost-effective first-line strategy. Background infection rates in the trials were 20–30%.
- Grade is high because effects are robust with narrow CIs, despite unblinded trials.

### Statement 4 — empirical PPI under 60

- **6 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), 2,709 patients**, PPI vs placebo or antacid: **50%** of the PPI group vs **73%** of placebo still symptomatic; RR remaining dyspeptic **0.75 (95% CI 0.64–0.88)**, **NNT 6 (95% CI 4–11)**.
- **Dose:** the trials used **once-daily standard dosing**. Higher doses are unlikely to increase benefit in dyspepsia.
- PPI vs histamine-2 receptor antagonist (H2RA) — **7 RCTs, 2,456 patients**: no significant difference overall (RR **0.93, 95% CI 0.76–1.16**) but with large heterogeneity (I² = 91%); 4 trials favored PPI, 2 showed no difference, 1 favored an H2RA not available in the West. Excluding that trial, PPI was significantly better (RR **0.81, 95% CI 0.72–0.91**). Cost is similar, so the group supported empirical PPI over H2RA.
- Prompt endoscopy vs empirical acid suppression (**5 RCTs, 1,752 patients**): no difference (RR **1.00, 95% CI 0.94–1.05**).

### Statements 5 and 6 — second line in uninvestigated dyspepsia

- **No randomized studies compare prokinetic with placebo in undiagnosed dyspepsia.** Three trials (680 patients, follow-up 4–52 weeks) compared PPI with prokinetic: trend favoring PPI, RR **0.78 (95% CI 0.60–1.02, P = 0.06)** — not significant. All trials high risk of bias → very low quality.
- **No randomized trials of antidepressants in undiagnosed dyspepsia at all.** The TCA estimate is imported indirectly from FD (3 trials, RR **0.74, 95% CI 0.61–0.91**), assuming most dyspepsia patients in North America have FD; indirectness is why the same data are graded **low** here and **moderate** for FD (Statement 9).
- **No selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) effect** was seen on this evidence.
- **Order:** the group **did not state a preference** between prokinetic and TCA in this population; the decision is case-by-case (patients may dislike the perceived stigma of an antidepressant).
- **Prokinetic safety/dosing where given:** use the lowest effective dose, consistent with country-specific safety guidance — **metoclopramide for less than 12 weeks**; **domperidone 30 mg daily or less**. The prokinetics evaluated in the randomized trials (**cisapride, mosapride**) are not available in most countries.

### Statement 7 — eradication in H. pylori-positive FD

- A diagnosis of FD can also be made without endoscopy using clinical symptoms and history. **At a normal endoscopy, take gastric biopsies** to assess for *H. pylori* if prior non-invasive testing has not been done.
- **22 RCTs, 4,896 *H. pylori*-positive FD patients**, eradication vs placebo antibiotics, follow-up 3–12 months: dyspepsia persisted in **1,767/2,604 (67.9%)** vs **1,751/2,292 (76.4%)**; RR dyspepsia remaining **0.91 (95% CI 0.88–0.94)**, no heterogeneity (I² = 0%), **NNT 12.5 (95% CI 10–20)**.
- Strong despite a modest effect because the approach is cost-effective, adverse events are usually mild, and eradication may also reduce future gastric cancer and peptic ulcer risk.
- Caveat the guideline flags: it is possible the benefit comes from treating other infectious agents rather than from eradicating *H. pylori* itself — this does not change the recommendation.

### Statement 8 — PPI in FD

- **15 RCTs (14 papers), 5,853 FD patients**, follow-up 2–8 weeks: **2,724/3,916 (69.6%)** on PPI vs **1,457/1,937 (75.2%)** on control still symptomatic; RR dyspepsia remaining **0.87 (95% CI 0.82–0.94, P < 0.00001)**, **NNT 10 (95% CI 7–20)**. Low-dose and standard-dose arms did not differ.
- **Stop at 8 weeks if no response.** Doubling the PPI dose has no value; discontinue if the patient does not respond after **8 weeks of standard-dose, once-daily** therapy.
- **Do not use symptom subtype to choose therapy.** Subgroup analysis suggests heartburn-predominant patients respond better, but there is **no evidence that epigastric pain syndrome (EPS) responds better than postprandial distress syndrome (PDS)**-type dyspepsia.
- PPI vs H2RA in FD (**2 RCTs, 740 patients**): no significant difference (RR **1.27, 95% CI 0.83–1.94**) — insufficient data to call H2RA non-inferior. PPI vs prokinetic in FD (**4 RCTs, 892 patients**): significant, favoring PPI (RR **0.90, 95% CI 0.81–1.00, P = 0.04**).

### Long-term PPI safety

- Reviewed hypothesized associations: hip fracture, community-acquired pneumonia, *Clostridioides difficile* infection, electrolyte disturbance, dementia. Most likely explanation is **residual confounding**; even if causal, the **number needed to harm is >1,000 in most cases** and benefits outweigh known harms.
- **Stop PPI if it is no longer providing benefit.** Patients should not be on long-term PPI **without attempts to withdraw it every 6–12 months**, consistent with US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance.

### Statement 9 — TCA in FD

- **3 trials, 339 FD patients**, TCA vs placebo: **77/170 (45.3%)** vs **104/169 (61.5%)** still symptomatic; RR **0.74 (95% CI 0.61–0.91)**, **NNT 6 (95% CI 6–18)**.
- **SSRIs do not work** — 2 trials, 388 FD patients, RR **1.01 (95% CI 0.89–1.15)**.
- Conditional because of uncertainty around the effect size, TCA adverse events (constipation, dry mouth, urinary retention, somnolence) and patient reluctance to take an antidepressant.
- **Explicit ordering rule:** "In contrast to Statements 5 and 6 above, it should be noted that we recommend TCA before prokinetic for treatment of FD based on the superior evidence for TCA in this indication."

### Statement 10 — prokinetics in FD

- **26 randomized trials (23 papers), 8,788 FD patients**: RR remaining dyspeptic **0.92 (95% CI 0.88–0.97)**, **NNT 12.5 (95% CI 8–25)**.
- Quality dropped to very low for **publication bias** — funnel plot asymmetry, small positive studies driving the result, larger trials showing little or no effect (Egger test P = 0.004) — plus significant unexplained heterogeneity.
- **None of the eligible prokinetics is available in the US, Canada or Europe**, and there are **no clinical trials of metoclopramide in FD**.
- **Domperidone:** 7 trials, 263 patients (all excluded a priori — barium meal before endoscopy and/or non-standard dyspepsia definitions — but synthesized because domperidone is available in Canada and elsewhere, though not the US): RR remaining symptomatic **0.71 (95% CI 0.53–0.97)**, **NNT 3 (95% CI 2–8)**; quality very low.
- **Harms:** metoclopramide — dystonia, parkinsonism-type movements, tardive dyskinesia. Domperidone — QT prolongation, which could increase serious arrhythmia risk in pre-existing cardiac disease.

### Statement 11 — psychological therapy in FD

- **12 RCTs, 1,563 FD patients**; all reported a statistically significant benefit over control (most commonly usual management). Commonest approaches: **cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** and other forms of psychotherapy.
- Only 4 papers reported a dichotomous dyspepsia outcome (**789 FD patients**): RR **0.53 (95% CI 0.44–0.65)**, **NNT 3 (95% CI 3–4)**.
- Very low quality despite the dramatic effect: all trials high risk of bias (blinding impossible), subjective outcome, heterogeneous interventions. Also may be expensive and requires significant patient time and motivation.

### Statement 12 — no routine complementary and alternative medicine (CAM)

- Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) is used by **~20%** of the general population for GI symptoms, probably more among secondary/tertiary FD patients; a qualitative review identified **26 CAM methods** for FD — too diverse for definitive conclusions.
- **STW 5** (herbal preparation: bitter candy tuft, matricaria flower, peppermint leaves, caraway, licorice root, lemon balm), 315 FD patients randomized to STW 5 or placebo for 8 weeks: Gastrointestinal Symptom Score improved **6.9 ± 4.8** vs **5.9 ± 4.3 (P = 0.04)** — marginal and of unclear clinical meaning.
- Chinese herbal medicine — 13 trials, 1,153 patients: possible signal, very poor methodological quality. Acupuncture (Cochrane) — 7 studies, 542 patients: data too low quality to conclude.
- Framed as insufficient evidence to determine benefit or risk, not as proven absence of effect; may be appropriate for individual patients who understand that.

### Statements 13 and 14 — motility testing in FD

- **Most FD patients have normal gastric emptying (70–80%).** Delayed emptying is found in up to **30%** (scintigraphy or breath tests) but the delay is usually **mild — 10–20% of material remaining at 4 h**.
- A large, multicenter 4-h solid-phase gastric-emptying trial read at one center found **21%** of patients meeting Rome III criteria for FD had delayed emptying.
- **Abnormal gastric accommodation** is present in up to **40%** of FD, but can only be measured accurately with **gastric barostat** or **single-photon emission computed tomography** — neither readily available, and barostat is expensive, invasive and uncomfortable.
- Delays in gastric emptying are **not accurately related to symptoms**, which is why routine testing is not recommended.
- **When to test (Statement 14):** a **4-h solid-phase gastric-emptying scan** should be performed in FD patients with **predominant symptoms of severe nausea and vomiting who fail empiric therapy**. Daily or intractable vomiting suggests [[gastroparesis]] rather than FD.
- FD and gastroparesis overlap significantly and likely represent part of a spectrum of gastric sensorimotor disorders. No RCT data answer how management should change once a marked delay is identified.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Age threshold change:** raises the endoscopy threshold to 60 and over, from 55 and over in previous ACG guidance. Not universally adopted — local cancer epidemiology may justify a lower threshold.
- **Alarm features:** earlier guidelines mandated endoscopy for any alarm feature at any age; this guideline explicitly declines to do so under 60 while keeping named exceptions (weight loss >20 lb, rapidly progressive dysphagia, feature combinations).
- **Prevalence-based first-line choice:** contradicts the earlier guidance that PPI should be first line where *H. pylori* prevalence is <15%.
- **Internal inconsistency on the PPI-in-FD estimate:** the Statement 8 text reports 5,853 patients (2,724/3,916 vs 1,457/1,937) with RR 0.87 (0.82–0.94), while the Table 5 GRADE profile for the same comparison reports 15 trials with 2,332/3,621 vs 1,293/1,777 and RR 0.83 (0.77–0.89). The page asserts the Statement 8 text values, which carry the NNT.
- **TCA evidence is reused across two populations:** the identical 3-trial dataset supports Statement 6 (uninvestigated, graded low for indirectness) and Statement 9 (FD, graded moderate). There are no antidepressant trials in undiagnosed dyspepsia.
- **Prokinetic recommendations are largely untestable in practice:** every eligible prokinetic is unavailable in the US, Canada and Europe, and the pooled effect is downgraded for publication bias.
- **Rome IV → V:** predates Rome V (2026); FD definitions are reasonably stable but gut–brain framing has evolved — see [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]] and [[rome-v-2026-dgbi]].
- **Numbering quirk:** Statements 7, 9 and 13 are worded "We recommend" but graded conditional. This is as published.
