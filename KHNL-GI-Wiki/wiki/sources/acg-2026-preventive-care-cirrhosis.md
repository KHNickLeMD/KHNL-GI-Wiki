## Summary

American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) clinical guideline on **preventive care in cirrhosis** — the practices that keep a patient with cirrhosis out of the hospital rather than the treatment of established decompensation. Developed under the ACG Practice Parameters Committee with two Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methodologists; PICO questions, librarian-assisted searches of PubMed/Embase/Cochrane from March 2025, prioritizing systematic reviews, meta-analyses and randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Output is **18 GRADE-assessed recommendations and 6 key concept statements** across four domains: prevention of cirrhosis-specific complications, infection prevention, lifestyle factors, and non-liver-specific conditions. Strong recommendations are limited to carvedilol or band ligation for high-risk varices, semiannual hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) surveillance, hepatitis A testing and vaccination, influenza, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) immunization, and smoking-cessation counseling. Conditional recommendations cover lactulose for minimal hepatic encephalopathy, adjunctive rifaximin after hospitalization, short-course antibiotic prophylaxis for upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding, postdischarge behavioral therapy for alcohol use disorder (AUD), dietitian-guided nutrition, structured exercise, and bisphosphonates for osteoporosis.

**Most evidence was of low certainty** — only one recommendation (varices, #4) rests on moderate certainty, and two (#8 aspirin, #9 AUD behavioral therapy) on very low. The guideline is explicit that several strong recommendations sit on low-certainty evidence, justified by excellent safety profile and large potential benefit (the vaccines, smoking cessation).

A closing **implementation** section proposes a three-pillar framework — education/dissemination, stakeholder engagement, practice integration (electronic health record alerts, order sets, standing orders, medical assistant roles, multidisciplinary clinics, quality metrics) — and emphasizes that nonphysicians are best positioned to execute vaccination documentation, osteoporosis screening referrals and structured exercise/nutrition counseling.

## Key Findings / Claims

### GRADE recommendations (the guideline's own numbering, Table 2)

**Cirrhosis-specific complications**

1. In patients with cirrhosis and **minimal hepatic encephalopathy (MHE)**, we suggest **lactulose over no treatment**. *(Conditional; low)*
2. In patients who experienced **overt hepatic encephalopathy (HE) while receiving lactulose**, we suggest **adding rifaximin** to prevent the recurrence of HE. *(Conditional; low)*
3. In patients with cirrhosis with **minimal encephalopathy**, we **do not suggest rifaximin as primary prevention** of the development of overt HE. *(Conditional; low)*
4. In patients with cirrhosis and **high-risk esophageal varices**, we recommend **carvedilol or esophageal variceal ligation** for primary prophylaxis against variceal bleeding. *(Strong; moderate)*
5. In patients with cirrhosis experiencing **upper GI bleeding**, we suggest a **short (2–3 day) course of prophylactic antibiotics** compared with a longer course or no antibiotics. *(Conditional; low)*
6. In patients with **compensated cirrhosis and clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH)**, we suggest **carvedilol** to reduce the incidence of **first-episode ascites**. *(Conditional; low)*
7. We recommend **HCC surveillance with liver ultrasound and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) every 6 months** in patients with cirrhosis who are **candidates for HCC therapies**. *(Strong; low)*
8. In patients with cirrhosis, we **do not suggest aspirin to prevent HCC**. *(Conditional; very low)*
9. In patients **hospitalized with cirrhosis and AUD**, we suggest **postdischarge behavioral therapy** for reduction of alcohol use. *(Conditional; very low)*

**Infection prevention (vaccines)**

10. In patients with cirrhosis, we recommend **testing for hepatitis A immunity and hepatitis A virus (HAV) vaccination** to prevent acute hepatitis. *(Strong; low)*
11. We suggest that patients with cirrhosis are **tested for immunity and receive immunization against hepatitis B**. *(Conditional; very low)*
12. We recommend that patients with cirrhosis receive **immunization against influenza**. *(Strong; low)*
13. We recommend that patients with cirrhosis receive the **SARS-CoV-2 vaccination series**. *(Strong; low)*
14. We recommend that patients with cirrhosis **≥50 years old receive the bivalent RSV vaccine**. *(Strong; low)*

**Lifestyle factors**

15. In patients with cirrhosis, we suggest **dietician-guided nutrition-based interventions that include adequate protein and calorie targets**. *(Conditional; low)*
16. In **ambulatory** patients with cirrhosis, we suggest **structured aerobic and resistance exercise** to improve muscle health and physical function. *(Conditional; low)*
17. In patients with cirrhosis who are **active smokers**, we recommend **smoking-cessation counseling**. *(Strong; low)*

**Non-liver-specific**

18. In patients with cirrhosis, we suggest **treatment of osteoporosis with bisphosphonates** to reduce the risk of fracture. *(Conditional; low)*

### Key concepts (ungraded, Table 3 — GRADE not applied)

1. **Ascites/paracentesis** — in cirrhosis with ascites undergoing **large-volume paracentesis (>5 L)**, advise **albumin infusion 6–8 g per L removed** to reduce circulatory dysfunction and acute kidney injury (AKI), consistent with AASLD/EASL guidance. For paracentesis **≤5 L**, albumin may be reserved for limited scenarios such as **hyponatremia or evidence of renal dysfunction**.
2. **Statins** — patients with cirrhosis **should not be offered statins for HCC chemoprevention** in the absence of conventional cardiovascular risks.
3. **Hospital readmissions** — intensified outpatient care after hospitalization for cirrhosis, **including early (<14 day) postdischarge follow-up**, may reduce readmission and mortality.
4. **Vaccines** — patients with cirrhosis **not immune to hepatitis B despite prior vaccination** should undergo **revaccination**, with consideration of the new **Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) vaccine**.
5. **AUD** — patients with cirrhosis may benefit from **screening for AUD with validated instruments such as AUDIT-C**, at **each clinical encounter**, especially at deterioration or decompensation. **Pharmacotherapy for AUD may be beneficial**.
6. **Extrahepatic cancer** — in patients **being evaluated for or listed for liver transplantation**, or those with **compensated cirrhosis who are nonfrail and without severe life-limiting comorbidities**, **age-appropriate cancer screening** should be considered per standard guidelines.

### Operative detail from the narrative (doses, thresholds, criteria)

- **Carvedilol dose:** optimal **12.5 mg daily** (daily or divided — long half-life, hepatically metabolized); **6.25 mg** may be used if 12.5 mg is not tolerated, though its relative effectiveness is unknown. Higher doses only for blood-pressure control. **Withhold for symptomatic bradycardia**, but the effect of carvedilol is **independent of its effect on heart rate** — titrating to pulse targets is not appropriate.
- **CSPH:** hepatic venous pressure gradient **≥10 mm Hg**. Noninvasive identification — probability of CSPH is high with **liver stiffness ≥25 kPa**, or **>20 kPa with platelets <150 × 10⁹/L** (ANTICIPATE nomogram).
- **Carvedilol vs ligation (Rec 4):** in a multicenter RCT of 152 adults with large varices, carvedilol gave lower first variceal bleeding (10% vs 23%; hazard ratio 0.41, 95% confidence interval 0.19–0.96) with **no difference in overall mortality (35% vs 37%) or bleeding-related mortality (3% vs 1%)**. **Either agent is acceptable first-line** depending on tolerance, comorbidity and access to endoscopy; evidence for combination over monotherapy as a primary strategy is limited.
- **HCC surveillance candidacy:** Child class **A–B** cirrhosis, or **Child C on the transplant waitlist**, who are fit for and accept the risks of potential HCC therapies.
- **Nutrition targets:** **1.2–1.5 g/kg ideal body weight/day protein** and **30–35 kcal/kg/day**. With obesity: **25–35 kcal/kg/day for body mass index (BMI) 30–40 kg/m²**, **20–25 kcal/kg/day for BMI ≥40 kg/m²**. Benefit is most consistent when nutrition is **paired with structured exercise**; branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) or protein supplementation **alone** did not significantly improve muscle mass in meta-analysis.
- **Exercise:** combined **aerobic + resistance** (± balance), typically **8–14 weeks**, center- or home-based; serious adverse events not increased. For nonambulatory patients — aquatic, chair-based or recumbent exercise, cardiopulmonary/physical rehabilitation.
- **AUD pharmacotherapy with the highest evidentiary support:** **naltrexone 50–100 mg orally daily or 380 mg intramuscularly every 4 weeks**, and **acamprosate 333–666 mg three times a day**.
- **Osteoporosis:** esophageal or gastric varices are **not by themselves a contraindication to oral bisphosphonate therapy** — cited studies in patients with varices showed no increased risk of GI bleeding. Osteopenia/osteoporosis in cirrhosis can otherwise be managed with the same agents as the general population (denosumab, anabolic agents such as teriparatide, hormone therapy where indicated).
- **Antibiotics for upper GI bleeding (Rec 5):** applies to **all** patients with cirrhosis and upper GI bleeding, **variceal or nonvariceal**, regardless of the presence of ascites. A meta-analysis of 14 RCTs (1,322 patients) found survival with short (0–3 day) duration **noninferior** to longer duration; a 2–3 vs 5–7 day subgroup showed noninferior mortality, but 1–10 days vs **no** prophylaxis did not.
- **Hepatitis B vaccine response in cirrhosis is ~50%.** Options for nonresponse: double-dose vaccination, newer TLR9-mediated vaccines, or checking hepatitis B surface antibody.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Antibiotic prophylaxis duration** — this guideline suggests **2–3 days** for upper GI bleeding in cirrhosis, shorter than the historical 7-day ceftriaxone/norfloxacin course. It notes declining baseline infection rates, poor adherence, adverse events and decreasing cephalosporin effectiveness, and cites an RCT in Child-Pugh A upper GI bleeding showing **no difference** in infection, bleeding or mortality with vs without prophylaxis. It stops short of withholding prophylaxis.
- **Aspirin and HCC** — the guideline explicitly **does not suggest** aspirin for HCC chemoprevention (very low certainty), against observational meta-analyses suggesting benefit; it cites a Danish study of 115,000 patients with cirrhosis finding no protection and an increased bleeding signal.
- **RSV age threshold** — the recommendation is written for cirrhosis **≥50 years**, while the supporting RCT enrolled adults **≥60 years** (including 335 with chronic liver disease, <2% of the cohort). The guideline's own threshold is what the wiki asserts.
- **Oral bisphosphonates with varices — a direct conflict with the cholestatic-disease guidance.** This guideline states that esophageal or gastric varices are **not by themselves a contraindication** to oral bisphosphonates. [[primary-biliary-cholangitis]] (AASLD 2021, Guidance Statement 18) calls oral bisphosphonates **contraindicated** with known varices and directs to a parenteral formulation; [[primary-sclerosing-cholangitis]] carries the same parenteral-if-varices rule. Both are tier-1; ACG 2026 is newer and governs [[cirrhosis]], while the two cholestatic pages keep their own society's rule and now carry the cross-reference.
- **Rifaximin for MHE** — reverses MHE but does **not** prevent progression to overt HE (odds ratio 0.44, 95% confidence interval 0.11–1.81), which is why #3 is a negative recommendation despite #1 and #2 being positive.
