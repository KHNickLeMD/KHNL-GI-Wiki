## Summary

The Rome V chapter on bowel disorders (BDs, previously "functional bowel disorders") — the document that **prints the actual Rome V diagnostic criteria** for the six bowel disorders, which the Rome V process overview ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]) deliberately does not. Six categories: irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), chronic constipation (CC), functional diarrhea (FDr), functional abdominal bloating (FAB), unclassified bowel disorder (U-BD), and opioid-induced constipation (OIC).

The committee frames BDs as a **spectrum rather than discrete diseases** (Figure 1): a two-axis field of pain/discomfort × bowel habit, in which CC, FDr and the IBS subtypes occupy overlapping territory and patients migrate between them over time. Two nomenclature changes follow from this: "functional constipation" becomes **chronic constipation**, and "unspecified functional bowel disorder" becomes **unclassified bowel disorder** — both dropping "functional."

Diagnosis is **positive, not exclusionary**. Testing is selective and limited: a short initial panel for all patients, a named set of tests *not* to order routinely, and explicit triggers for colonoscopy, bile acid malabsorption (BAM) testing and anorectal physiology testing (Figure 4). Treatment is organized as a stepwise pyramid (Figure 5) escalating from diagnosis and explanation → dietary advice → symptom-dominant pharmacotherapy → brain–gut behavioral therapies → multidisciplinary care, with drug choice driven by the predominant symptom.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Classification

- Six bowel disorders: **IBS** · **CC** · **FDr** · **FAB** · **U-BD** · **OIC**. OIC is kept in this article despite its specific pharmacologic etiology because it overlaps heavily with the other BDs.
- Nomenclature: **CC** replaces functional constipation (FC) / chronic idiopathic constipation (CIC) — the three are interchangeable in general use; **U-BD** replaces unspecified functional bowel disorder.
- Subtype categories are **mutually exclusive**; IBS must not be diagnosed alongside CC, and CC requires insufficient criteria for IBS.

### C1. Rome V diagnostic criteria for IBS

Recurrent, **but not continuous**, abdominal pain **or discomfort** on average **≥3 days per month in the past 3 months**, associated with **2 or more** of:

1. Related to defecation
2. Associated with a change in frequency of stool
3. Associated with a change in form (appearance) of stool

- Symptom onset **≥6 months before diagnosis**.
- **Supportive criterion:** abdominal pain/discomfort should **not be only related to menses**.

**Changes from Rome IV, and why:**

- **"Discomfort" re-added** — patients, particularly in some countries, report discomfort without pain.
- **Frequency lowered to ≥3 days/month** (Rome IV: ≥1 day/week). The Rome IV threshold is the main reason measured IBS prevalence fell between Rome III and Rome IV.
- **"Not continuous" added** — separates IBS from centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome.
- **Menses supportive criterion added** — separates IBS from menstrual-related symptoms.

### Table 1 — IBS subtypes (the 25% rule)

Subtyping uses the [[bristol-stool-form-scale|Bristol Stool Form Scale]] and counts **only days with at least 1 abnormal bowel movement**.

| Subtype | Criterion |
|---|---|
| IBS with constipation (IBS-C) | >25% of bowel movements Bristol 1–2 **and** <25% Bristol 6–7 |
| IBS with diarrhea (IBS-D) | >25% Bristol 6–7 **and** <25% Bristol 1–2 |
| IBS with mixed bowel habits (IBS-M) | >25% Bristol 1–2 **and** >25% Bristol 6–7 |
| IBS unclassified (IBS-U) | Meets IBS criteria but bowel habits cannot be accurately categorized into one of the other 3 |

- **Alternative for epidemiology or routine clinical practice:** ask the patient which picture on the Bristol chart their abnormal stools usually look like (IBS-C = usually type 1 or 2; IBS-D = usually 6 or 7; IBS-M = both >25%; IBS-U = abnormal stools are rare).
- **Confidence requires ≥4 days of abnormal bowel habit per month.**
- Subtype must be assessed **off all therapies for bowel habit** (laxatives, antidiarrheals) — otherwise IBS-C/D/M cannot be confidently established.
- For clinical trials: ≥2 weeks of daily diary data.

### Figure 4 — Stepwise initial diagnostic evaluation of bowel disorders

1. **Abdominal examination** — tenderness, distension, masses; distinguish distension from ascites and adiposity.
2. **Digital rectal examination (DRE)** — anorectal causes of bleeding (hemorrhoids, fissures); perianal appearance, sensation, anal reflexes, sphincter tone, puborectalis and anal sphincter contraction; abnormal abdominal wall contraction on simulated evacuation (suggests dyssynergic defecation / pelvic floor dysfunction).
3. **Blood tests** — *all patients:* complete blood count (CBC) to exclude anemia, C-reactive protein (CRP), celiac serology. *Selective:* complete metabolic profile (CMP), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH). **Do not routinely check:** erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), anti-vinculin / anti-cytolethal distending toxin B (CdtB), malabsorption tests.
4. **Stool tests** — *all patients:* none. *Selective:* *Giardia lamblia* antigen, enteric pathogens, calprotectin, lactoferrin, *Clostridioides difficile*. **Do not routinely check:** elastase, fecal fat, occult blood, ova and parasites (O&P), culture, microbiota assays.
5. **Colonoscopy** — consider if fecal calprotectin abnormal, or suspected microscopic colitis (female, age ≥50 y, severe watery diarrhea, duration of diarrhea <12 mo, co-existing autoimmune disease, nocturnal or severe watery diarrhea, weight loss, or use of potential precipitating drugs — nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)).
6. **Bile acid malabsorption testing** — consider in chronic diarrhea not responding to treatment, with atypical presentation (e.g. nocturnal diarrhea), and/or with obesity or previous cholecystectomy.
7. **Anorectal physiology testing** — consider with CC or FAB not responsive to treatment, symptoms and/or DRE suggestive of a defecatory disorder, and/or fecal incontinence.

**Fecal calprotectin bands (Figure 4, Box 1):** <100 normal · >100 but <250 **indeterminate — repeat off NSAIDs** · ≥250 abnormal.

### Alarm features (Figure 3, Box 2)

Anemia · rectal bleeding (clarify nature and pattern — blood mixed with stool vs only on toilet paper) · unexplained objective weight loss · nocturnal diarrhea · onset of symptoms after age 50 · fecal incontinence · family history of colorectal cancer (CRC), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or celiac disease.

### Testing stances specific to IBS

- **Positive diagnosis** based on symptoms; IBS is **not a diagnosis of exclusion**. Predictive value of alarm features for organic disease is **modest**.
- **Celiac serology** for all: immunoglobulin A (IgA) anti-tissue transglutaminase (anti-tTG) + quantitative serum IgA to exclude selective IgA deficiency. Sensitivity of anti-tTG is **reduced on a gluten-free diet**. Pooled celiac prevalence in IBS **3.3%** (36-study meta-analysis).
- **Fecal calprotectin** ≤50 µg/g has a **99.8% negative predictive value but only 9% positive predictive value** for distinguishing IBS from IBD; CRP <0.5 mg/dL carries a <1% probability of IBD. ESR and fecal lactoferrin add little.
- **Colonoscopy yield is low** in IBS without alarm features — 2% in nonconstipated IBS, mostly microscopic colitis in women >45 y. When colonoscopy *is* done for suspected IBS, **obtain biopsies to rule out microscopic colitis**.
- **Fecal hemoglobin tests** (fecal occult blood, fecal immunochemical test, multitarget stool DNA) are CRC screening tests, **not** tests to differentiate IBS from structural disease. Routine **fecal elastase** is not recommended.
- **BAM testing not recommended in the initial evaluation**; consider in treatment-refractory chronic diarrhea. Selenium-75-taurocholic acid (SeHCAT) retention is the gold standard but unavailable in many countries; serum 7α-hydroxy-4-cholesten-3-one (C4) is more accessible but not rigorously evaluated in IBS-D.
- **Lactose hydrogen breath testing** may be useful in selected patients but is **not recommended as part of the initial evaluation**.
- **Sucrase-isomaltase deficiency** — gold standard is quantitative disaccharidase activity on duodenal biopsies; prevalence in IBS uncertain; **routine testing not recommended**.
- **Breath testing for small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) or dysbiosis is not recommended** in the initial diagnostic evaluation of IBS — testing limitations and insufficient evidence of diagnostic or therapeutic value. SIBO associates more with IBS-D, intestinal methanogen overgrowth more with IBS-C.

### C2. Rome V diagnostic criteria for chronic constipation

**Must include:**

1. **Two or more** of the following:
   a. Straining during more than one-fourth (25%) of defecations
   b. Lumpy or hard stools (Bristol 1–2) in more than 25% of defecations
   c. Sensation of incomplete evacuation in more than 25% of defecations
   d. Sensation of anorectal obstruction/blockage in more than 25% of defecations
   e. Manual maneuvers to facilitate more than 25% of defecations (e.g. digital evacuation, support of the pelvic floor)
   f. Fewer than 3 spontaneous bowel movements (SBMs) per week
2. Loose stools are rarely present without the use of laxatives
3. Insufficient criteria for IBS

Criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months with symptom onset **≥6 months before diagnosis**.

- **Occasional constipation** (Rome Foundation Working Team definition) — recurrent but intermittent constipation **not meeting the definition of CC**: "the presence of at least 1 functional constipation symptom, in the absence of alarm signs or symptoms, occurring at irregular and infrequent intervals, which is bothersome enough to induce a patient to seek management."
- CC vs IBS-C: the distinction is predominant abdominal pain/discomfort. Pain may occur in CC but is **not the predominant feature**.

### CC evaluation

- CBC to exclude anemia (an alarm feature). TSH and serum calcium **when clinically indicated** (suspected hypothyroidism or hypercalcemia-inducing disease) — not routine.
- Stool, breath and endoscopic testing: same approach as IBS.
- **Imaging** (barium enema, ultrasound, computed tomography, magnetic resonance) has **minimal diagnostic value in CC and is not generally recommended**.
- **Anorectal function testing** is recommended **for patients who do not respond to therapeutic interventions**, rather than at initial evaluation — earlier if digital rectal examination strongly suggests a defecatory disorder.
- **Colon transit testing** (radiopaque markers, scintigraphy, wireless motility capsule) after a defecatory disorder has been excluded, in persistent symptoms despite medical therapy. Delayed transit suggests a motility disorder but **does not reliably predict treatment response**. If surgery for refractory constipation is considered, **upper gut transit must be evaluated** to exclude a diffuse GI motility disorder.
- **Colonic manometry** is not widely available outside academic centers and its clinical relevance to CC management remains uncertain.

### C3. Rome V diagnostic criteria for functional diarrhea

1. Loose or watery stools occurring in **more than 25% of stools**, and
2. **Hard stools are rarely present** without medication that can cause constipation

Criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months with symptom onset **≥6 months before diagnosis**. Patients meeting criteria for IBS-D **should be excluded**.

- **Change from Rome IV:** point 2 is new, added for consistency with the CC criteria.
- Abdominal pain, discomfort and/or bloating may be present but **must not be predominant**.
- Diagnostic evaluation of suspected FDr **should be like IBS-D**; the utility of testing in FDr specifically is uncertain.

### C4. Rome V diagnostic criteria for functional abdominal bloating

**Must include both:**

1. Recurrent abdominal bloating and/or visible distension on average **at least 1 day per week**; bloating and/or visible distension **predominant over other symptoms**
2. Insufficient criteria for IBS, CC, FDr, and the postprandial distress syndrome subtype of functional dyspepsia

Criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months with symptom onset **≥6 months before diagnosis**. Mild pain or discomfort related to bloating, and minor bowel movement abnormalities, may be present but must not predominate.

- **Bloating** (subjective sensation of abdominal pressure/fullness/gassiness) and **distension** (objective, measurable increase in abdominal girth) are distinct phenomena but are **classified together under FAB** — insufficient research to separate them.
- Evaluation follows the principles applied to IBS and CC; evidence-based guidance for testing in FAB is lacking.

### C5. Rome V diagnostic criteria for unclassified bowel disorder

Must include: bothersome bowel symptoms **not attributable to structural abnormalities** and **not fully meeting criteria for IBS, CC, FDr, or FAB**. Criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months with symptom onset ≥6 months before diagnosis.

- In a tertiary-center study of 813 patients meeting Rome IV criteria for a bowel disorder, **13.1% met criteria for U-BD**; they had lower overall symptom severity than other BD subtypes.
- **Only 40% still met U-BD criteria at 1 year** — most transitioned to another bowel disorder, commonly IBS.

### C6. Rome V diagnostic criteria for opioid-induced constipation

**Must include both:**

1. **New, or worsening, symptoms of constipation when initiating, changing, or increasing opioid therapy**, that must include **2 or more** of:
   a. Straining during >25% of defecations
   b. Lumpy or hard stools (Bristol 1–2) in >25% of defecations
   c. Sensation of incomplete evacuation in >25% of defecations
   d. Sensation of anorectal obstruction/blockage in >25% of defecations
   e. Manual maneuvers to facilitate >25% of defecations (e.g. digital evacuation, support of the pelvic floor)
   f. Fewer than 3 SBMs per week
2. Loose stools are rarely present without the use of laxatives

- Mechanism: opioid receptor activation (mu, kappa, delta) → decreased propulsive activity, increased nonpropulsive contractions, fluid resorption, decreased pancreatic/biliary/gastric secretions, increased anal tone.
- Diagnostic approach as for IBS-C and CC, but **no conclusive data** on test yield in OIC.

### Treatment — general

- Stepwise pyramid (Figure 5): **diagnosis and explanation** (reassurance, precipitating factors, lifestyle, symptom profile and severity) → **dietary advice including probiotics** → **pharmacotherapy for the dominant symptom** → **brain–gut behavioral therapies** (cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), hypnotherapy, psychotherapy) → **multidisciplinary, patient-centered care with physician continuity**.
- A strong patient–provider relationship is associated with improved symptom control, greater treatment adherence and reduced health care utilization. Identify the patient's **most bothersome symptom** to guide shared decision making.

### IBS treatment by predominant symptom

**Diet and lifestyle.** Exercise modestly improves global IBS symptoms (very low certainty). **Soluble fiber** (psyllium/ispaghula) benefits global symptoms (relative risk 0.83; number needed to treat 7) — **insoluble (bran) fiber does not** (RR 0.90). A **low-FODMAP** (fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols) diet has the strongest network-meta-analysis evidence; the British Dietetic Association / National Institute for Health and Care Excellence diet is more effective than a habitual diet.

**Constipation-predominant.**

- **Polyethylene glycol (PEG) 13.8–41.4 g/d** — commonly recommended as initial treatment; in a 4-week randomized controlled trial (RCT) in IBS-C it improved stool frequency, consistency and straining but **did not improve abdominal pain or bloating**.
- **Lubiprostone 8 µg twice daily** (12-week trials) — higher overall response (17.9% vs 10.1% placebo); nausea 8%, diarrhea 6%.
- **Linaclotide 290 µg once daily** (12- and 26-week trials) — more patients with considerable/complete relief for ≥6 of the first 12 weeks. Stool frequency responds sooner than pain or bloating (maximal benefit 6–8 weeks). Diarrhea 18%–20% vs 2% placebo.
- **Plecanatide 3 mg once daily** — composite endpoint (≥30% pain improvement **and** ≥1 increase in complete SBMs per week) 27.4% vs 16.9% placebo; diarrhea 4%–5% vs 1%.
- **Tenapanor 50 mg twice daily** (sodium/hydrogen exchanger isoform 3 inhibitor; 12- and 26-week trials) — 27.0% vs 18.7% and 36.5% vs 23.7%; diarrhea 16% vs 3.7%.
- **Tegaserod** improved IBS-C in phase 3 trials but **is no longer available for commercial reasons**. Mosapride and prucalopride have not been widely evaluated in IBS-C.

**Diarrhea-predominant.**

- **Bile acid sequestrants** (colestipol, colesevelam) — two small open-label studies suggest benefit; no comparators, no head-to-head data.
- **Loperamide** — recommended by some guidelines as initial treatment for IBS-D, particularly for stool frequency and urgency.
- **Eluxadoline 75 or 100 mg twice daily** (26- and 52-week phase 3 trials) — composite endpoint met in 23.9%/25.1% vs 17.1% and 28.9%/29.6% vs 16.2%. Nausea 7.7%, constipation 8.0%, abdominal pain 6.5%. **Do not prescribe** in a history of pancreatitis, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, alcohol abuse, prior cholecystectomy, or severe hepatic impairment.
- **Alosetron** — highly selective 5-hydroxytryptamine-3 (5-HT₃) antagonist, Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved **for women with severe IBS-D**, starting dose **0.5 mg twice daily**. Ramosetron and ondansetron also alleviate IBS-D symptoms, but **ondansetron does not improve abdominal pain**.
- **Rifaximin 550 mg 3 times daily for 14 days** — adequate relief of global IBS-D symptoms 40.8% vs 31.2%, bloating 39.5% vs 28.7%; benefit persisted through 10-week follow-up. **Up to 2 retreatments** superior to placebo.

**Pain-predominant.**

- **Antispasmodics and peppermint oil** — meta-analysis of 22 trials found otilonium, hyoscine and peppermint oil the most consistently effective; number needed to treat 3–4 for peppermint oil. Antispasmodic side effects: dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention, visual disturbance; peppermint oil is generally well tolerated but may cause **heartburn**.
- **Central neuromodulators** — **tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) are the best studied**; low-dose amitriptyline **10–30 mg nightly** (ATLANTIS RCT) significantly improved global symptom severity and patient-reported relief over 6 months, with dry mouth and drowsiness the most common adverse events. **Tertiary amines (imipramine, amitriptyline) cause more side effects than secondary amines (desipramine, nortriptyline).** Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors have **not** shown benefit on abdominal pain but may help patients with coexisting anxiety or depression.

**Microbiome and immune modulators.**

- **Probiotics** — moderate certainty that *Escherichia*-containing probiotics improve global IBS symptoms; low certainty for *Lactobacillus*. Adverse-event risk similar to placebo.
- **Fecal microbiota transplantation** — meta-analysis of 8 trials (4484 subjects) found **no significant improvement** in global symptoms at 12 weeks. Colonoscopic/gastroscopic delivery was superior to placebo in subgroup analysis, capsules were not; evidence quality very low. **Current guidelines recommend against FMT in IBS outside a clinical trial.**
- **5-aminosalicylic acid** — meta-analysis of 8 RCTs found mesalamine more efficacious than placebo for global symptoms (RR of not improving 0.86) but **not** for abdominal pain or bowel habits; low overall evidence quality.
- **Mast cell stabilizers and H1-receptor antagonists** (disodium cromoglycate, ebastine) — small studies report improvement in IBS-D; confirmatory multicenter results needed.

**Brain–gut behavioral and multidisciplinary therapy.** Self-administered or minimal-contact CBT, face-to-face CBT, and gut-directed hypnotherapy have the most evidence (all studies at high risk of bias). Digital therapeutics (apps) are an accessible alternative but clinicians should evaluate each patient's needs, mental health status and potential risks. Complementary and alternative medicine: herbal therapy, dietary supplements and mind–body therapy improved overall response (low certainty); **body-based and energy healing therapies showed no significant benefit over placebo/sham**.

### CC treatment

- **Lifestyle:** increase physical activity, schedule a regular bathroom routine, use a **foot-stool to elevate the feet** during defecation — supporting evidence remains limited.
- **Fiber:** total intake **20–30 g/d**, soluble or insoluble nonfermentable. **Psyllium is the best studied**, effective at mean doses **7–20 g/d**. Kiwi and prunes have benefited some patients in small RCTs.
- **Osmotic laxatives:** **lactulose 15–30 mL once or twice daily** — effective, but frequently causes abdominal cramping and bloating. **Magnesium products** are generally safe and effective but may cause **electrolyte imbalance at high doses in older individuals or renal impairment**. **PEG** is the most extensively studied osmotic and improves CC symptoms in high-quality RCTs **lasting up to 6 months**.
- **Stimulant laxatives:** bisacodyl and sodium picosulphate improved stool frequency in 4-week RCTs. **No evidence that stimulant laxatives are habit forming or cause structural or functional damage to the colon.**
- **Secretagogues:** **lubiprostone 24 µg twice daily** (4-week phase 3 trials) increased SBMs; nausea 31.7% vs 3.3% placebo, diarrhea 11.7% vs 5.7%. **Linaclotide 145 and 290 µg once daily** (12-week trials) improved stool frequency, consistency, straining and overall symptoms; diarrhea 16% vs 5%. Japanese phase 3: linaclotide 0.5 mg once daily. **Plecanatide 3 mg once daily** improved stool frequency; diarrhea 5.1% vs 1.3%.
- **Bile acid transport modulation:** **elobixibat 10 and 15 mg** (phase 2B) improved stool frequency; diarrhea 13% vs 0% placebo. **Approved for CC in Japan.**
- **Prokinetics:** **prucalopride 1, 2 and 4 mg** relieved constipation in phase 3 RCTs; the 4-mg dose was **not significantly better than 2 mg** for most endpoints and caused more GI side effects, so **only 1 mg and 2 mg are currently approved**. Diarrhea 11%–19% vs 3%–5% placebo. Tegaserod is no longer commercially available.
- **Other:** a **vibrating colon capsule** improved CC symptoms in a phase 3 trial. Occasional enemas may be effective; long-term safety of chronic enema use is unknown. **Transanal irrigation** may help severe medically refractory constipation. **Subtotal colectomy with ileo-rectostomy** is an option for the rare patient with severe, medically refractory **colonic inertia** — patient selection is critical.

### FDr treatment

- Few studies specifically in FDr; most recommendations are extrapolated from IBS-D.
- **Neuromodulators should not be initial therapy** when abdominal pain is not predominant.
- Review medications, over-the-counter agents, supplements and alternative therapies that may contribute (e.g. **magnesium, PPIs**).
- **Loperamide 2–4 mg, up to 4 times a day**, can be used routinely **without laboratory monitoring**.
- **Empiric bile acid sequestrant** (cholestyramine, colesevelam, colestipol) for **a minimum of 4 weeks** is reasonable when BAM testing is unavailable.
- **Ondansetron** improves stool consistency, frequency and urgency but **not abdominal pain**; may be effective in FDr given its antidiarrheal effect, though not studied in this population.

### FAB treatment

- **Diet:** symptom improvement reported with a low-FODMAP diet; others note benefit from reducing gluten.
- **Abdominophrenic biofeedback:** an RCT (n = 42) in meal-triggered visible abdominal distension found **thoracoabdominal wall motion–guided biofeedback using inductance plethysmography** improved distension scores; not seen in placebo.
- **Over-the-counter:** simethicone may improve gas and fullness; **α-galactosidase** may benefit patients eating oligosaccharide-rich foods; **peppermint oil 2–4 times daily** may improve bloating and distension.
- **Secretagogues** were superior to placebo for bloating (meta-analysis of RCTs).
- **Neuromodulators:** desipramine with CBT improved bloating in moderate–severe disorders of gut–brain interaction; low-dose amitriptyline, citalopram and pregabalin showed signals in small studies.
- **Prucalopride 2 mg once daily** — post hoc analysis of phase 3/4 CC studies showed greater bloating improvement vs placebo.
- **Probiotics:** low certainty of benefit for combination probiotics; **some patients report worsening of bloating with probiotics**.
- **Rifaximin:** 400 mg twice daily for 7 days improved global and bloating scores in bloating with excessive flatulence and negative lactulose breath test; 400 mg 3 times daily for 10 days reduced bloating severity over 10 weeks; 550 mg 3 times daily for 14 days gave greater adequate relief of bloating (40% vs 30%).

### OIC treatment

- Mirrors CC treatment **except** for peripherally acting mu-opioid receptor antagonists (PAMORAs).
- **Non-PAMORA:** **lubiprostone 24 µg twice daily** is the only secretagogue FDA-approved for OIC; the first 2 phase 2 studies were negative, a third RCT excluding patients on methadone was positive (27.1% vs 18.9%). Prucalopride and linaclotide showed benefit in phase 2, but large phase 3 RCTs have not been performed.
- **Methylnaltrexone** — subcutaneous **8 or 12 mg** or oral tablet **150 mg**. Subcutaneous dosing daily or every other day was superior to placebo for SBMs without rescue medication within 4 hours; oral methylnaltrexone superior at the recommended **450 mg daily**.
- **Naloxegol 12.5 and 25 mg** — 25 mg met the primary endpoint in 2 phase 3 12-week RCTs (44.4% and 39.7% vs 29.3% placebo). An open-label crossover trial (n = 246) found **no difference between PEG and naloxegol** over 2 weeks, and patients did not prefer one over the other.
- **Naldemedine 0.2 mg** — primary endpoint met in 47.6% and 52.5% vs ~34% placebo in two 12-week phase 3 RCTs in noncancer pain. Adverse events like other PAMORAs (diarrhea, abdominal pain).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **IBS pain-frequency threshold:** Rome V's ≥3 days/month supersedes the Rome IV ≥1 day/week threshold used by [[acg-2020-ibs]]. Rome V is the newer document and its threshold is what the wiki asserts; the ACG recommendations themselves were written against Rome IV.
- **"Discomfort":** reinstated by Rome V after Rome IV removed it — IBS can now be diagnosed in a patient with discomfort and no pain.
- **Continuous pain:** Rome V excludes continuous pain from IBS; neither Rome IV nor ACG 2020 did.
- **Nomenclature:** this chapter uses **chronic constipation (CC)**; the wiki page and most US guidelines ([[aga-acg-2023-constipation]]) use chronic idiopathic constipation. Rome V states the terms are interchangeable.
- **SIBO breath testing:** the Rome V committee does not recommend routine breath testing in the initial IBS evaluation, noting guideline recommendations across societies are inconsistent — [[acg-2020-sibo]] is more permissive.
- **FMT in IBS:** Rome V reports no significant benefit at 12 weeks and endorses the recommendation against FMT outside trials.
