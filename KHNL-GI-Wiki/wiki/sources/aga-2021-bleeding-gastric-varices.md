## Summary

Bleeding gastric varices (GV) are the uncommon-but-lethal end of portal hypertensive bleeding: GV are present in only 17%–25% of patients with portal hypertension (vs up to 85% for esophageal varices), and bleed less often, but when they bleed the hemorrhage is more severe — greater transfusion requirement and higher mortality — with a 3-year bleeding incidence of 16%–45%. Rates of bleeding and mortality are similar in noncirrhotic and cirrhotic portal hypertension. The update is explicit that the evidence base is weak: no definitive natural-history studies, no prospective trials, only case series, restricted cohorts and a few small randomized trials with significant selection bias. All 12 statements are Best Practice Advice grounded in expert opinion.

The central argument is that GV cannot be managed like esophageal varices, because their vascular anatomy is heterogeneous rather than uniform. Esophageal varices have consistent anatomy, so band ligation or TIPS works universally; GV drain through variable shunts and may bleed at lower portal pressures, often via a left-sided gastrorenal shunt (GRS). This drives the two structural recommendations of the paper: (1) after initial hemostasis, obtain **cross-sectional imaging with portal venous phase contrast** (CT or MR) to map the vascular anatomy before choosing definitive therapy, and (2) make that choice in a **multidisciplinary discussion** between gastroenterology/hepatology and interventional radiology, at a center resourced for it.

Therapy is then selected by GV location plus vascular anatomy. The authors propose simplifying the Sarin classification to a location-based scheme — **cardiofundal, lesser curve, distal** — and adding variceal size and high-risk stigmata (discolored marks, platelet plugs) to the description, by analogy to the NIEC criteria for esophageal varices. Lesser-curve GV extend from esophageal varices and behave like them: band ligation is often definitive. Cardiofundal GV are large and mass-like; band ligation is inferior, and **endoscopic cyanoacrylate injection (ECI) is the only definitive endoscopic therapy**. Endovascularly, BRTO is preferred when a GRS is present and severe portal hypertensive complications are absent; TIPS is preferred when there is significant coronary-vein inflow to the GV or significant comorbid portal hypertensive complications — and TIPS alone is often insufficient for cardiofundal GV, so it should be combined with direct embolization or endovascular sclerosis.

Notably, the update **rejects primary prophylaxis**: endoscopic classification systems should *not* be used to guide primary prophylaxis of GV bleeding, and practice guidelines have not supported any form of primary prophylaxis for GV. Endovascular management is likewise restricted to proven GV bleeding — after or during the first sentinel bleed — never prophylactically.

### Epidemiology and natural history
- GV prevalence in portal hypertension **17%–25%**; esophageal varices up to **85%**.
- GV bleed less often than EV but more severely — higher transfusion requirement, higher mortality; incidence of hemorrhage **16%–45% at 3 years**.
- Similar bleeding and mortality rates in **noncirrhotic** vs cirrhotic portal hypertension.
- No definitive natural-history studies, no prospective trials; evidence = case series, restricted cohorts, few small RCTs, all with significant selection bias.

### Classification
- Most gastroenterologists use the **Sarin classification** (main distinction cardiofundal vs lesser curvature), but vascular supply and therapy for GV differ from EV, so a merged classification is problematic for therapeutic planning.
- Recommended **endoscopic classification (Figure 1)** — location-based: **cardiofundal GV, lesser curve GV, distal GV**. Clearer than Sarin and correlates with vascular imaging. *Distal GV are explicitly excluded from this review* (rare, poor data, complex multidisciplinary management).
- **Add to the Sarin description**: an estimate of **variceal size** and **high-risk stigmata** (discolored marks, platelet plugs) — analogous to the North Italian Endoscopic Club (NIEC) criteria for predicting EV bleeding. Sarin identified cardiofundal GV as higher bleeding risk; other groups identified GV size, red mark, or discoloration.
- Recommended **vascular classification (Figure 2)** — the **Saad-Caldwell system**, describing afferent inflow to the GV and efferent flow through portosystemic shunts. Use it to map anatomy before any definitive therapy.

### Initial medical management (Table 2 — evidence-based algorithm for suspected portal hypertensive bleeding)

| Domain | Advice |
|---|---|
| Circulatory status | 2 large-bore peripheral IV cannulae or central venous access; fluid resuscitation (colloid or crystalloid). |
| Respiratory status | Tracheal intubation advised for active hematemesis, inability to maintain/protect airway, and as needed for optimal sedation to complete endoscopy. |
| Vasoactive drug | Reduces mortality and transfusion requirements. **Octreotide IV bolus 50 µg** (may repeat in the first hour if ongoing bleeding), then **continuous infusion 50 µg/h for 2–5 d** (may stop after definitive hemostasis). Somatostatin analogs inhibit gastric acid secretion — **co-administration of a PPI is not required**. |
| Antibiotic prophylaxis | Reduces infections, rebleeding, and mortality. **IV ceftriaxone 1 g q24h, maximum duration 7 d.** |
| Transfusion | **Restrictive**: transfuse at Hgb threshold **7 g/dL**, maintain **7–9 g/dL**. Favorable effect on HVPG, decreased mortality, decreased early rebleeding. |
| Coagulation parameters | GV bleeding is precipitated by portal hypertension, **not** a bleeding diathesis. **No data-driven INR or platelet cutoff exists** at which procedural bleeding risk is reliably increased — specific transfusion cutoffs cannot be recommended. No validated fibrinogen transfusion threshold either. Overuse of blood products in cirrhosis risks precipitating portal venous thrombosis. |
| Timing of endoscopy | After circulatory/respiratory stabilization, **within 12 h of presentation** (as soon as possible). Consider **erythromycin 250 mg IV, 30–120 min before** emergency endoscopy to optimize visualization — **check the QT interval first**. |
| Setting | Manage acute variceal hemorrhage in an ICU or other well-monitored unit. |

### Initial endoscopic evaluation and temporizing measures
- Give a **promotility agent** pre-procedure and use a **therapeutic endoscope or advanced suctioning device** — intragastric blood frequently obscures the cardia/fundus.
- Fully classify **EV as well as GV** at index endoscopy: definitive GV therapies differ in their effect on subsequent EV bleeding risk.
- **Band ligation** — initial hemostasis **45%–93%**; inferior to cyanoacrylate for long-term control of cardiofundal GV, so a reasonable *temporizing* measure that must be followed by definitive therapy. For **lesser curve GV it is often the best definitive therapy**, given their similarity to EV.
- **Alcohol-based sclerotherapy (e.g. ethanolamine)** — marginal initial hemostasis, high early rebleeding, deep ulceration; **not recommended unless no other option**.
- **Gastric compression balloons** (Sengstaken-Blakemore, Linton-Nachlas) — highly effective temporizing for cardiofundal and lesser curve GV. **Inflate only the gastric balloon** and proceed to definitive therapy as fast as possible — prolonged balloon tension precipitates mucosal breakdown.
- **ECI is not recommended as a temporizing measure** absent other options — logistics limit its use at the index diagnostic exam, and undefined vascular anatomy (thrombosis, shunting) may affect its result.
- **Procoagulants (activated factor VII) not recommended** — thrombotic risk, no efficacy data in GV bleeding.

### Definitive endoscopic management — cyanoacrylate injection (ECI)
- **ECI is the only definitive endoscopic therapy recommended for cardiofundal GV.** Their size and mass-like configuration make band ligation difficult; injection into the deeper submucosal varices is physiologically superior.
- Effective at preventing early and late rebleeding with fewer complications than alcohol sclerotherapy or band ligation.
- **Prefer the fastest-polymerizing formulation** to avoid embolization: **4-carbon (butyl) preparations polymerize much faster than 8-carbon (octyl)** and have more supporting data.
- **Do not add plant-based oils (lipiodol)** — radiographic confirmation of success is unnecessary and the additive may increase distal embolization risk.
- No cyanoacrylate formulation is **FDA-approved** as a long-term implant into vascularized human tissue, though it is routinely used for GV.
- **Complications:** glue embolization (pulmonary embolus, stroke) — **0.7% in the largest series**, with clinically significant events very rare; needle impaction into the GV (case reports only); exacerbation of bleeding (rare if glue is injected immediately after needle insertion); portal/splenic vein thrombosis (extremely rare); infection (mainly in actively bleeding patients, who should receive antibiotic prophylaxis anyway).
- Perform **only** by endoscopists with specific training, with interventional radiology available for complications.
- **EUS-guided ECI** (coils and/or glue via FNA needle) — large case series show efficacy and safety; potential advantages are real-time Doppler assessment and reduced embolization risk. **Not directly compared with standard ECI; routine use cannot yet be recommended.**

### Endoscopic follow-up after ECI
- Repeat endoscopy **every 2–4 weeks until obliteration is complete**, for both lesser curve and cardiofundal GV.
- At follow-up, **palpate a previously treated cardiofundal GV** with a blunt-tipped instrument or Doppler probe — areas that **dimple or invert merit repeat ECI**.
- For lesser curve GV, repeat band ligation as per EV eradication.
- After endoscopically confirmed eradication: reassess **within 3–6 months, then yearly**.
- For de novo or recurrent GV on long-term (>12 months) follow-up: repeat cross-sectional imaging and return to multidisciplinary discussion.

### Definitive endovascular management
**Restriction:** endovascular management of GV should only be performed as treatment for **proven GV bleeding** — electively after, or emergently during, the first sentinel bleed.

**TIPS**
- Less efficacious in GV than in EV. Cardiofundal GV bleed at lower portal pressures, and even after TIPS **up to 50% may rebleed**.
- Direct comparisons with ECI: similar initial hemostasis, slightly better long-term rebleeding with TIPS, but **added cost and increased complications (encephalopathy)**.
- **Favor TIPS for lesser curve GV refractory to band ligation, or recurrent bleeding** — analogous to EV management.
- TIPS risks (hepatic encephalopathy, hepatic ischemia) **may be greater when a GRS is present**, which is common with cardiofundal GV.
- For cardiofundal GV, TIPS is most efficacious **combined with direct embolization or endovascular sclerosis**.
- **Endoscopy 1 month after TIPS** to confirm GV resolution. Without direct obliteration, continued flow through cardiofundal GV is likely; shrinking GV may need nothing further, but **stable or enlarging cardiofundal GV merit subsequent ECI or BRTO** — and after TIPS, ECI carries increased risk of glue embolization or portal vein thrombosis, so **BRTO is favored** (or direct GV embolization through the patent TIPS). Persistent lesser curve GV after TIPS → repeat band ligation to eradication.

**BRTO**
- Conventional BRTO approaches cardiofundal GV with an indwelling occlusive balloon from the systemic veins **via a GRS**. **Up to 85% of cardiofundal GV** are associated with a left-sided spontaneous portosystemic shunt (GRS) draining into the left renal vein.
- Accelerated variants replace the balloon with permanent hardware — **CARTO** (coil-assisted) and **PARTO** (plug-assisted); physiologically plausible but **not supported by the same volume of data** as conventional BRTO.
- Efficacy: cessation of active bleeding in **>90%**; intention-to-treat GV rebleed rate consistently **<5%–7% at 1 year**. No adequately sized RCTs.
- Meta-analysis of TIPS vs BRTO for cardiofundal GV: no difference in initial hemostasis or procedure-related complications, but **BRTO had less rebleeding and less encephalopathy**.
- **Complications:** post-BRTO bleeding is most commonly from **EV, which are exacerbated after BRTO — up to 30%–35% progress in size**. New/worsened **ascites or hepatic hydrothorax** requiring intervention in ~**15% within 1 year** (present on imaging in **35%–40%**).
- **Hepatic encephalopathy improves** after BRTO — incidence **0%–5% at 1 year**; type B (portosystemic-shunt-related) HE is in fact an *indication* for BRTO. Liver synthetic function may improve from increased portal inflow, though no outcome benefit has been demonstrated.
- **Post-BRTO follow-up:** EUS within **48 h** to confirm obliteration, assess EV exacerbation, and set a new baseline. Unobliterated minimal areas usually thrombose spontaneously within **1–2 months**. Clinic visit + labs + contrast-enhanced CT/MR at **4–6 weeks**, then **3 and 6 months** and as clinically indicated. **Repeat upper endoscopy within 2 weeks if high-risk EV were present at BRTO, within 4–6 weeks if low-risk EV**; treat EV per standard guidelines.

### Primary prophylaxis
- GV are rare relative to EV and the endoscopic/clinical predictors of bleeding are less robust; available data are heterogeneous in populations, risk factors, and bleeding definitions.
- **Practice guidelines have not supported any form of primary prophylaxis for GV**, and endoscopic classification must not be used to guide it (BPA 1).

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Timing of endoscopy.** This update says "within 12 h of presentation (as soon as possible)" for suspected portal hypertensive bleeding. Confirm against [[baveno-viii-2026-portal-hypertension]] and [[aasld-2023-portal-hypertension]] before the number is asserted as the wiki's standing recommendation — as a 2021 tier-1 expert review it is **outranked by the newer consensus documents** under the source-priority rule.
- **Primary prophylaxis of GV.** BPA 1 forbids using endoscopic classification to guide it and states no form of primary prophylaxis is supported. Baveno VII/VIII and AASLD carry their own positions on NSBB in patients with gastric varices; where they differ, the newer document wins the page and this 2021 statement is recorded as what the AGA advised in 2021.
- **Sarin vs the location-based scheme.** The update proposes replacing Sarin with cardiofundal/lesser curve/distal, but acknowledges "most gastroenterologists use the Sarin classification" and that the outcome literature is not stratified by GV type at all. Both nomenclatures should appear on the wiki, mapped to each other, rather than one silently replacing the other.
- **EUS-guided coil/glue** is explicitly *not yet* recommended for routine use here (2021). Check against [[aga-2023-interventional-eus-vascular]], which is newer and may have moved.
- **Evidence quality is uniformly low.** Every statement is Best Practice Advice from expert opinion; there is no GRADE rating anywhere in the document, and the authors state that strong evidence-based recommendations are not currently possible.
- Not captured: **Supplementary Figure 1** (ECI procedural detail) is online-only and absent from the ingested PDF, so the update's step-by-step injection technique is not on the wiki.
