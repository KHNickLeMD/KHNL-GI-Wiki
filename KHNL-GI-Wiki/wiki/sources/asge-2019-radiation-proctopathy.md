- **Year:** 2019
- **Journal/Publisher:** Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE)
- **DOI:** [10.1016/j.gie.2019.04.234](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gie.2019.04.234)
- **Type:** guideline (ASGE Standards of Practice; systematic review + meta-analysis, GRADE-rated recommendations)

## Summary

American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) Standards of Practice guideline on endoscopic therapy for bleeding from **chronic radiation proctopathy**, a common late sequela of pelvic radiation therapy (bladder, cervical, prostate, rectal cancer) affecting up to 30% of irradiated patients. It is a **revision of the 2010 ASGE statement on endoscopy in anorectal disorders** ([[asge-2010-anorectal-disorders|ASGE 2010]]). Literature search: Ovid Medline 1946 to January 2017 and Embase 1988 to January 2017, English only, case series restricted to >5 patients.

The document separates **acute** radiation proctopathy (during or within 3 months of radiation therapy; superficial epithelial depletion with acute inflammatory infiltrate; usually self-limiting; treat supportively) from **chronic** radiation proctopathy (≥3 months, median onset 8–12 months; obliterative/ischemic endarteritis of submucosal arterioles, submucosal fibrosis, neovascularization, with a relative *lack* of inflammatory infiltrate). Endoscopy is the main diagnostic tool — pallor, edema, friability, spontaneous bleeding, telangiectasias. **Biopsy is discouraged** (risk of non-healing ulcer or fistula); biopsy only to exclude another proctitis such as infection or [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]].

Evidence is limited — mostly small case series, few comparative trials, no uniform disease definition or endpoint. Pooled analyses nevertheless found [[argon-plasma-coagulation|argon plasma coagulation (APC)]] (87% clinical success), [[electrosurgery|bipolar electrocoagulation]] (88%), heater probe, and [[radiofrequency-ablation|radiofrequency ablation (RFA)]] all effective for rectal bleeding, with **no modality proven superior**. The panel suggests *against* 4% formalin compared with APC because of higher adverse-event rates, and makes **no recommendation** on newer-generation cryoablation. Most of the document's clinical value is in the per-modality device settings, session counts, intervals, and adverse-event profiles reproduced below.

## Key Findings / Claims

### How this document numbers and grades

- **The document numbers its own recommendations 1–3** (Table 3, "Recommendations", repeated in the closing RECOMMENDATIONS section). The numbering below is the document's, not the wiki's.
- Two separate labels, from the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework:
  - **Strength of recommendation:** *Strong* or *Conditional*. Strength is also carried by the verb — "we recommend" is strong, "we suggest" conditional.
  - **Quality of evidence:** *High*, *Moderate*, *Low*, *Very low* (Table 1). No circle glyphs are used.
- **Statement 3 carries no strength and no quality rating** — both Table 3 cells are blank. It is an explicit *insufficient evidence* statement, not a recommendation for or against.

### Definitions used to read the results

- **Clinical success** (the document's own composite, because trial definitions varied) = *any* of: bleeding cessation · hemoglobin improvement by 10% or normalization · bleeding-score improvement · eradication of telangiectasias. The pooled 87%–88% figures are that composite, **not** bleeding cessation alone.
- **Severe adverse event**, defined a priori across all modalities = colonic **fistula, perforation, explosion, or stricture**.
- **No validated diagnostic criterion and no unified endoscopic grading system exist.** Two scoring systems appear in the literature but require further validation before clinical use; the document does not print their criteria. Trials in the evidence tables used ad hoc instruments (a rectal telangiectasia density score, a radiation proctitis severity assessment scale) whose criteria the document also does not give.

### Disease background

- Bladder, cervical, prostate, and rectal cancers account for ~300,000 new United States cases in 2019; up to **30%** of patients irradiated for pelvic malignancy develop radiation proctopathy.
- **Acute** (during or ≤3 months): abdominal/pelvic pain, tenesmus, diarrhea, urgency; rectal bleeding and [[fecal-incontinence|fecal incontinence]] less common; usually self-limiting. Most respond to hydration, antidiarrheal medication, and discontinuation of radiation therapy.
- **Chronic** (≥3 months, median 8–12 months): persisting acute symptoms, or rectal bleeding, fecal incontinence, or symptoms from stricture or fistula. Medical/supportive therapy is often insufficient, particularly for rectal bleeding → endoscopic therapy has become the widely used treatment.

### Before any endoscopic therapy (ungraded panel statements)

- Patients should have a **clear understanding of the risks, benefits, and alternatives** to endoscopic therapy.
- **Informed consent must cover:** natural history of chronic radiation proctopathy · treatment options · risks and benefits of each approach · **how many therapy sessions** and **how far apart** · duration of follow-up.
- **No mandate to treat "cosmetic" rectal bleeding** — minor rectal bleeding *without anemia* — because no studies support endoscopic management for that indication.
- Weigh patient preferences, comorbidities, life expectancy, and which endoscopic therapies are locally available.
- The choice of medical vs endoscopic management should be individualized to patient and provider preference, local resources, and expertise.

### APC — technique and safety

- **Target individual vessels; avoid "painting" with APC**, which causes large confluent ulcers.
- **Remove insufflated argon gas periodically** — bowel distension from rapid argon instillation causes abdominal, rectal, or anal pain, the most common APC-related adverse event. Ulceration from the treatment itself also contributes.
- **Adequate bowel preparation is required before APC.** Colonic explosions were reported in **2 poorly prepped patients who received an enema preparation only**.
- Success was essentially unchanged by flow rate: **87% at ≤1 L/min vs 86% at >1 L/min**.
- Most studies used the **first-generation APC machine (ICC/APC 300)**, which may not be available in most endoscopy units; only 3 studies used the **second-generation machine (VIO APC/APC 2)**.
- **vs 4% formalin:** randomized trial of 30 patients with intractable bleeding (≥1 episode/week or transfusion-requiring despite medical therapy) — bleeding ceased in **94% with APC vs 100% with formalin** (median 2 sessions each), no difference in efficacy or durability, no adverse events in either arm. Cohort of 25 patients — clinical success **79% (11/14) with APC vs 27% (3/11) with formalin** (P = .017), similar session numbers (1.7 vs 1.8), but **fewer adverse events with APC (36% vs 82%, P = .001)** — nausea, vomiting, rectal pain, fever.
- **vs bipolar electrocoagulation:** randomized trial of 30 patients — no significant difference in telangiectasia eradication (**93% vs 80%**, P = .6) or sessions needed (3.7 vs 2.9, P = .3); bleeding from treatment ulcers was higher with bipolar but not significant (33.3% vs 6.7%, P = .17); over mean follow-up 12.5 months no difference in relapse (8% vs 14%, P = 1.0).

### Bipolar electrocoagulation — safety

- Data on serious adverse events are limited. In one randomized trial, **5/15 (33%) had worsening rectal bleeding during or after the procedure** and **4/15 (27%) developed rectal stenosis** (only 1 symptomatic). A second trial reported 0/12 serious adverse events at 12 months.
- **No reports of perforation or fistula** after bipolar electrocoagulation for this indication.

### Heater probe

- Only 1 randomized trial compares heater probe with bipolar electrocoagulation (21 patients with recurrent hematochezia and anemia): bleeding control **67% vs 75%** at 12 months, no major adverse events in either arm.

### RFA — technique

- RFA ablates epithelium and muscularis mucosa **without injuring the submucosa**.
- **Generally 2 applications of RFA per site.**
- Ablate **~1 mm proximal to the dentate line** (prevents sensory injury to the anal mucosa) and restrict treatment to a **short length, <6 cm to the dentate line**.
- **Withdraw the endoscope and device for cleaning every 8 applications** to preserve electrode surface effectiveness.
- **Leave the coagulum on the treated mucosa — do not scrape it off** (promotes hemostasis).
- Larger series (39 patients failing medical therapy): bleeding improved in all, hemoglobin 11.8 → 13.5 g/dL, mean 1.5 sessions, mean follow-up 28 months.

### Cryoablation

- Effect is mainly **ischemic necrosis** of affected rectal mucosa, immediate or delayed; like APC and RFA it can treat a large surface area.
- 7 patients refractory to prior laser, thermal, or electrosurgical coagulation: **100% clinical success with liquid nitrogen**, mean 3.7 sessions, **2–3 days apart**, no serious adverse events at 6 months.
- 10 patients: **70% clinical success after a single session**; the radiation proctitis severity assessment scale (a composite of diarrhea, urgency, rectal pain, tenesmus, rectal bleeding, and fecal incontinence) fell **51%, from 27.7 to 13.6** (P = .009); **no significant change in hemoglobin**; mean follow-up 3.3 months.
- **Both cryoablation systems studied have been discontinued**, and there are no published data on new-generation systems — the basis for statement 3.
- The single perforation (1 of 10 patients, requiring colectomy) was attributed to **over-insufflation, likely a failure of the decompression tube rather than cryotherapy itself**.

### Non-endoscopic therapies — named but not detailed

- The systematic review covered **endoscopic** interventions only. Medical and surgical therapies were **not** systematically reviewed.
- Randomized trials exist for oral metronidazole, short-chain fatty acids, formalin, and sulfasalazine/[[mesalamine-5-asa|5-aminosalicylic acids]].
- Other medical treatments used, **some with mixed success**: steroids, antioxidants, **sucralfate enemas**, and **hyperbaric oxygen**. This document names them only — it gives no dose, concentration, or schedule for any of them, and makes no recommendation about hyperbaric oxygen.
- **Surgery is usually the last therapeutic option**; fewer than **10%** of patients with chronic radiation proctopathy ultimately require it. **Indications: perforation · intractable bleeding or bleeding refractory to medical and/or endoscopic therapy · obstructing stricture · fistula formation.**

## Recommendations (verbatim)

*All three are numbered by the document. Statement 3 is printed with no strength and no quality rating.*

1. In patients with chronic radiation proctopathy, we suggest argon plasma coagulation, bipolar electrocoagulation, heater probe, and radiofrequency ablation for treatment of bleeding from chronic radiation proctopathy. There is insufficient evidence to recommend a specific endoscopic modality over another for treatment of bleeding from chronic proctopathy. *(Conditional recommendation, Low quality of evidence)*
2. In patients with chronic radiation proctopathy, we suggest against the use of 4% formalin compared with argon plasma coagulation because of higher adverse event rates compared with argon plasma coagulation for treatment of bleeding from chronic radiation proctopathy. *(Conditional recommendation, Low quality of evidence)*
3. In patients with chronic radiation proctopathy, there is insufficient evidence for or against the use of the newer-generation cryoablation system for treatment of bleeding from chronic radiation proctopathy. *(No strength or quality rating given; this is a non-recommendation)*

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Formalin: a direct reversal of ASGE 2010.** [[asge-2010-anorectal-disorders|ASGE 2010]] *suggested* topical formalin as an alternative treatment for chronic radiation proctopathy (Low quality). This 2019 revision **suggests against** 4% formalin compared with APC, on adverse-event rates. The newer document governs; readers carrying the 2010 guidance should know the direction flipped.
- **APC lost its first-line status.** ASGE 2010 suggested APC as the first-line endoscopic approach. This document endorses APC, bipolar electrocoagulation, heater probe, and RFA together and states there is insufficient evidence to prefer any one of them.
- **The formalin recommendation is narrower than the evidence behind it.** The two comparative studies disagree on efficacy — one randomized trial found formalin at least as effective as APC with no adverse events in either arm, while the cohort study found formalin both less effective and far more toxic. The recommendation rests on adverse-event rates alone and the document does not reconcile the efficacy discrepancy.
- **Heater probe energy is stated two ways.** The narrative gives 200–400 joules per session, while the randomized trial in the evidence table used 10–15 J. The document does not reconcile them or say which to use.
- **Statement 3 is sometimes read as a recommendation against cryoablation — it is not.** It is an insufficient-evidence statement, ungraded. Both systems actually studied have been discontinued, so the evidence that exists does not apply to currently available devices.
- **No grading system to anchor severity.** With no validated diagnostic criterion and no unified endoscopic grading system, "severity" cannot be scored; the only threshold the document offers for treating is the presence of anemia.
- **APC settings differ from newer same-tier guidance for a different target lesion.** [[acg-2023-lgib|ACG 2023]] gives 20–40 W at 0.8–1.0 L/min for colonic [[angioectasia|angioectasias]]; this document gives 25–80 W (median 50 W) at 0.6–2.5 L/min for radiation proctopathy. Different lesions, not a conflict — but do not carry one setting to the other indication.
- Evidence base is small, uncontrolled, and heterogeneous in its definitions of both the disease and clinical success; no head-to-head superiority is established, and no data exist on patient preferences, resource utilization, or cost-effectiveness.
