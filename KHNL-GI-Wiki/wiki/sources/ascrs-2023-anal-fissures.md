## Summary

The ASCRS update of its 2017 anal fissure guideline, and the **only colorectal-surgery guideline on fissure in the corpus**. It is the document that supplies what the gastroenterology guidelines on [[anal-fissure]] do not: the **surgical** half of the decision — when lateral internal sphincterotomy (LIS) may be offered up front rather than after medical failure, who should be steered away from it, how long to cut, what to do for a fissure that recurs after sphincterotomy, and what the sphincter-preserving alternative is.

Its most consequential divergence from [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]] is at the top of the ladder. ACG 2021 sequences chronic fissure as topical calcium channel blocker (CCB) → nitrate → botulinum toxin of uncertain role → refer for LIS. ASCRS 2023 treats **topical nitrates, CCBs and botulinum toxin as broadly interchangeable first-line options** (each 1B, with CCBs preferred on side effects rather than efficacy) and then says something ACG does not: **LIS may be offered in selected pharmacologically naive patients** — first-line surgery, graded **1A** — because medical compliance is poor and long-term continence and quality of life are preserved in the vast majority. It names the patients for whom that door is closed: **women with prior obstetric injury, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), previous anorectal operations, and documented anal sphincter injury.**

The technical content is the other reason this document matters, and it is entirely net-new to the wiki. **Open and closed LIS are equivalent (1A).** **"Tailored" LIS — cutting only to the apex of the fissure rather than up to the dentate line — heals equally well with less fecal incontinence (FI)**, backed by a 287-patient series with a **1.4% flatus-incontinence rate and no stool incontinence**, and by an endoanal-ultrasound study showing Wexner scores separate at a **25%-of-sphincter-length** cut. For the fissure that comes back after LIS, there is a **repeat contralateral tailored LIS** (98% healing, 4% minor FI at 12.5 years, single study) or **botulinum toxin into the internal anal sphincter** (74% healing, 10% temporary flatus incontinence), both graded 2C. For the patient at high FI risk, the **anocutaneous (dermal V-Y or house) flap** is the sphincter-preserving alternative (2B).

⚠ **The document contradicts itself on one grade.** Recommendation 8 (tailored LIS) is printed as **1A** in Table 1, the "what is new" box, and as **1B** in the body text that states it. Both are reproduced below; neither has been silently chosen.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Grading system

GRADE as adapted from Guyatt et al.: **1A/1B/1C** = strong recommendation on high / moderate / low-quality evidence; **2A/2B/2C** = weak recommendation on high / moderate / low-quality evidence. **The document numbers its own 11 recommendations** — the numbering below is the document's, not the wiki's.

|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Nonoperative treatment of acute anal fissures is safe and should typically be first-line treatment.** | Strong, moderate-quality — **1B** |

| 3 | Compared with topical nitrates, **calcium channel blockers for chronic anal fissure have similar efficacy with a superior side-effect profile, and can be used as first-line treatment.** | Strong, moderate-quality — **1B** |

**What changed from the 2017 ASCRS guideline (Table 1):** the evidence grade was updated on **4 recommendations — 2, 3, 4 and 8**; supporting text was refreshed for all the rest. The document states explicitly why nitrates were moved to 1B: *"the benefits must be closely balanced with the side effects."*

### Definitions and epidemiology

- **Anal fissure** = a linear tear within the anal canal, usually extending from the dentate line toward the anal verge.
- **Acute = symptoms present <6 weeks**, appearing as a longitudinal tear. Longer duration manifests one or more **stigmata of chronicity**: hypertrophied anal papilla proximally, sentinel tag distally, and/or **exposed internal anal sphincter muscle in the fissure base**.
- **Pathogenesis of chronic fissure:** internal anal sphincter hypertonicity → local ischemia → impaired wound healing.
- **Location:** **posterior midline 73%**; anterior midline in **13% of women and 8% of men**; **2.6% simultaneously anterior and posterior**.
- **Lateral or multiple fissures are atypical** and require more comprehensive evaluation, for association with **HIV infection, Crohn's disease, syphilis, tuberculosis, and hematologic malignancies**.
- No population-level incidence data exist.

### Recommendation 1 — acute fissure, nonoperative

- **Nearly half of acute fissures resolve** with sitz baths and **psyllium or other bulking agents**, with or without topical anesthetics or topical steroids; minimal to no side effects.
- Randomized trial, 103 patients: pain relief with **sitz baths + fiber 91%**, vs topical anesthetic **60%**, vs topical hydrocortisone **68%** (*p* < 0.05).
- Maintenance **fiber after healing cuts recurrence**: 16% vs 60% on placebo (*p* < 0.01), 75 patients.
- **No data support one type of fiber over another.**
- ⚠ **Healing falls as symptom duration rises** — 60-patient prospective study: **100% healing at <1 month of symptoms vs 33.3% at >6 months**.

### Recommendation 2 — topical nitrates

- Topical nitroglycerin heals **~50%** of chronic fissures. Cochrane meta-analysis, 18 trials, 734 patients: odds of persistence or recurrence **OR 0.35 (95% CI 0.19–0.65)** vs placebo.
- ⚠ **Escalating the concentration buys headache, not healing** — 200-patient multicentre trial of glyceryl trinitrate (GTN): severe headache **2% / 6.5% / 24%** at **0.1% / 0.2% / 0.4%**, with healing **47% / 40% / 54%** (*p* = 0.3).
- **Headache in at least 30%**, near-ubiquitous in some reports, and **stops therapy in up to 20%**.

### Recommendation 3 — calcium channel blockers

- Topical GTN vs diltiazem, 45 patients: healing **54.9% vs 66.7%** (*p* = 0.2); ultimate topical failure 45% vs 33% (*p* > 0.05).
- 2013 systematic review, 7 trials, 238 patients: diltiazem had fewer side effects (**RR 0.48, 0.27–0.86**) and less headache (**RR 0.39, 0.24–0.66**) with **no healing difference (RR 1.10, 0.90–1.34)**. 2020 meta-analysis of 8 RCTs: headache **RR 0.15 (0.07–0.34)** favouring diltiazem.
- **Topical is preferred to oral** — one RCT: healing **73.3% topical vs 49.5% oral** (*p* < 0.05); systemic effects **4.3% topical vs 38.0% oral** (*p* < 0.0001). Another report found equal success, so the evidence conflicts on efficacy but not on systemic toxicity.

### Recommendation 4 — botulinum toxin

- ⚠ **No consensus protocol for dose, injection site or number of injections**, and the trials are heterogeneous on all three.
- Meta-analysis of 1,577 patients: **no dose dependency** for healing (range **33%–96%**) or complications; **5% overall transient FI**.
- Meta-analysis of 1,158 patients, 18 trials: **lower doses did slightly better** — per unit increase, healing fell 0.34% (0%–0.68%), incontinence **RR 1.02 (1.0002–1.049)** and recurrence **RR 1.04 (1.02–1.06)**.
- **Doses used in comparative trials: 20–60 units** (healing 67%, vs 71% for topical therapy); **20 units vs topical 2% diltiazem both healed 43%** at 3 months, with ≥50% pain reduction in 82% vs 78% (*p* = 0.142).
- 2014 multicentre randomized study: botulinum toxin beat topical nitroglycerin on healing (**67% vs 33%**, *p* = 0.01); the 1-year recurrence difference (28% vs 50%) did **not** reach significance.
- **Combination with nitroglycerin** beat botulinum toxin alone in a 30-patient trial: **60% vs 20%** healing (*p* = 0.025).
- Predictors of botulinum toxin success: **female sex and lower body mass index** (single retrospective review).

### Recommendations 5–8 — lateral internal sphincterotomy

- LIS is superior to nitrates, CCBs and botulinum toxin across multiple randomized trials: **healing 88%–100%**, **FI 8%–30%** depending on follow-up out to 6 years.
- ⚠ **Part of LIS's advantage is compliance, not biology** — long-term medical therapy is poorly adhered to, and patients with **symptoms exceeding 1 year** respond less well to medical therapy.
- **LIS may be excluded as first-line therapy in:** women with prior obstetrical injuries · patients with IBD · patients with previous anorectal operations · patients with a documented anal sphincter injury.
- **Open vs closed (Rec 7):** no significant outcome difference; healing **90%–100% open** and **85%–100% closed**, minor FI **5%–25%**. One study found delayed healing at 1 year in the open group vs none closed (*p* = 0.08).
- **Tailored LIS (Rec 8)** = sphincterotomy limited to **the apex of the fissure**, versus conventional LIS **transecting internal sphincter up to the dentate line**.
  - 287-patient retrospective series over 30 years: **1.4% flatus incontinence, no stool incontinence**.
  - 31 women after tailored LIS, grouped by endoanal ultrasound: mean **Wexner score 1 when <25% of sphincter length was divided vs 3 when ≥25%** (*p* = 0.004).
  - Comparative trials: postoperative Wexner scores rose significantly from baseline in the conventional arm (0.58 → 0.16, *p* = 0.02) but not in the tailored arm; a third trial found slightly higher tailored-arm scores that were not significant (0.8 vs 0.2, *p* = 0.37).

### Recommendation 9 — the fissure that recurs after LIS

- **Repeat contralateral tailored LIS**, 57 patients, single study: **98% healing, 4% minor FI** at 12.5 years mean follow-up.
- **Botulinum toxin into the internal anal sphincter after failed LIS**, 80 patients: **74% healing, 10% temporary flatus incontinence**.
- Both rest on one study each; the grade is 2C for that reason.

### Recommendations 10–11 — anocutaneous flap

- **Dermal V-Y or "house" flap**, for patients at higher FI risk after LIS: healing **81%–100%**, minor FI **0%–6%**.
- Randomized and comparative data vs LIS: FI **2.5% flap vs 17% LIS** (*p* = 0.01) and **2% vs 17%** (*p* = 0.01) in two prospective studies — **neither of which assessed healing**. A retrospective comparison found healing **96% flap vs 88% LIS** (*p* = 0.27) with no FI in either arm.
- **Flap added to botulinum toxin:** healing 86.7%–100%, pain resolution 76.7%–100%, negligible FI (2 small prospective studies, 30 patients).
- **Flap added to LIS:** tailored LIS + V-Y flap vs conventional LIS alone — less postoperative pain (*p* < 0.001), faster healing (**80% vs 40% at 2 weeks**), flatus incontinence **6.6% vs 28.1%** (*p* < 0.05).
- Three-arm randomized study, 150 patients (LIS vs V-Y flap vs both), 1 year: healing **84% / 48% / 94%** (*p* = 0.001), recurrence **4% / 22% / 2%** (*p* = 0.01), FI **14% / 0% / 2%** (*p* = 0.03).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- ⚠ **Internal to the document:** Recommendation 8's grade is **1A in Table 1 and 1B in the body**. Both printed above; neither chosen.
- ⚠ **First-line sequencing — ASCRS 2023 vs [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]].** ACG 2021 recommends topical **CCB as *the* initial medical treatment**, nitrates second, botulinum toxin of uncertain role, LIS after medical failure. ASCRS 2023 makes **nitrates, CCBs and botulinum toxin each a first-line option** (CCBs preferred on side-effect profile, not efficacy) and adds **LIS as a first-line option in pharmacologically naive patients without baseline FI (1A)** — which ACG does not offer at all. **ASCRS 2023 is the newer tier-1 document and governs the page**, with ACG's sequence kept visible, because the two agree on the one thing that drives the choice: CCB beats nitrate on headache, not on healing.
- ⚠ **Acute/chronic cut-point.** ASCRS 2023: **acute = <6 weeks**. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]: acute <8 weeks, chronic >8–12 weeks. Newer tier-1 wins on the page; both printed, because the practical consequence is the same (duration is a continuum and healing falls steadily with it — 100% at <1 month vs 33.3% at >6 months).
- ⚠ **Fissure location percentages.** ASCRS 2023 gives **73% posterior, 13% of women / 8% of men anterior, 2.6% both**. The page carried "almost 90% posterior" from ACG 2021 and "anterior 19%" from [[asge-2010-anorectal-disorders]], with a note that ACG gives no anterior figure and no sex split. **ASCRS supersedes both** and supplies the sex split that was missing.
- **The page's standing gap note is only partly closed.** ASCRS 2023 **does not** give an examination-under-anesthesia rule, a biopsy rule for the non-healing fissure, or an age threshold for colonoscopy at first presentation. It says only that **lateral or multiple fissures are atypical and warrant more comprehensive evaluation** for HIV, Crohn's disease, syphilis, tuberculosis and hematologic malignancy. The narrowed gap stays flagged.
- **Open:** the document gives **no botulinum toxin dosing protocol** and says explicitly that no consensus one exists — so the wiki still cannot state a recommended dose, only the 20–60 unit range used in trials and the signal that lower is better.
