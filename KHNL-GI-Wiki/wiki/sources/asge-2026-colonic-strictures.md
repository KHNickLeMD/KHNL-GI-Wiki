## Summary

The first ASGE document to cover **both benign and malignant colonic strictures** since the 2010 Standards of Practice guideline on colonic obstruction and pseudo-obstruction (the 2019 update on acute colonic pseudo-obstruction and volvulus explicitly excluded obstruction treatment). It uses the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework across five areas: initial assessment, management of malignant colonic obstruction from potentially resectable colorectal cancer (CRC), from unresectable CRC, from extracolonic malignancy (MCO-ECM), and management of benign colonic strictures.

Six comparative questions were answered with full GRADE methodology; **all six recommendations are conditional, and the certainty of evidence is low or very low throughout**. A further five questions (noninvasive testing, endoscopic examination, biopsy technique, tattooing, and procedural technique) were answered by extensive literature review and panel consensus **without GRADE** and are presented as best practice advice.

Several of the panel's own meta-analyses are new to this document (permanent stoma, cancer recurrence, stricturotomy vs balloon dilation), and two of the headline results carry a **fragility index of 2** — the cancer-recurrence signal for stent-as-bridge-to-surgery, and the recurrence advantage for stricturotomy.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definitions used by the guideline

- **Asymptomatic colorectal stricture** — narrowing of the colonic lumen that does not cause symptoms, found incidentally on endoscopic examination or cross-sectional images.
- **Symptomatic colorectal stricture** — narrowing of variable severity causing symptoms of partial or complete colonic obstruction, often accompanied by diffuse upstream bowel dilation of variable degree.
- **Partial colonic obstruction** — subacute to chronic constipation, bloating, cramping, vomiting and/or abdominal distension, **while maintaining the ability to pass gas and stools**.
- **Complete colonic obstruction** — acute symptoms of abdominal distension, nausea, vomiting and/or abdominal pain, typically associated with **inability to pass stools and/or gas**. Fever, tachycardia and hypotension can also occur if ischemia, gangrene, perforation or sepsis develops.

### The six GRADE recommendations (all conditional; the wiki supplies the numbering, the document does not)

1. **Potentially resectable CRC.** In patients with colonic obstruction from highly suspected or confirmed CRC that appears surgically resectable, the ASGE **suggests endoscopic placement of a colonic stent, when feasible, as a bridge to surgery (BTS) over emergency surgery without stent placement**. *Conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence.*
2. **Unresectable CRC.** In patients with colonic obstruction from highly suspected or confirmed CRC deemed not curable by surgical resection, the ASGE **suggests endoscopic placement of a palliative colonic stent over palliative surgery**. In patients currently receiving or who may receive **bevacizumab**, the ASGE **suggests shared decision-making with surgeons, oncologists and patients** when choosing between a colonic stent and palliative surgery. *Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.*
3. **Extracolonic malignancy.** In patients with colonic obstruction from extracolonic malignancy, the ASGE **suggests guiding the decision between stent placement or surgery based on the specific tumor biology and multidisciplinary discussion**. Stent placement is feasible in this setting; however, it has a **higher risk of clinical and technical failure than intraluminal malignancy**, and this should be openly discussed with patients. *Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.*
4. **Stent type.** In patients with colonic obstruction from CRC undergoing endoscopic placement of a colonic stent, the ASGE **suggests using uncovered self-expandable metal stents (SEMSs) over covered SEMSs**. *Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.*
5. **Benign stricture — stent vs dilation.** In patients with symptomatic benign noninflammatory colonic stricture, the ASGE **suggests the use of endoscopic balloon dilation (EBD) over endoscopic stent placement as a first-line therapy**. Endoscopic stent placement with **fully covered metal stents** may be considered for benign colonic strictures that **failed EBD** after multidisciplinary evaluation. In patients with **anastomotic colonic strictures of <15-mm length that are refractory to EBD**, the ASGE suggests the use of **lumen-apposing metal stents (LAMSs)**. *Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.*
6. **Benign stricture — stricturotomy vs dilation.** In patients with symptomatic benign noninflammatory colonic strictures, the ASGE **suggests using EBD over endoscopic stricturotomy (ES) as first-line therapy**. ES **can be considered for short (<3 cm) benign colonic strictures refractory to EBD when technical expertise is available**. *Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.*

The panel added that ES should be considered when **(1)** the length is <3 cm, **(2)** the stricture is refractory to EBD, and **(3)** technical expertise is available.

### Important considerations the panel attached to the recommendations

- **Cancer recurrence after BTS** has a **fragility index of 2** and very low certainty (variable follow-up and radiologic surveillance). It was **not** associated with higher 3-year mortality. Proposed mechanism: microperforation during stent placement with lymphovascular seeding of tumor cells. The panel directs that this be **discussed with the patient** and contrasted against the significant reduction in permanent stoma.
- **Stent-related perforation** was deliberately **excluded from the GRADE outcomes** (it has no counterpart in the emergency-surgery arm) but informed the recommendations: 13% overall perforation in one RCT (9% stent-related, 4% procedure-related), with microperforation detected intraoperatively in 7%; 7.5% in a meta-analysis of 4086 patients, most in the first few days after insertion.
- **Right-sided stent placement as BTS is technically more challenging and less feasible than left-sided**, and the RCTs excluded right-sided obstruction. A meta-analysis of 7 observational studies (n=5136) found BTS associated with lower postoperative adverse events and mortality there.
- **Histopathologic diagnosis before stenting is preferred but often unavailable** — all included RCTs allowed the decision to be guided by degree of radiologic suspicion. **Placement without histopathologic diagnosis is accepted.**
- **Synchronous lesions:** decompression by stent creates the chance for a preoperative staging workup that emergency surgery forecloses — complete colonoscopy through the SEMS, or CT/MR colonography.
- **Bevacizumab:** an older meta-analysis put colonic stent–related perforation at **12.7% with bevacizumab vs 7% without**; more recent observational studies show similar risk with or without. The debate is unresolved, hence the shared-decision recommendation.
- **Covered stents are not FDA-approved or commercially available in the US** for the colon — most data are from abroad or off-label use. Uncovered stents' advantage is lower migration (longer patency, fewer reinterventions), but they can become **permanently embedded by tissue ingrowth** — so uncovered SEMSs should be **reserved for confirmed or highly suspected malignancy** and used cautiously otherwise. If the malignancy is potentially curable with chemotherapy or radiation (e.g. lymphoma), an uncovered SEMS is **not** a good option because tissue ingrowth makes removal difficult.
- **Crohn's disease:** in the one RCT comparing SEMSs and EBD in predominantly fibrotic Crohn's disease, fully covered SEMSs had **lower clinical success at 1 year (51% vs 80%, P=.006)** and similar adverse events (8% vs 5%); stent migration occurred in **97%**. Technical success was higher with SEMSs (92% vs 76%, P<.05).
- **Anastomotic stricture:** the single study comparing LAMSs and EBD in benign left-sided colorectal anastomotic strictures found **no significant difference** in technical success, clinical success, stricture recurrence or adverse events; 89.7% of strictures were ≤10 mm. LAMS migration was **46%**.
- **Diverticulitis-related stricture:** in a systematic review of case series (n=66), SEMSs were used as BTS in 40, with a **52% rate of adverse events** (perforation, reobstruction, migration) but **43% avoided a stoma**. In a retrospective study of 21 patients, all BTS patients had segmental resection with primary anastomosis at a median of 1 month and **none required a stoma**.
- **Inflammatory colorectal stricture:** evidence comparing EBD and SEMSs is **absent**. With active inflammatory bowel disease and a colorectal stricture, **medical therapy is mandatory**. For acute diverticulitis-related stricture, SEMS is feasible as BTS or as palliation if the patient is not a surgical candidate; **surgery should be performed within 7 to 10 days of stent placement** as migration and adverse-event risk increases afterward. **Avoid colonic SEMS if there is a pericolic abscess.**
- **Costs:** 2024 Medicare data — flexible sigmoidoscopy with balloon dilation **$612–$1124**; with stent placement **$4016–$5430**. No formal cost-effectiveness studies exist.

### Best practice advice — initial assessment (ungraded, panel consensus)

- **Preprocedural:** focus on symptoms and signs suggesting the need for **emergency surgery** — diffuse abdominal tenderness with peritoneal signs, hemodynamic instability, and/or laboratory findings consistent with sepsis and lactic acidosis. Cross-sectional imaging with **contrast-enhanced CT or MRI of the abdomen and pelvis** to assess site of obstruction, degree of upstream bowel dilation and suspected etiology.
- **Stricture characteristics** to record: number, location, length, severity, and the presence of an associated mass, inflammation and abscess.
- **Location classification:** **left** = from the rectum to the splenic flexure; **right** = between the cecum and the transverse colon.
- **Factors that reduce technical success:** long stricture length, right-sided location, sharp angulation, multifocality, deep ulceration, associated inflammation.
- **Surgical consultation should be obtained in all cases** of partial and complete colonic obstruction, for multidisciplinary management.
- **Contraindications to colonoscopy** — signs of impending perforation such as pneumatosis coli and bowel wall ischemia, or perforation with free air; these suggest the need for emergency surgery.
- **Preparation and sedation:** water enemas if signs of complete obstruction; full oral bowel preparation may be administered in partial obstruction. **Monitored anesthesia care or moderate sedation suffices for most partial obstruction; general anesthesia is strongly preferred for complete obstruction or critically ill patients.**
- **Avoid air insufflation; advance predominantly using waterjet** with minimal carbon dioxide insufflation — introducing gas proximal to the stricture creates a "closed-loop" system at risk of proximal perforation. **Fluoroscopy is necessary** if stent placement is planned and helpful for EBD of severe strictures.
- **Endoscopic assessment:** photo documentation is **mandatory**. Determine whether the stricture can be traversed; if not and fluoroscopy is available, an extraction balloon catheter with contrast injection delineates it. Estimate diameter, length and location including **distance from the anal verge**, and characterize as fibrotic, inflammatory, ulcerated, anastomotic, or associated with a mass or diverticulum.
- **Biopsies:** with no prior diagnosis of cancer, inspect with white-light high-definition endoscopy and virtual chromoendoscopy; take **about 6 targeted biopsy specimens** from any area suspicious for neoplasm or related to inflammatory bowel disease. **Anastomotic strictures** — biopsies are generally not helpful unless there is endoscopic concern for disease recurrence. **Diverticular strictures — avoid biopsies inside the diverticulum because of the risk of perforation.**
- **Tattooing:** consider for suspected or confirmed malignancy if a colonic stent is not placed, or for symptomatic strictures of any etiology if endoscopic treatment is not feasible. **No tattoo in the cecum or distal rectum.** **Carbon black is the preferred permanent tattoo.** Place **3 to 5 cm distal to the lesion**, in **2 to 4 quadrants** nearly touching each other. For a traversable long stricture, consider a **proximal tattoo** at similar distance and quadrant number. Confine the tattoo to the **submucosal layer**: insert the needle tangentially, lift toward the center of the lumen, inject saline or tattoo first to confirm submucosal position by a **submucosal bleb**, then inject **0.75 to 1 mL of carbon black** per area. The common error is placing the needle too deep and injecting into the peritoneal cavity or extracolonic tissue. Document tattoo location in the report **and support it with an endoscopic photograph**.

### Best practice advice — procedural technique (ungraded, panel consensus)

**Endoscopic balloon dilation**

- Consent should include the **likely need for multiple procedures over time**.
- Initial balloon diameter guided by the **estimated initial diameter of the stricture**; balloon length usually **5.5 to 8 cm**. If the stricture is longer than the longest available balloon, **overlapping serial dilation** may be needed.
- Traversable stricture: cross with the endoscope first, introduce the balloon sheath into the lumen, withdraw the endoscope until the balloon is positioned across the stricture. Not traversable but upstream colon visible: pass the balloon sheath with or without a wire. Neither: **wire passage under endoscopic — ideally fluoroscopic — guidance first**, then pass the balloon over the wire. If the balloon is longer than the stricture, **center it**.
- Inflate with water; add contrast if using fluoroscopy. Duration is **not standardized**; **30 to 60 seconds** is common practice. Hold **back-tension** on the catheter during inflation to prevent proximal migration.
- Multi-size balloons may be upsized to the next diameter **if there was no tension at the prior diameter**. If there was tension, or the maximal diameter is reached, deflate and **inspect the mucosa for tears, bleeding, perforation or stricture improvement**.
- No evidence of treatment effect (no tear, no stricture improvement) → additional EBD with upsized diameters until a treatment effect can be **safely** reached. Initially non-traversable → attempt to traverse and **document whether the stricture was traversable at the end of the procedure**.
- **Treatment goal: dilation up to 18 to 20 mm**, often not attainable at the first procedure. Repeat in **1 to 4 weeks** — **1 to 2 weeks for severe strictures**, **3 to 4 weeks for mild to moderate** ones.

**Endoscopic SEMS placement**

- Technical success **96%** and clinical success **99%** for malignant colonic stricture; for extracolonic malignancy stricture, technical **89%** and clinical **74%**; for benign stricture, technical **94%** and clinical **88%**.
- **Overall adverse-event risk 24%** with colonic SEMS for malignant obstruction: stent-induced perforation 5%, procedure-related 3%, migration 7%, occlusion 8%, ulceration 2%. **Adverse events are higher with benign strictures, with perforation risk 18%.**
- **Relative contraindication:** rectal stricture with its distal end within **5 cm of the anal verge** — risk of pain and incontinence. **Absolute: signs of ischemia, perforation or peritonitis.**
- Equipment: extraction balloon catheter, long wire (0.035″ or 0.025″), fluoroscopy, water-soluble contrast, and an endoscope compatible with the stent deployment system. **Through-the-scope SEMSs are ideal in the colon.**
- Diameter options **18 to 30 mm**, chosen on the healthy lumen diameter; **stent length chosen to leave at least 2 cm on each side of the stricture**.
- Deployment: advance a wire across the stricture and coil it in the upstream colon; advance the stent across and deploy under fluoroscopy with the **proximal end at least 2 cm proximal to the stricture**; deploy the distal end under endoscopic view, **at least 2 cm from the stricture end and at least 2 cm upstream of the anal verge** to reduce tenesmus and pain.
- **Immediate stool passage through the stent confirms transluminal placement. Balloon dilation of the stent is unnecessary and may increase the risk of perforation.**
- Afterwards: diet advanced as tolerated to a maximum of **low residue**, and **laxatives titrated to keep stools soft**.

**LAMS placement**

- For colonic strictures, **diameters of 15 and 20 mm and lengths of 10 and 15 mm** are the ones used.
- **Rarely possible in the right-sided colon** — insufficient catheter length for use with adult colonoscopes. Equipment: therapeutic gastroscope, long wire, possibly fluoroscopy.
- **Distal flange at least 2 cm from the anal verge.** Balloon dilation of the LAMS to its maximal diameter can be considered after deployment for immediate symptom relief.
- Because these stents are fully covered, **removal or exchange can be performed at 2 to 3 months**. Diet and stool-softener advice is the same as for colonic SEMSs.

**Stricturotomy**

- Opening or removing strictured tissue by **electroincision**, using radial, horizontal and/or circumferential cuts. **Not standardized; requires significant training, experience, and surgical backup.** Prestricturotomy dilation can be considered.
- Devices: needle-knife catheter or insulated tip knives suitable for endoscopic submucosal dissection. Electrosurgical generators set to **cutting settings with a monopolar high-frequency, 2-stage cutting cycle followed by a coagulation cycle**.
- Best suited to strictures **shorter than 3 cm, refractory to EBD, nonangulated and fibrotic** — such as those caused by inflammatory bowel disease, postsurgical anastomosis, or rarely extensive endoscopic resection.

### Sustainability best practices (ASGE Sustainable Endoscopy Task Force)

1. Avoid low-yield procedures and duplicative testing where possible — e.g. combine diagnostic and therapeutic colonoscopy where possible, and avoid taking biopsy samples of an anastomotic stricture if it will not change management.
2. Avoid stents in scenarios without a clear established clinical benefit — e.g. multifocal colonic obstruction from extracolonic malignancy, and initial management of benign colonic stricture when dilation is a viable option.
3. Minimize waste from endoscopic stents, which are costly and carry a substantial carbon footprint — confirm correct size or length before opening, and adopt inventory practices that reduce expired products.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Against [[aga-2021-malignant-alimentary-tract-obstruction]] on palliation (tier-1 vs tier-1).** AGA 2021 BPA 12 holds that **"either SEMS placement or a diverting colostomy are reasonable choices"** for the non-resection candidate — explicitly declining to pick. ASGE 2026 **suggests the palliative colonic stent over palliative surgery** (conditional, very low). ASGE 2026 is newer by publication date and is a GRADE guideline rather than an ungraded CPU, so it **governs [[colonic-stenting]]**. The AGA position and the reason the question was close are kept on the page.
- **Against AGA 2021 on bevacizumab.** The wiki currently carries AGA 2021's statement that bevacizumab has **not** been associated with higher perforation rates. ASGE 2026 reports the older meta-analysis figure of **12.7% vs 7%**, notes that more recent observational studies show similar risk either way, calls the association **still debated**, and responds with a **shared decision-making recommendation** rather than a reassurance. The newer source's unresolved-risk framing governs; both are stated.
- **Agreement, now graded:** uncovered over covered SEMS in the colon — AGA 2021 said the same thing ungraded; ASGE 2026 makes it a conditional recommendation.
- **Fragility.** Two headline results have a **fragility index of 2** (cancer recurrence after BTS; recurrence advantage of stricturotomy). Neither should be carried as a settled effect.
- **Open:** no evidence compares EBD and SEMSs for **inflammatory** colorectal stricture; no studies compare stent with surgery for MCO-ECM; no formal cost-effectiveness study exists for any of these choices; long-term disease-specific survival after BTS is unresolved.
