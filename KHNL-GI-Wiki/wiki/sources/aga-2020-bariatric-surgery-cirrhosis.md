## Summary

Obesity now affects a large share of patients with cirrhosis (reported prevalence ~30%, similar to the general population), and it is an independent driver of decompensation, [[portal-vein-thrombosis|portal vein thrombosis]], [[portal-hypertension|portal hypertension]], [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|HCC]], [[acute-on-chronic-liver-failure|acute-on-chronic liver failure]], and worse [[liver-transplantation|liver transplant]] outcomes. Weight loss is therefore a therapeutic goal, not a cosmetic one — but the method and pace must be individualized, because the same patient may carry sarcopenia, edema/ascites, and clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH) that BMI does not capture.

Lifestyle-based weight loss works when it is achieved — ≥10% total body weight loss is associated with fibrosis regression and with declines in HVPG — but durability in routine practice is poor, and muscle wasting must be anticipated while restricting calories. When lifestyle fails, bariatric surgery is the most effective and durable modality, and the update supports offering it to **selected patients with compensated cirrhosis at high-volume centers by experienced surgeons**, after extrahepatic comorbidities are addressed. The operative mortality signal is stage-dependent: in the Nationwide Inpatient Sample, mortality was 0.3% without cirrhosis, 0.9% in compensated cirrhosis, and 16.3% in decompensated cirrhosis.

Preoperative evaluation centers on two things the general bariatric pathway does not screen for: **CSPH** (cross-sectional imaging plus upper endoscopy, since noninvasive CSPH criteria are not validated in this population) and **alcohol use disorder** (AUD rises after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, and peak blood alcohol concentration roughly doubles after both RYGB and sleeve gastrectomy). Laparoscopic **sleeve gastrectomy** is the likely procedure of choice because it preserves endoscopic access to the stomach and biliary tree, avoids malabsorption, and produces gradual weight loss. Timing follows the stage of disease: in decompensated disease the only acceptable option is bariatric surgery concurrent with or after liver transplantation.

Endoscopic bariatric therapies (intragastric balloon, percutaneous gastric aspiration system, endoscopic sleeve gastrectomy) are less invasive but unproven here, and CSPH — specifically gastric and/or esophageal varices — is a contraindication to all endoscopic devices.

## Best Practice Advice Statements (Table 2 — verbatim, 10 statements, ungraded)

**Target audience:** Hepatologists, gastroenterologists, liver transplant surgeons, bariatric surgeons, anesthesiologists, critical care physicians, nutrition experts, and other subspecialists seeing patients with obesity and cirrhosis.

**Target population:** Obese patients with cirrhosis undergoing bariatric surgery, liver transplantation, or both.

**Baseline risk factors for surgical morbidity and mortality:** CTP score, MELD score, ASA physical status classification, BMI, visceral fat, sarcopenia, portal hypertension, centers performing low-volume surgeries, age, and non-hepatic comorbidities prevalent in this patient population (cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, renal impairment, and restrictive lung disease).

**Postoperative complications common in these patients:** Hepatic decompensation, protein calorie malnutrition, portal vein thrombosis, malabsorption of nutrients, vitamins, and trace metals, impaired absorption of vital medications including immunosuppressive agents, potential inability to endoscopically evaluate the biliary tree or endoscopically access portal hypertensive gastropathy or varices in the excluded gastric remnant, and the onset of alcohol use disorders.

1. Because obesity in patients with cirrhosis is a major risk factor for hepatic decompensation, portal vein thrombosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and the development of acute on chronic liver disease, weight loss should be an important therapeutic goal for these patients.
2. The method and rapidity for obtaining a sustained loss of excess body fat in obese patients with cirrhosis need to be individualized and are dependent not only on the BMI but also the presence and degree of sarcopenia, edema/ascites, CSPH, whether the patient has compensated or decompensated cirrhosis, patient age, and potential candidacy for liver transplantation.
3. Weight management, ideally ≥10% total body weight loss, via lifestyle modification may decrease portal hypertension and histologic progression; however, success in implementing these interventions in clinical practice and uncertainties regarding durability of this approach limit the utility of this method for treatment of obese patients with cirrhosis.
4. Bariatric surgery should be considered in selected patients with compensated cirrhosis in an effort to reduce risk for hepatocellular carcinoma and improve survival.
5. Bariatric surgery in obese patients with cirrhosis should only be performed in those with compensated disease by an experienced surgeon at a high-volume bariatric center. Bariatric surgery in this patient population should only be performed after careful evaluation and management of extrahepatic comorbidities.
6. Assessment for CSPH should be included in the preoperative evaluation for bariatric surgery in patients with cirrhosis. Pending data to validate noninvasive testing for this purpose, cross-sectional imaging and upper endoscopy should be performed to evaluate features of CSPH.
7. Bariatric surgery may be associated with significant changes in alcohol drinking habits and deleterious changes in alcohol metabolism. The consequences of alcohol consumption after bariatric surgery among patients with cirrhosis warrant intensive assessment of these candidates preoperatively and long-term efforts to mitigate risk after surgery.
8. Currently approved endoscopic bariatric therapies include the intragastric balloon, a percutaneous gastric aspiration system, and endoscopic sleeve gastrectomy. Endoscopic bariatric therapies may have lower risk(s) compared with surgical approaches, although direct comparative studies to support this, as well as long-term efficacy data, are currently lacking. Endoscopic bariatric therapies should not be performed in patients with CSPH.
9. Programs offering bariatric surgical services for patients with cirrhosis must include a surgical and anesthesia team with experience in operating on patients with portal hypertension and cirrhosis as well as a medical team with experience in treating a postoperative patient with cirrhosis. Potential candidacy for liver transplantation should be determined as part of the preoperative assessment of obese patients with cirrhosis.
10. Because of preservation of endoscopic access to the biliary tree, gradual weight loss, and absence of malabsorption, the optimal bariatric surgical procedure for patients with cirrhosis is most likely a laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy. The optimal timing is determined by the stage of liver disease. In decompensated liver disease, the only acceptable option at present is bariatric surgery concurrent with or after liver transplant.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Epidemiology and risk**
- Age-adjusted US adult prevalence: obesity (BMI >30 kg/m²) **42.2%**; severe obesity (BMI >40 kg/m²) **9.2%**.
- Reported prevalence of obesity among patients with cirrhosis: **~30%**.
- Transplant recipients with Class I (BMI ≥30 kg/m²) / Class II (BMI ≥35 kg/m²) obesity: **39% and 15% in 2017** vs **21% and 12% in 2007**.
- Clinical decompensation rates by weight category in cirrhosis: **14% normal weight, 31% overweight, 43% obese**.
- Obesity is no longer an absolute contraindication to LT, but is associated with higher waitlist mortality, increased postoperative complications, and possibly altered long-term survival after LT.
- **Sarcopenia occurs in 40%–70% of cirrhotic patients**; sarcopenic obesity is associated with higher mortality, poor outcomes after LT, recurrent HCC, and perioperative complications. CT measurement of the **psoas muscle at the level of the third lumbar vertebra** is the most common assessment method; muscle and adipose tissue can be quantified from routine HCC surveillance exams.

**Lifestyle/medical weight loss**
- Meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (mostly NAFLD populations): **≥3% weight loss → improvement in hepatic steatosis in 52%; ≥5% → improved ballooning inflammation; ≥7% → NASH resolution; ≥10% → decreased fibrosis.**
- SportDiet study — 16-week intensive diet and exercise intervention in cirrhosis: safe, achieved durable (6-month) weight loss of **5% in 52%** and **10% in 16%**, with reduction in portal hypertension.
- Prospective cohort of cirrhotic patients (mean BMI 40 kg/m²): lifestyle intervention achieved and maintained **mean BMI 33 kg/m² before LT**; 3 years after LT, **22%** maintained BMI <35 kg/m².
- Per EASL guidelines, for cirrhotic patients with **BMI >30 kg/m² (corrected for water retention)**: nutritional and lifestyle changes to achieve **progressive weight loss of >5%–10%**, using a **modest tailored hypocaloric diet (restriction of 500–800 kcal/day)** with **protein intake ≥1.5 g/kg per ideal weight/day**; avoid periods of starvation; frequent meals and a nighttime snack are recommended.
- Obesity clinical practice guidelines emphasize **caution in considering weight-loss medications in patients with liver disease**; preliminary data for liraglutide in NASH with advanced fibrosis appear promising, but the role of pharmacotherapy-assisted weight loss in cirrhosis is not established.

**Bariatric surgery outcomes in cirrhosis**
- NASH serial-biopsy study (mean interval 4.6 years): fibrosis regression in **63.2% of patients achieving ≥10% weight loss vs 9.1%** (*P* = .001) and in **47.4% of those undergoing bariatric surgery vs 4.5%** (*P* = .003).
- Weight loss decreases HVPG, with greater declines among those with ≥10% weight loss (HVPG 3.7% ± 19.9% vs −8.2% ± 16.6%; *P* = .024); HVPG declined in 39%.
- HCC risk rises with obesity severity — **39% increase in HCC risk per 5-unit increase in BMI** in NAFLD in one meta-analysis.
- Propensity-matched cohort (baseline BMI 47 kg/m² surgical vs 46 kg/m² non-surgical, median follow-up 7.1 years): fewer incident HCC after bariatric surgery (**0.05% vs 0.34%**, *P* = .03).
- US Nationwide Inpatient Sample 2004–2014, morbid obesity discharges (n = 2,881,414), propensity-matched: prior bariatric surgery (n = 267,082) associated with lower HCC prevalence (**OR 0.11; 95% CI 0.03–0.48; *P* < .001**) and lower in-hospital mortality (**0.1% vs 0.5%; OR 0.22; 95% CI 0.20–0.26; *P* < .001**).
- Nationwide Inpatient Sample: mortality risk of bariatric surgery **slightly higher in compensated cirrhosis than without cirrhosis (0.9% vs 0.3%)** but **significantly higher in decompensated cirrhosis (16.3%)**.

**Portal hypertension evaluation**
- Evaluation for CSPH is advised before **any elective surgical procedure** in cirrhosis. Portal hypertension may be assessed via transjugular pressure measurement (HVPG), imaging, esophagogastroduodenoscopy, endoscopic ultrasound, and magnetic resonance or ultrasound-based elastography.
- **HVPG <10 mm Hg is an accepted threshold for safe segmental liver resection in cirrhosis**; absent validated bariatric-specific data, adopting similar preoperative candidate criteria is prudent.
- Baveno VI support for avoiding endoscopic variceal screening based on elastography and platelet count **has not been validated in bariatric surgical candidates**.
- Meta-analysis of liver resection for HCC: CSPH associated with significantly higher postoperative mortality, complications, liver-related morbidity, liver failure, and poorer overall survival.
- Small series report feasibility of bariatric surgery in patients with CSPH with low rates of postoperative mortality or portal hypertension complications; performance should nonetheless be **restricted to selected medical centers** with experience and resources to manage complications.

**Alcohol use**
- LABS-2 consortium: prevalence of AUD (symptoms of alcohol dependence or **AUDIT score ≥8**) increased over time after **RYGB from ~7% before surgery to 16% at year 7**, while remaining stable after **laparoscopic adjustable gastric banding between 6% and 8%**.
- Meta-analysis: no increase in AUD at 2 years after bariatric surgery; at **3 years, pooled OR 1.825 (95% CI 1.53–2.178; *P* < .001)**, particularly with RYGB.
- Reported risk factors for postoperative AUD: regular or problematic alcohol use before surgery, male gender, younger age, tobacco or recreational drug use, lower interpersonal support, and history of RYGB.
- Blood alcohol concentration (BAC) after RYGB and sleeve gastrectomy vs presurgical controls: **time to peak BAC significantly shorter, peak BAC approximately doubled**, and subjective intoxication increased after both procedures — a **2-fold increase in BAC per drink equivalent**, so the threshold for clinically significant liver injury in preoperative advanced fibrosis is likely quite low.
- Patient recall and retention of education about alcohol metabolism and post-bariatric risks is suboptimal → reiterative education is required.

**Endoscopic bariatric therapies**
- Endoscopic procedures generally produce **less weight loss than bariatric surgery** but have a **lower reported complication rate and shorter recovery time**; no direct comparative studies.
- **CSPH — specifically gastric and/or esophageal varices — is a contraindication for the use of all endoscopic devices.**
- Intragastric balloons occupy space in the stomach, inhibit intake, reduce gastric motility, and are **typically removed after 6 months**; meta-analysis showed **25.4% excess body weight loss at 6 months after removal**; long-term data lacking and **weight regain is expected**.
- FDA-approved **liquid-filled balloons (Orbera; ReShape Integrated Dual Balloon System)** list **hepatic insufficiency or cirrhosis and esophageal or gastric varices as contraindications**; deaths due to gastric perforation have been reported. The **Obalon Balloon system (air-filled)** lists **gastric varices, but not cirrhosis**, as a contraindication for placement.
- Published case series from India: intragastric balloon in **8 cirrhotic patients awaiting LT — 5 underwent LT** after weight loss with the device.
- Paired-biopsy series of **20 non-cirrhotic patients** with intragastric balloon: improved NASH activity scores in the majority; fibrosis improved in only **3**, unchanged in **12**, worsened in **5**.

**Programmatic criteria**
- MBSAQIP (Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery Accreditation and Quality Improvement Program) standards require: a bariatric surgeon meeting specific training and annual case-volume requirements (**50 cases annually for comprehensive bariatric centers**), specialized anesthesia protocols, and a multidisciplinary team including registered dieticians, licensed behavioral health providers, and physical therapists; centers of excellence must also have access to consultative services from cardiology, nephrology, and pulmonology.
- Beyond those standards, cirrhosis programs need surgical and anesthesia teams experienced in operating on patients with portal hypertension and cirrhosis, plus a medical team experienced in postoperative cirrhosis care.
- **LT candidacy must be determined in advance of bariatric surgery**, so that patients ineligible for transplant understand this before deterioration; if the bariatric procedure is performed at a non-transplant center, consultation with a hepatologist with transplant expertise is advised at a minimum, and **incidentally discovered cirrhosis at the time of bariatric surgery should prompt transfer to a center with appropriate resources for cirrhosis care** if intraoperative or postoperative liver-related complications occur.

**Procedure choice and timing**
- **Adjustable gastric band** is now much less commonly performed (reduced long-term efficacy; safety concerns related to band erosion and displacement).
- **Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy** is the likely optimal procedure: preserves endoscopic access to the stomach and biliary tree, does not induce malabsorption, and produces more gradual weight loss — beneficial in cirrhosis and LT.
- UCSF series — **32 patients with previously decompensated cirrhosis (compensated at the time of SG), median MELD 12**: no perioperative deaths and no liver-related morbidity; 7 improved, 14 underwent LT, 3 transferred to other centers, 1 was ineligible for LT.
- Single-center analysis — **78 patients with cirrhosis and prior bariatric surgery** evaluated for LT vs **156 matched patients** (age, MELD, underlying liver disease) without prior surgery: **higher risk of delisting and death on the list with prior bariatric surgery (33% vs 10%; *P* = .03)**; those with prior **RYGB** were more likely to be delisted and had more sarcopenia.
- Decompensated cirrhosis: options include **SG concurrent with or after transplant**. Mayo series of 7 combined **LT + SG** patients vs patients who reduced BMI <35 kg/m² without surgery and underwent LT alone — LT + SG attained and maintained significant weight loss with improved metabolic parameters, while the non-bariatric group had more weight gain after LT; hospital stay and complication rates were similar. A larger series with 3-year outcomes showed durable weight loss, favorable metabolic profiles, and excellent graft and patient outcomes for LT + SG.
- Multiple small case series of **SG after transplant** reported acceptable outcomes but **longer operating room times and increased complication rates**.
- Advantage of combined LT + SG: one procedure and one recovery; disadvantage: adapting to the challenges of LT and bariatric surgery simultaneously. **Risk of the combined approach vs sequential operations is unknown**; patient preference should also be considered.

**Figure 1 — algorithm for class II (BMI 35–39.9 kg/m²) and class III (BMI ≥40 kg/m²) obesity in individuals with cirrhosis**
- Start: cirrhosis with class II/III obesity → **goal weight loss ≥10%** → specialized nutrition management and activity protocol for gradual weight loss while maintaining muscle mass. If achieved → continue program for maintenance.
- If weight-loss goal not achieved, branch by stage:
  - **Compensated cirrhosis** → **HVPG ≥10 mm Hg, or EGD + cross-sectional imaging with features of portal hypertension?**
    - **Yes** → assess LT candidacy.
    - **No** → assess for sarcopenia. Sarcopenia present → continue specialized nutrition/activity protocol. Absent → assess and manage comorbidities and risk for alcohol use → **laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy in a bariatric program with appropriate resources**, with reiterative interventions to avoid alcohol use following surgery.
  - **Decompensated cirrhosis** → assess LT candidacy.
  - **LT candidate? Yes** → consider bariatric surgery concurrent with or following liver transplantation. **No** → continue specialized nutrition management and activity protocol.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Bariatric surgery in compensated cirrhosis — permissive vs restrictive framing.** [[aasld-2023-nafld|AASLD 2023]] calls decompensated cirrhosis an absolute contraindication and restricts compensated NASH cirrhosis to high-volume centers or the transplant context; this update agrees on the restriction but goes further, actively recommending that surgery **be considered** in selected compensated patients to reduce HCC risk and improve survival (statement 4). [[acg-2025-perioperative-cirrhosis|ACG 2025]] (newer, guideline-tier) likewise states bariatric surgery can be safely performed in selected well-compensated patients with laparoscopic SG as the procedure of choice — so the three are aligned in direction; the ACG 2025 statement governs where wording differs.
- **CSPH work-up — endoscopy vs noninvasive criteria.** This update requires cross-sectional imaging **and** upper endoscopy and explicitly rejects Baveno VI's elastography-plus-platelet rule-out as unvalidated in bariatric candidates. [[acg-2025-perioperative-cirrhosis|ACG 2025]] instead endorses LSM + platelet count to rule in CSPH before surgery (conditional, very low quality), plus cross-sectional imaging. Newer guideline-tier source wins on the wiki; the 2020 caution that noninvasive criteria were never validated specifically in bariatric candidates is preserved.
- **Open questions named by the source:** no randomized data on bariatric surgery in cirrhosis; long-term data on whether weight-loss–induced improvement in fibrosis and portal hypertension changes liver-related morbidity/mortality are unavailable; the risk of combined LT + SG vs sequential operations is unknown; direct comparative and long-term efficacy data for endoscopic bariatric therapies are lacking; pharmacotherapy-assisted weight loss in cirrhosis is unestablished; validation of the pathophysiologically plausible HCC and survival benefit of bariatric surgery in this population is described as an unmet need.
