## Summary

- Problem: "laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR)" is used both as a symptom label and a diagnosis, often without objective reflux evidence
  - LPR accounts for up to 10% of otolaryngology and gastroenterology visits; health care costs 5.4× those of gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) — partly from prolonged acid-suppression trials and diagnostic uncertainty
  - Up to 60% of patients labelled "LPR" have normal ambulatory reflux monitoring
  - Life-threatening long-term sequelae (e.g., cancer) are infrequent
- Core reframe: **laryngopharyngeal symptoms (LPS)** = symptom descriptor; **laryngopharyngeal reflux disease (LPRD)** = LPS **plus** objective evidence linking symptoms to reflux. LPS ≠ LPRD; ~35% of evaluated LPS patients have objective LPRD.
- Algorithm splits early on **concurrent esophageal reflux symptoms** (heartburn, regurgitation, chest pain) vs **isolated LPS** (Figure 1)
  - With esophageal symptoms: empiric trial (twice-daily proton pump inhibitor [PPI] × 3 months ± alginate) + lifestyle
  - Isolated LPS, non-response, or before invasive anti-reflux therapy: upper endoscopy and ambulatory reflux monitoring
- Laryngeal hyperresponsiveness and hypervigilance contribute to LPS with or without LPRD → laryngeal recalibration therapy (LRT), cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), neuromodulators
- Methods: October 2023–October 2024; six sub-groups (definition/terminology, initial diagnostic evaluation, reflux monitoring, therapeutic trial, behavioral factors/therapy, risk stratification); ≥2 rounds of RAND voting; statements rated appropriate (score ≥7) with **≥80% agreement** became recommendations
- Reflux *management* recommendations are explicitly outside the scope
- Limitations (authors): agreement based on mostly observational evidence; RAND without GRADE reflects the quality of available literature

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definitions (Table 2 glossary)

- **LPS:** chronic, frequent throat and upper-airway symptoms with potential to be induced by retrograde flow of gastric content to the upper esophagus, pharynx, larynx
  - **Frequent** = ≥2×/week; **chronic** = ≥8 weeks
  - Agreed LPS (agreement %): cough (89%), regurgitation (89%), throat pain/sore throat (89%), throat clearing (86%), excess phlegm (82%), hoarseness/voice change (80%)
  - Globus: approached but did not reach agreement as an LPS; still warrants evaluation; often a *negative* predictor of reflux physiology
  - Did not reach agreement as LPS: tongue burning, nasal burning, post-nasal drip, halitosis, belching, dysphagia
- **LPRD:** chronic troublesome LPS **AND** objective evidence supporting a relationship between symptoms and gastroesophageal reflux
- **Objective evidence supporting LPRD:** LPS AND either
  1. concurrent esophageal reflux-like symptoms with LPS that respond to an anti-reflux trial, **or**
  2. diagnostic evidence of reflux on upper endoscopy or ambulatory reflux monitoring
- **Diagnostic evidence of gastroesophageal reflux:** Los Angeles (LA) grade B, C, or D esophagitis and/or biopsy-proven Barrett's esophagus on endoscopy, and/or distal esophageal acid exposure time (AET) >6.0% on monitoring off acid suppression
- **Empiric anti-reflux trial:** PPI twice daily (omeprazole 20 mg twice daily equivalent) ± alginate × 3 months
- **Invasive anti-reflux therapy:** endoscopic or surgical — e.g., endoscopic fundoplication, surgical fundoplication, magnetic sphincter augmentation
- **Laryngeal hyperresponse:** repetitive laryngeal behaviors (throat clearing, coughing, breath-holding) or vocal-fold strain altering voice
- **Laryngeal hypervigilance:** increased threat-induced attention to the larynx; often with symptom-specific anxiety (fear/worry about LPS and its consequences)
- **LRT:** structured therapy combining traditional speech-language pathologist (SLP) techniques with heart-rate-variability biofeedback and acceptance and commitment therapy

### Laryngoscopy

- Necessary to assess non-reflux otolaryngologic processes incl. malignancy; cannot diagnose LPRD alone
- White-light findings often attributed to reflux — infraglottic edema, interarytenoid mucosal hypertrophy, erythema, post-cricoid edema — are non-specific; also seen with other laryngeal inflammation and in healthy volunteers
- Benign vocal-fold lesions and epithelial lesions (keratotic, pre-malignant dysplastic, malignant) are common and can mimic "LPR" (dysphonia, throat clearing, globus)
- Hoarseness unexplained by white-light flexible laryngoscopy → videostroboscopy (symmetry, amplitude, mucosal wave, glottic closure)
- Reflux Finding Score (RFS) and Reflux Sign Assessment (RSA): suboptimal specificity → not diagnostic
- Vocal process granuloma (spontaneous, not intubation-related): historically called pathognomonic for LPRD, but uncertain — surveyed laryngologists ranked phonotrauma (88%) > reflux (67%) > glottic insufficiency (48%)
- Other non-reflux causes recognizable on laryngoscopy: allergy, glottic pathology, rarely neoplasia

### Empiric medical trial (LPS + esophageal reflux symptoms)

- Standard-dose PPI (e.g., omeprazole 20 mg) **twice daily × 3 months** is adequate to judge LPS response
- Alginate four times daily (after meals and at bedtime): reduced LPS vs vocal hygiene/lifestyle; can augment acid suppression; inexpensive, well tolerated, may reduce globus perception
- Potassium-competitive acid blockers (PCABs): approved for non-erosive reflux disease and erosive esophagitis; could be used in place of PPI; LPS outcome data pending
- Lifestyle:
  - ↓ intragastric pressure — weight loss/management, avoid tight abdominal clothing
  - ↓ post-prandial reflux — stay upright / avoid recumbency 2–3 h after meals; diaphragmatic breathing
  - Nocturnal reflux — head-of-bed elevation, left lateral sleep
  - Avoid trigger foods
- Isolated LPS: acid-reducing drugs not recommended first-line without diagnostic evidence of reflux
- Fewer than 50% of LPS patients respond to anti-secretory therapy; anti-reflux surgery often yields suboptimal outcomes (mechanisms other than reflux)

### Upper GI endoscopy

- Upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy ideally performed off antisecretory therapy in unproven GERD
- Conclusive GERD (Lyon Consensus paradigm): LA grade B–D esophagitis and/or biopsy-proven Barrett's esophagus (Table 3 also lists peptic stricture)
- Disrupted esophagogastric junction (EGJ) (e.g., hiatal hernia; Hill grade, AFS grade) = supportive/adjunctive only
- Multicenter LPS study: up to 47% had erosive findings and/or hiatal hernia — in both isolated LPS and LPS with esophageal symptoms
- Office-based transnasal esophagoscopy (TNE): similar diagnostic performance for Barrett's esophagus; should be done by providers with specialized esophageal-endoscopy training
- Cervical inlet patch (heterotopic gastric mucosa, proximal esophagus; may secrete acid, mucus, pepsin): associated with LPS and globus; ablation reduced LPS in small case series; **no agreement** on value of finding/treating it; not diagnostic of GERD/LPRD
- No endoscopic GERD evidence → wireless pH capsule can be placed during the same endoscopy (cost-effective)

### Ambulatory reflux monitoring

- Reference standard for LPRD when endoscopy shows no GERD evidence
- Absent GERD makes reflux an unlikely mechanism and anti-reflux therapy unlikely to help; GERD present does not prove it causes the LPS
- Off acid suppression in unproven GERD
- Also next step for LPS + esophageal symptoms not responding to anti-secretory and/or alginate trials; consider before long-term acid suppression
- Distal AET thresholds (both modalities): **>6.0% = proven GERD**; **<4.0% = physiologic**, especially if <4.0% on all 4 days of a 96-hour study

**pH-impedance (24-hour)**

- Detects proximal reflux, weakly acidic and non-acidic events, symptom association; baseline impedance (mucosal integrity; little LPS data)
- Hypopharyngeal-esophageal multichannel intraluminal impedance with dual pH (HEMII-pH): pharyngeal reflux events found in 81% of 42 patients with suspected reflux-mediated LPS, incl. 72% negative by esophageal-only criteria
- **No agreement** on >80 esophageal or >10 hypopharyngeal events as abnormal thresholds
- Near (not full) agreement that HEMII-pH helps when standard monitoring is inconclusive and suspicion of reflux mechanism stays high
- Best for *mechanism* (reflux-mediated vs reflex), especially isolated LPS
- Limits: no day-to-day variability; suboptimal for ruling out reflux disease

**Prolonged wireless pH (96-hour)**

- Single distal pH sensor placed at sedated endoscopy; captures day-to-day variability
- Outperforms 24- or 48-hour studies for predicting need for acid-suppressive and anti-reflux therapy
- Double-blind multicenter trial, 100 patients with troublesome *esophageal* reflux symptoms and inadequate PPI response: 0 days elevated AET → 10× odds of stopping PPI vs 4 days elevated; AET elevated ≥2 days → odds ratio (OR) 5.3 (95% confidence interval [CI] 2.19–13.44) for needing to continue PPI
- Recent guidance (Lyon 2.0) positions 96-hour wireless pH off therapy as preferred test for unproven GERD
- Multicenter study, 813 patients referred for LPS: GERD yield 50% on prolonged wireless vs 27% on 24-hour pH-impedance (incl. HEMII-pH)
- Better tolerated than catheter; main adverse effect chest discomfort, rarely needing removal
- Limits: no proximal/pharyngeal data, no baseline impedance, cannot directly ascribe LPS to reflux

**Oropharyngeal pH (Restech Dx-pH)**

- Not specific; misses weakly acidic/non-acidic reflux; poor concordance with pH-impedance; no correlation with outcomes → not supported as stand-alone tool

### Laryngeal hyperresponsiveness, hypervigilance, behavioral therapy

- Laryngeal hyperresponse (throat clearing, coughing, breath-holding, vocal-fold strain, globus) and hypervigilance/symptom-specific anxiety may contribute to or be the primary cause of LPS — with or without LPRD
- Survey of 1,217 adults: 16–46% self-identified as "laryngoresponsive"
- Laryngeal Cognitive Affective Tool (LCAT): validated patient-reported outcome (PRO) instrument identifying elevated hypervigilance/anxiety and likely benefit from behavioral therapy
- LRT: by voice-specialized SLP; voice techniques + vagal / heart-rate-variability biofeedback + cognitive interventions; precedent in confirmed reflux, isolated cough, inducible laryngeal obstruction / paradoxical vocal fold movement, muscle tension dysphonia
- CBT: for persisting psychological drivers or incomplete relief after LRT or neuromodulation; targets symptom-specific cognitive, emotional, physiological, behavioral processes (not general mental-health disorders); delivered by a mental health professional with health-psychology training
- Laryngeal involvement does not imply a mental health disorder (Figure 3: cycle of laryngeal hypersensitivity → hyperresponsiveness → hypervigilance/anxiety)

### Neuromodulation

- Consider in LPRD with suboptimal response to anti-secretory therapy or SLP intervention, and in isolated LPS with negative esophageal testing
- Gabapentin, pregabalin (delta ligands): evidence mainly in chronic cough (vagal hypersensitivity)
- Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs): use with caution — anticholinergic effects (dry mouth) may aggravate LPS
- Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) / serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs): little data; consider with concomitant anxiety/depression

### PRO instruments and risk tools

- GERD PROs (GERDQ, GERD-HRQL): useful for esophageal symptom severity/response; suboptimal diagnostic yield for LPS/LPRD
- Reflux Symptom Index (RSI): non-specific; can follow laryngeal complaints over time; the previously used cutoff **≥13** did not distinguish increased proximal/pharyngeal reflux or symptom correlation on HEMII-pH
- Reflux Symptom Score (RSS): higher sensitivity/specificity for proximal reflux burden and treatment outcome, but not definitively diagnostic
- Voice Handicap Index-10 (VHI), Leicester Cough Questionnaire (LCQ), Cough Severity Index (CSI): PROs only, not diagnostic
- HAS-BEER, COuGH RefluX risk scores: promising, need more validation before adoption into the algorithm

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Response to an empiric trial as evidence:** San Diego 2025 (Table 2) accepts LPS + esophageal reflux symptoms responding to an anti-reflux trial as objective evidence supporting LPRD. [[aga-2023-extraesophageal-gerd|AGA 2023 extraesophageal GERD]] Best Practice Advice 5: improvement on PPI may reflect non-acid mechanisms and should not be regarded as confirmation of GERD. Both tier 1; San Diego 2025 is newer and is what [[laryngopharyngeal-symptoms]] follows — the LPS page notes the AGA caveat.
- **PPI trial dose and duration:** San Diego 2025 — twice daily × 3 months from the start. AGA 2023 — single-dose PPI, titrating to twice daily, one trial up to 12 weeks. [[aga-2018-extraesophageal-gerd|AGA 2018]] — aggressive acid suppression (twice daily) for 6–8 weeks. San Diego 2025 is newest.
- **Endoscopic threshold, internal inconsistency:** Table 1 statement reads ">LA grade B"; the text, Table 2, and Table 3 all say LA grade B, C, or D. The text/tables (LA B–D, consistent with [[lyon-2024-gerd-diagnosis|Lyon 2.0]]) are used on the wiki.
- **Globus, internal inconsistency:** the text says globus did not reach agreement as an LPS; Table 1 lists "Globus is a laryngopharyngeal symptom that warrants evaluation and management" at 88%. Wiki: not an agreed LPS, but warrants evaluation.
- **Event-count thresholds:** Table 3 lists >80 distal and >10 proximal reflux events as *supportive* findings, yet the panel did not agree on >80 esophageal / >10 hypopharyngeal events as abnormal cut-offs.
- Inlet patch: no agreement on value of identifying or ablating it; controlled ablation data needed.
- PCAB outcomes in LPS not yet studied; LRT evidence mainly non-randomized; HAS-BEER and COuGH RefluX need validation.
