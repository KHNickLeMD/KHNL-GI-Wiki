## Summary

ECCO's guideline on extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs) of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) — **32 numbered consensus statements** (several with lettered sub-parts: 2.1–2.2, 5.1–5.3, 6.1–6.4), each carrying an **evidence level EL1–EL5** and a **consensus percentage** from the voting round. The document deliberately covers both the classical EIMs and the systems a gastroenterologist is expected to monitor rather than treat.

Its organising framework divides EIMs into **four categories**: **Classical** (an inflammatory process occurring at a distant site), **Associations** (other immune-mediated disorders that co-occur), **Complications** (consequences of systemic inflammation), and **Treatment** (side effects of IBD therapy). The distinction matters because it predicts whether controlling the bowel disease will control the manifestation.

Content spans thromboembolism and cardiovascular risk, anaemia (the most detailed section, with explicit diagnostic criteria, monitoring intervals and retreatment thresholds), pulmonary disease, hepatobiliary disease including primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) and autoimmune hepatitis (AIH), pancreatitis, metabolic bone disease, axial and peripheral spondyloarthropathy, cutaneous manifestations, ear-nose-throat, ocular manifestations, neurological manifestations, and fatigue.

This is a **European** guideline. Where a United States society guideline already covers the same question — [[aga-2024-ida-management|AGA 2024]] on iron deficiency anaemia, [[aasld-2022-psc|AASLD 2022]] on PSC, [[aasld-2020-autoimmune-hepatitis|AASLD 2020]] on AIH — the US document governs the wiki and this one adds only the IBD-specific detail it uniquely supplies.

### Statements 1–4 — Thromboembolism and cardiovascular disease

1. **Thrombotic events are more than twice as frequent** in patients with IBD than in the general population, with **similar prevalence in ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD)** *[EL2]*. The most important risk factors are **active disease, hospitalization, and surgery** *[EL2]*. *[consensus 100%]*
2.1. **Prophylactic-dose low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) or fondaparinux is recommended during hospitalization for acute medical illness or major surgery** *[EL1]*. After a **major surgical procedure**, prophylactic-dose LMWH should continue **until at least 3 weeks post-discharge** *[EL3]*. For post-hospitalization patients or ambulatory patients with a **severe IBD flare, thromboprophylaxis can be considered** *[EL4]*. *[consensus 100%]* — in-hospital pharmacological thromboprophylaxis is associated with a **54% decreased risk** of venous thromboembolism (VTE) after discharge; LMWH is recommended over unfractionated heparin in acutely and critically ill patients.
2.2. **Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) at therapeutic dose are recommended** for IBD patients presenting with an acute VTE *[EL1]*. **Risk factors for VTE should be investigated to guide duration of anticoagulation** *[EL1]*. *[consensus 100%]* — LMWH is an alternative in the acute phase; DOACs may be of particular benefit in **portal or cerebral venous thrombosis**, with a **fourfold greater chance of complete radiological resolution** and better recanalization than warfarin.
3. **Marginally increased risk** of **cerebrovascular accident** *[EL2]*, **ischaemic heart disease** *[EL2]*, **mesenteric ischaemia** *[EL4]*, **atrial fibrillation** *[EL3]* and **heart failure** *[EL3]* versus the general population, **independent of IBD therapy**. **No difference in cardiovascular mortality** *[EL2]*. *[consensus 94%]* — risk more pronounced in CD, in women, and at younger age; data on peripheral arterial disease are conflicting.
4. **Small increased risk of myocarditis and pericarditis**, although the overall incidence of both in IBD is **low** *[EL4]*. *[consensus 97%]* — absolute myocarditis risk **8.3 per 100 000 patient-years in CD** and **2.6 per 100 000 patient-years in UC**. Myocarditis has been associated with **mesalazine** and **tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) antagonists**; **pericarditis is almost universally driven by therapy**, including TNFα antagonists, with complete resolution after withdrawal.

### Statements 5.1–6.4 — Anaemia

5.1. **Anaemia is one of the most common EIMs of IBD.** The **World Health Organization (WHO) definitions of anaemia apply** to patients with IBD. **Iron deficiency and anaemia of chronic disease are the most frequent types**, alone or with other micronutrient deficiencies *[EL4]*. *[consensus 100%]* — WHO cut-offs: haemoglobin **<130 g/L for adult men**, **<120 g/L for adult non-pregnant women**, **<110 g/L for children aged 6–59 months** (rising with age). Reported anaemia prevalence in IBD ranges **6–74%**.
5.2. **Assess regularly for anaemia.** Anaemia parameters should be evaluated **every 6–12 months in remission or mild disease activity**, and **at least every 3 months in active disease** *[EL5]*. **In the presence of biochemical or clinical evidence of inflammation, the diagnostic criteria for anaemia of chronic disease are serum ferritin >100 µg/L and transferrin saturation <16%. A serum ferritin between 30 and 100 µg/L makes a combination of true iron deficiency and anaemia of chronic disease likely** *[EL2]*. *[consensus 100%]* — a **normal mean corpuscular volume does not exclude iron deficiency** (up to **40%** of pure iron-deficiency anaemia cases are normocytic).
5.3. Patients at risk for **vitamin B12 or folate deficiency** — especially **small-bowel CD or resection**, and patients with **macrocytosis** — should have serum B12 and folate **screened at least annually** *[EL4]*. *[consensus 95%]*
6.1. Patients with IBD and iron deficiency anaemia should receive **iron supplementation to normalize haemoglobin levels and iron stores** *[EL1]*. *[consensus 97%]* — treatment of **non-anaemic iron deficiency** is advisable, factoring in clinical status and patient preference.
6.2. **Intravenous iron is recommended first-line** in patients with **clinically active IBD**, **previous intolerance to oral iron**, and those who need **erythropoiesis-stimulating agents** *[EL1]*. **Oral iron is recommended in mild iron deficiency anaemia whose disease is clinically inactive** *[EL1]*. *[consensus 100%]* — when oral is chosen, monitor the haemoglobin response over the **first 4 weeks** and continue for **~3 months after haemoglobin normalizes** to replete stores.
6.3. After treatment, **monitor haemoglobin and ferritin every 3–6 months for at least a year** after deficiency restoration, then **every 6–12 months** *[EL4]*. **Re-treat when ferritin drops below 100 µg/L, or haemoglobin below 12 or 13 g/dL depending on sex** *[EL2]*. The goal of preventive treatment is to keep haemoglobin and serum ferritin within the normal range *[EL3]*. *[consensus 95%]* — after effective repletion, anaemia **recurs in ~50% within 10 months**.
6.4. **Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents can be considered** in patients with inadequate response to intravenous iron, with a **target haemoglobin not above 12 g/dL**, provided IBD-related therapy has been optimized *[EL5]*. *[consensus 95%]*

### Statement 7 — Pulmonary

7. **Increased risk of bronchiectasis, interstitial lung disease, and granulomatous lung disease** in IBD *[EL4]*. *[consensus 95%]* — data for other pulmonary conditions such as asthma are conflicting.

### Statements 8–12 — Hepatobiliary

8. **Alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, γ-glutamyltransferase and total serum bilirubin should be measured in the treatment-naive patient with suspected IBD, then at 6-month intervals throughout follow-up** *[EL4]*. *[consensus 97%]* — adults with IBD carry an estimated **5% risk of an immune-related liver disease** (PSC, AIH, or both), more common in children; **one-third** of all IBD patients have nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, **twice** the prevalence in healthy controls.
9. Patients with IBD, **especially UC, are at increased risk of PSC**; **prevalence of PSC in IBD is 2–8%**. In IBD patients with **persistent elevation of cholestatic liver enzymes and symptoms of cholestasis such as pruritus, or prominent perihilar lymphadenopathy, PSC should be investigated with high-quality magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)** *[EL2]*. *[consensus 100%]* — PSC may progress to cirrhosis and end-stage liver disease within **12–20 years**; it raises colorectal cancer risk (**hazard ratio 2.4**) and cholangiocarcinoma risk **up to 400-fold** versus the general population.
10. **Ursodeoxycholic acid may improve liver biochemistry in PSC but has no demonstrated impact on disease progression** *[EL1]*. *[consensus 100%]* — at **15–20 mg/kg/day** it improves biochemistry but not fatigue, pruritus, cholangiocarcinoma risk or mortality. Its effect on colorectal cancer risk is controversial: a **medium–low dose (<25 mg/kg/day)** was associated with reduced neoplasia risk in some studies, a higher dose with higher risk and more adverse events in others, and no difference in still others. Vedolizumab, adalimumab, infliximab, antibiotics and antifibrotics have **no demonstrated benefit** in PSC.
11. **Patients with PSC-IBD should be referred to a hepatologist with expertise in PSC at diagnosis, and joint care should continue thereafter** *[EL5]*. *[consensus 100%]*
12. **The prevalence of AIH in adult patients with IBD is less than 0.5%**, with higher rates in children and adolescents *[EL2]* *[consensus 100%]*. In people with **AIH and IBD, additional sclerosing cholangitis should be investigated using high-quality MRCP** *[EL3]* *[consensus 84%]*. **For maintenance of remission in people with IBD and AIH, thiopurines should be considered first-line** *[EL2]* *[consensus 85%]*. — in adolescents with IBD the odds ratio for AIH was **8 in UC** and **4 in CD**; **2–7%** of AIH patients have a concurrent PSC diagnosis and **up to 10%** of adult AIH patients show concurrent features of PSC.

### Statement 13 — Pancreatitis

13. **Acute pancreatitis in IBD is commonly associated with gallstone disease, alcohol intake, medications (thiopurines and 5-aminosalicylates), and duodenal CD. Autoimmune pancreatitis may also occur** *[EL3]*. *[consensus 90%]* — pooled annual incidence of acute pancreatitis in IBD **210 per 100 000 patient-years**; autoimmune pancreatitis prevalence **three times higher** in IBD (odds ratio 3.11, 95% confidence interval 2.93–3.30), and higher in CD than UC (odds ratio **4.12 vs 2.61**). ⚠ **Asymptomatic increases in serum amylase, lipase, or both occur in 8–21% of IBD patients** — a diagnostic trap, since recurrent abdominal pain is itself common in IBD.

### Statements 14–15 — Metabolic bone disease

14. Patients with IBD have an **increased risk of developing osteoporosis and osteoporotic fractures, particularly of the spine** *[EL2]*. **In patients with IBD at high risk for osteoporosis, bone mineral density measurement through a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scan is recommended** *[EL2]*. *[consensus 100%]* — by WHO criteria a **T-score below −2.5 standard deviations (SD) is diagnostic of osteoporosis** and a **T-score between −1 and −2.5 SD of osteopenia**. Meta-analysis: **32% increased risk** of osteoporosis or osteoporotic fracture versus controls (odds ratio 1.32, 95% confidence interval 1.20–1.45); incidence odds ratio **2.9 in CD** (95% confidence interval 2.4–4.1) and **2.8 in UC** (2.1–3.9); **two- and three-fold** increased risk of spinal and hip fracture respectively. Risk factors for low bone mineral density include systemic inflammation, frequent corticosteroid use, low body mass index, CD diagnosis, smoking, malabsorption of vitamins D and K and calcium, malnutrition, reduced physical activity, and genetic factors.
15. **Appropriate daily dietary intake of calcium and vitamin D, smoking cessation, and physical activity should be promoted to prevent osteopenia and osteoporosis** *[EL5]*. **Supplementation with calcium and vitamin D is recommended in patients treated with corticosteroids and/or with known osteopenia or risk factors for low bone mineral density** *[EL2]*. **Treatment with bisphosphonates is recommended in patients with corticosteroid-induced or postmenopausal osteoporosis** *[EL1]*. *[consensus 84%]*

### Statements 16–19 — Joints

16. **Clinical symptoms of joint disease in IBD are common**, such as **peripheral and axial spondyloarthropathy** *[EL2]*. **Articular symptoms may occur before and after the diagnosis of IBD** *[EL2]*. *[consensus 100%]* — the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis Society (ASAS) criteria distinguish **axial** (back pain, morning spinal stiffness) from **peripheral** (upper and lower limb) spondyloarthritis; ASAS includes IBD as a classification criterion, with a reported **lifetime IBD risk of 4–14%** in spondyloarthritis.
17. **There is no evidence of an association between non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) use and UC flare** *[EL1]*, **although there is potentially an association with CD flare** *[EL2]*. **The decision to use NSAIDs for arthropathy is made on a case-by-case basis** *[EL3]*. **Selective cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) inhibitors may be used for short periods of time** *[EL2]*. *[consensus 91%]* — a meta-analysis of 13 studies and 6276 participants found no consistent association (CD relative risk 1.42, 95% confidence interval 0.65–3.09; UC relative risk 1.52, 95% confidence interval 0.87–2.63).
18. **TNFα antagonists are recommended for treatment of axial spondyloarthropathy associated with IBD. Vedolizumab and ustekinumab are not recommended in axial spondyloarthropathy associated with IBD** *[EL2]*. *[consensus 96%]* — no randomized controlled trial has evaluated any biologic, Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitor or NSAID in this setting; four open-label trials (100 patients total) all showed a decrease in spondyloarthritis with TNFα antagonists. ⚠ **Etanercept causes paradoxical gastrointestinal inflammation in a subset of patients and should be avoided in the IBD population.** JAK inhibitors may also be considered given their efficacy in ankylosing spondylitis.
19. **TNFα antagonists are recommended for treatment of IBD-associated non-axial spondyloarthropathy** *[EL2]*. **There are also data to support use of methotrexate, sulfasalazine, and ustekinumab** *[EL3]*. *[consensus 100%]* — this guidance addresses peripheral arthritis **as an enteroarthropathy**, not as a coexisting rheumatological diagnosis such as rheumatoid or psoriatic arthropathy.

### Statements 20–26 — Skin and orofacial

20. **Skin diseases associated with IBD are among the most common EIMs**, and their relationship with the underlying intestinal disease may be **specific** (metastatic CD), **reactive** (pyoderma gangrenosum, Sweet syndrome, erythema nodosum, oral lesions), **associated** (hidradenitis suppurativa, psoriasis), or **treatment-related** (TNFα antagonist-induced skin lesions, other drug hypersensitivities, skin cancer) *[EL3]*. **Early involvement of a dermatologist is recommended when appropriate.** *[consensus 95%]* — approximately **15–20%** of patients with IBD will develop cutaneous EIMs.
21. **Erythema nodosum is very often associated with underlying active intestinal IBD**, although other causes should be excluded and managed. **When erythema nodosum is associated with IBD activity, the primary aim is control of underlying intestinal disease activity; the skin lesions usually resolve when intestinal disease activity is controlled** *[EL3]*. *[consensus 100%]* — prevalence up to 15% (**CD 4–15%; UC 2.8–10%**), the most common skin disorder in IBD; painful erythematous-violaceous subcutaneous plaques or nodules **usually 1–5 cm**, symmetrically on **extensor surfaces of the lower extremities (anterior tibia)**, also thighs and forearms. **Skin biopsy is needed only if the presentation is atypical.**
22. **TNFα antagonists, particularly infliximab, are recommended for pyoderma gangrenosum** *[EL2]*. **Early use should be considered, particularly for severe manifestations.** Other options that may be considered: **systemic steroids** *[EL3]*, **ciclosporin** *[EL3]*, **ustekinumab** *[EL4]*, **dapsone** *[EL3]*, **metronidazole** *[EL3]*, **tetracyclines** *[EL4]*. **For milder disease, topical steroids and topical calcineurin inhibitors may be considered** *[EL3]*. **Early involvement of a dermatologist is recommended.** *[consensus 95%]* — the second most common reactive cutaneous EIM and the most debilitating; prevalence in IBD **0.4% to 2.6%** across 14 studies; more common in **females, black Africans, and those with a positive family history of UC**; lesions often arise **at a site of trauma**.
23. **Systemic corticosteroids are recommended as first-line treatment for Sweet syndrome** *[EL4]*. **TNFα antagonists may be used in steroid-dependent or refractory cases** *[EL4]*. *[consensus 95%]* — diagnosis rests on sudden eruption of erythematous-to-violaceous tender papules and plaques **plus histological neutrophilic infiltration of the dermis without vasculitis**; fever, peripheral neutrophilia and raised erythrocyte sedimentation rate or C-reactive protein are typical. IBD is the **third most common** underlying condition in Sweet syndrome, after haematological malignancy and infection; **azathioprine may induce a Sweet-syndrome-like dermatosis**.
24. **Hidradenitis suppurativa is associated with IBD and is more common in CD than UC.** **Topical or systemic treatment (antibiotics and dapsone) may be used in mild-to-moderate disease. Adalimumab is recommended as first-line treatment for severe disease, with early dose intensification frequently required. Other options include infliximab, ustekinumab, or surgery** *[EL3]*. *[consensus 100%]* — ⚠ **its sinus tracts and scarring can be mistaken for perianal CD**.
25. **Metastatic CD is a rare extraintestinal manifestation affecting the skin and genital area. Systemic and intralesional corticosteroids, azathioprine, metronidazole, and TNFα antagonists can be used** *[EL3]*. *[consensus 90%]* — shares the **non-caseating granulomatous histology** of CD but occurs at sites **not contiguous** with the bowel; usually chronic penile, scrotal, vulval or perianal oedema. Differential includes sarcoidosis, tuberculosis and lymphogranuloma venereum.
26. **Orofacial manifestations of IBD are common** and include **oral aphthous ulcers, periodontitis, pyostomatitis vegetans, and orofacial granulomatosis**. In orofacial and buccal IBD, **management of the underlying IBD and supportive mouth care, including topical steroids**, is recommended *[EL5]*. *[consensus 95%]*

### Statements 27–30 — Ear, nose, throat and eye

27. **Aural and nasal manifestations in IBD are very rare and there is not sufficient evidence to recommend any specific treatment** *[EL5]*. *[consensus 94%]* — all information rests on case reports, series and expert opinion. In an ECCO CONFER project of 22 cases of concomitant **Cogan's syndrome** and IBD, immunomodulatory maintenance and even TNFα antagonists did **not** prevent disease onset, and IBD activity did not appear to trigger it.
28. **Ocular manifestations associated with IBD are relatively frequent. The most frequent is anterior uveitis.** Less frequent: **episcleritis, conjunctivitis, scleritis, optic neuritis, ischaemic optic neuropathy, other uveitis subtypes, central retinal vein occlusion, and orbital inflammation or myositis** *[EL3]*. *[consensus 100%]*
29. **The choice of therapy for IBD-associated uveitis depends on the type of uveitis and the underlying IBD activity. Topical corticosteroid drops are recommended first-line for anterior uveitis. Intravitreal or systemic corticosteroids, with or without second-line steroid-sparing agents, may be required for intermediate, posterior and panuveitis. Adalimumab or infliximab are recommended as third-line treatment when sight-threatening ocular inflammation persists and/or there is active underlying IBD** *[EL4]*. **There is currently no trial evidence regarding the efficacy of non-TNFα-antagonist biologics and small molecules for IBD-associated uveitis. Uveitis should be jointly managed by a gastroenterologist and a specialized ophthalmologist on an urgent basis.** *[consensus 97%]* — uveitis/iritis prevalence **11.1% (205/1840)** in the prospective Swiss IBD Cohort Study.
30. **Early use of oral NSAIDs and consideration of systemic corticosteroids is recommended for scleritis. Steroid-sparing agents, including TNFα antagonists, may be used in refractory cases or when corticosteroid response is incomplete** *[EL4]*. **Topical lubricants and cool compresses are recommended for episcleritis; topical NSAIDs and corticosteroids should be considered if symptoms persist despite controlling IBD** *[EL4]*. ⚠ **Patients with symptoms suggestive of scleritis, in particular eye pain preventing sleep, should be urgently referred to an ophthalmologist**, as correct classification and management impact severity and prognosis *[EL4]*. *[consensus 100%]* — scleritis occurs in **<1%** of IBD cases (incidence ~2.8 per 100 000 person-years); CD carries an over three-fold (odds ratio 3.6, *p* < 0.001) and UC an over two-fold (odds ratio 2.2, *p* < 0.001) increased risk.

### Statements 31–32 — Neurological and fatigue

31. **Central nervous system manifestations may be more common in IBD patients than in the general population** *[EL4]*, including **venous sinus thrombosis, stroke, and central demyelination**. ⚠ **In patients with IBD and central demyelination, TNFα antagonists are contraindicated** *[EL4]*. *[consensus 98%]* **IBD is rarely associated with peripheral neuropathies** *[EL4]*; **treatable causes should be identified**. Central neuropathies have only been described in a case series *[EL5]* and **drug adverse effects should be considered**. *[consensus 100%]* — reported prevalence of neurological manifestations varies **0.2% to 36%**; a known **bidirectional association with multiple sclerosis** reflects shared genetic susceptibility.
32. **Fatigue is more frequent and more severe in patients with IBD than in the general population; prevalence ranges from 40–60% in inactive to mild disease to over 80% in active disease.** There is evidence for **fatigue as an independent entity** — a prevalence of 40% has been reported in patients in remission. **Fatigue negatively impacts health-related quality of life, independent of disease activity or anaemia** *[EL4]*. *[consensus 96%]* — fatigue is more severe in CD than UC and is the most common reason for work absence in IBD.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **NSAIDs in IBD.** ECCO 2024 finds **no** evidence of an association between NSAID use and UC flare *[EL1]* and only a potential association with CD flare, recommending case-by-case decisions and permitting short-course COX-2 inhibitors. This is a notably more permissive stance than the blanket NSAID avoidance often taught; ECCO's own meta-analysis (13 studies, 6276 participants) found neither relative risk reached significance.
- **Ursodeoxycholic acid dose in PSC-IBD.** The document reports directly conflicting data on colorectal-cancer risk — medium–low dose (<25 mg/kg/day) associated with **reduced** neoplasia risk in some studies, higher dose with **higher** risk and more adverse events in others, and no difference in still others — and does not resolve it.
- **Myocarditis attribution.** Whether the increased myocarditis risk is driven by IBD itself or by mesalazine and TNFα antagonists **remains undetermined on the available data**; pericarditis, by contrast, is described as almost universally therapy-driven.
- **European versus US guidance.** This is an ECCO document. On iron deficiency anaemia, PSC and AIH the wiki follows [[aga-2024-ida-management|AGA 2024]], [[aasld-2022-psc|AASLD 2022]] and [[aasld-2020-autoimmune-hepatitis|AASLD 2020]] respectively; ECCO's contribution on those pages is restricted to the IBD-specific criteria and intervals those documents do not supply.
