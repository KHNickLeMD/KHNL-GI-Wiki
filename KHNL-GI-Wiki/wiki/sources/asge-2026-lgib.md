## Summary

ASGE Standards of Practice guideline on endoscopy in acute lower gastrointestinal bleeding (LGIB), using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. **It supersedes [[asge-2014-lgib]]** as the ASGE position. Five questions were answered through full GRADE; three more (timing of upper endoscopy, when to consider surgery, modality by lesion type) were answered by systematic evidence review **without** GRADE and carry **no strength or certainty rating** — the document marks those rows "N/A" rather than inventing grades.

The guideline's central move is that **CT with contrast ± angiography and colonoscopy are both acceptable as the initial modality**, split by hemodynamics: colonoscopy for patients stable on presentation or after resuscitation, contrast CT ± angiography for those with **ongoing** hemodynamic instability. The panel deliberately **declined to define "stable" by any score or criterion**, holding that no single criterion or validated score can replace clinical judgment, and that neither transfusion requirement nor ongoing clinical signs of bleeding should by itself select the modality.

On timing it reverses the usual framing: **colonoscopy should be performed when the patient is stable enough and able to tolerate a full bowel preparation, regardless of timing**. Meta-analysis found no 30-day mortality or rebleeding difference between urgent and nonurgent colonoscopy; in the sensitivity analysis restricted to the 24-hour cutoff, **rebleeding was higher** with urgent colonoscopy (OR 3.05, 95% CI 1.23–7.56), while hospital stay was ~1 day shorter. Preparation is the one **strong** recommendation in the document: full bowel preparation unless a distal source or an iatrogenic bleed of known location is strongly suspected.

For diverticular bleeding the panel splits by **colonic side**: band ligation where feasible for transverse and more proximal lesions, clipping for distal lesions — the banding benefit was driven entirely by right-sided lesions, with no apparent benefit on the left. Where clipping is chosen, **direct clipping of the visualized vessel is suggested over indirect (orifice-closing) clipping**, particularly in the proximal colon. Both recommendations carry heavy caveats: essentially all the evidence is Asian and observational, and colonoscope-compatible banding devices are not readily available in North America.

### Figure 1 — clinical decision-making algorithm
Suspected LGIB (hematochezia) → stabilize/resuscitate, transfuse to target, **exclude upper GI bleeding** → split:
- **Severe LGIB** (hemodynamic instability **despite** standard initial resuscitation) → **CT/angiogram** → **contrast extravasation** → **urgent transcatheter angiography with embolization**; if unavailable/unsuccessful and bleeding ongoing, consider urgent colonoscopy with expedited purgative prep. **No contrast extravasation** → colonoscopy with full bowel preparation (consider expedited prep if severe).
- **Non-severe LGIB** (hemodynamically stable, including after initial resuscitation) → **endoscopic evaluation** (or **consider expectant management**, e.g. stable bleeding with a recent high-quality colonoscopy showing only diverticulosis) → **unknown source/location** → colonoscopy with full bowel preparation; **strongly suspected distal or iatrogenic source** → **colonoscopy or sigmoidoscopy, unprepped or with enema(s)**.
- Both arms → **treat lesion if found**, with the endoscopic modality tailored to the lesion.
- Figure footnotes: the **timing of endoscopic evaluation for non-severe LGIB has not been associated with improved outcomes**; examples of a strongly suspected distal/iatrogenic source are postpolypectomy, post-endoscopic submucosal dissection or post-EMR bleeding, malignancy of known location, known left-sided diverticular disease, and rectal trauma.

### Initial assessment
- **Risk scores:** the **Oakland** and **SHA₂PE** scores were developed specifically to identify patients for whom discharge from acute care and outpatient investigation are safe. They are "undoubtedly useful" but **should never replace clinical judgment**.
- **Resuscitation:** hemodynamically unstable patients should be observed and treated in a **monitored setting**, resuscitated with IV crystalloid to normalize heart rate and blood pressure **ideally before any further diagnostic or therapeutic modality**; **2 large-bore IV catheters** (or equivalent).
- **Transfusion:** restrictive strategy with a **hemoglobin threshold of 7 g/L** [as printed] for most patients without established cardiovascular disease; a more liberal strategy may be appropriate with a history of cardiovascular disease or LGIB amid acute coronary syndrome, though the evidence is not clear enough to define an optimal strategy in that population.
- **Yield of upper endoscopy:** in an RCT of 85 patients with suspected LGIB (hematochezia without hematemesis) **and hemodynamic instability**, an upper GI source was found in **15%** when upper endoscopy was performed within 6 hours — peptic ulcers and esophagogastric varices the most common. Retrospective yield of subsequent upper endoscopy after a negative colonoscopy: **11%–22%**.
- **Other risk factors for an upper source** in a patient with hematochezia: **male sex, known peptic ulcer disease or cirrhosis, blood urea nitrogen–to-creatinine ratio >30**.
- **Urgent or emergent capsule endoscopy** is an alternative approach to upper endoscopy in stable patients with hematochezia, with reasonable ability to identify bleeding lesions.

### Epidemiology and etiology
- Annual hospitalization for major LGIB: **20–40 per 100,000** in the US and Europe; rates substantially higher with relevant comorbidities and antithrombotic use.
- **Diverticular disease is the most common etiology, ~30% of LGIB presentations.** Anorectal pathology, colonic neoplasms and the colitides are next, together ~20% of major bleeding. Less common: iatrogenic LGIB after endoscopic or surgical intervention, and vascular lesions such as angioectasia.
- **Mortality 2%–4%**, with advanced age, coagulopathy and concomitant comorbidities carrying higher risk.
- LGIB natural history is **highly variable** — spontaneous cessation through to ongoing bleeding with instability — unlike upper GI bleeding where emergent management is frequently necessary.

### Evidence behind the recommendations
- **CT ± angiography vs colonoscopy first** (meta-analysis, 8 retrospective cohorts): CT-first patients had **higher 30-day mortality (OR 12.64, 95% CI 2.97–53.75; I² = 0%)**, higher transfusion requirement (OR 2.29, 1.56–3.35) and longer stay (SMD 2.09 days, 0.62–3.56). **No difference in diagnostic yield** (OR 1.44, 0.74–2.82) or in ability to provide initial hemostasis by either route (OR 0.77, 0.25–2.42). The panel reads the mortality signal as **selection bias**, not effect: CT-first patients were older, more comorbid, more hypotensive and more transfused.
- **Urgent vs nonurgent colonoscopy** (18 studies, 4 RCTs): no difference in 30-day mortality (OR 0.76, 0.16–3.50; 3 RCTs, 385 patients) or rebleeding (OR 1.23, 0.46–3.27; 4 RCTs, 463 patients). **Restricting to the 24-hour cutoff, rebleeding was higher with urgent colonoscopy (OR 3.05, 1.23–7.56, I² = 0%).** Urgent colonoscopy shortened stay (SMD −0.96 days, −1.46 to −0.47). No difference in need for surgery, radiologic intervention, transfusion or adverse events.
- **If colonoscopy is pursued within 24 hours**, the panel holds that a **full bowel preparation should be given** and the procedure done **in a controlled setting with the full range of endoscopic devices and techniques available**, so a lesion found can be definitively treated.
- **Band ligation vs clipping for diverticular bleeding** (7 comparative studies, 6 retrospective + 1 prospective; 2329 patients for rebleeding): banding had **lower recurrent bleeding (OR 0.33, 0.23–0.47)** and **less need for radiologic intervention (OR 0.43, 0.24–0.76)**, with a non-significant trend to fewer transfusions (OR 0.73, 0.52–1.02) and **no difference in need for surgery** (OR 1.08, 0.36–3.23). **In subgroup data the benefit was confined to right-sided lesions with no apparent benefit for left-sided**, which is why the recommendation is restricted by side. A non-significant trend toward **more adverse events with banding** (OR 2.41, 0.70–8.24); banding adverse events included **perforation — which required subsequent surgery 100% of the time** — and diverticulitis and fever, versus diverticulitis with clipping.
- **Direct vs indirect clipping** (9 comparative studies, 8 observational + 1 prospective): **direct clipping had lower recurrent bleeding (OR 0.58, 0.44–0.77)** and less need for radiologic intervention (OR 0.50, 0.25–0.97), with **no difference in initial hemostasis** (OR 1.17, 0.43–3.20), mortality, adverse events or transfusion. The panel is **more comfortable with this recommendation than with banding**, chiefly because adverse events did not differ between the two techniques.
- **Generalizability caveat, stated repeatedly:** all the diverticular-bleeding studies were carried out in **Asia**, with far higher proportions of right-sided diverticular lesions than in Western settings.

### Technique notes the document gives
- **Indirect clipping** closes the entire diverticular orifice — sometimes needing a "zipper" closure with multiple clips, as for a postpolypectomy defect. **Direct clipping** visualizes the vessel (in the dome or along the lip) and applies clips to it. **Small-mouthed diverticula make clip deployment inside a diverticulum difficult or impossible** and are a stated reason direct clipping may not be feasible.
- **Banding is technically demanding in this setting:** it requires removing the colonoscope entirely to load the bander, then reinserting to the bleeding site — hard in an angulated or redundant colon and harder amid active bleeding, and particularly difficult navigating a loaded scope to the right colon in higher-BMI patients. **Reidentify the site with a marking clip and/or a tattoo**; a tattoo has the advantage of permanence, guiding future endoscopic or surgical management. **Attempt banding only where the provider is experienced and a colonoscope-loadable banding device is available** — most devices load onto a gastroscope and are not readily available in North America.
- **Thermal modalities in diverticular bleeding:** possible but risky given thin-walled diverticular anatomy, especially at the **base**. Minimize risk by applying at the **rim** rather than the base and by using **lower power and shorter duration of coagulation**.
- **APC for vascular lesions:** rebleeding after APC for actively bleeding and recently bleeding angioectasias was **7.4%** in a large series. **Pulsed APC modes give more superficial cauterization than continuous modes** — know the electrosurgical unit settings. In the thinner-walled right colon, **submucosal injection before APC** can further reduce perforation risk.
- **Radiation proctopathy** is treated as a vascular lesion; APC is effective. **Exception to the unprepped-distal-source rule:** radiation proctopathy, though a suspected distal source, is generally treated with APC and therefore **should still occur only after a full bowel preparation**, given the risk of **colonic explosion** with APC at higher intestinal gas levels.
- **Postresection bleeding** can occur after routine polypectomy, EMR or ESD, typically **within 14 days** but rarely up to **30 days**. Predictors of active bleeding at colonoscopy: **left-sided lesions, pedunculated lesions, anticoagulant use**. Decision modelling: repeat colonoscopy for suspected delayed postpolypectomy bleeding identified and treated a lesion in **22% of cases, number needed to treat 4.5**. Apply thermal therapy cautiously to a resection bed, particularly in the right colon; epinephrine injection is a **temporizing** measure only.
- **Malignant bleeding:** endoscopic therapy is **temporizing only** — a bridge to definitive surgery or palliation. Hemostatic powder appears to have become the mainstay; Nd:YAG laser availability is waning in Western settings.
- **Over-the-scope clipping** has not been extensively studied in acute diverticular bleeding, but early evidence appears promising. **Snare ligation** has been studied in a limited way. **Doppler probes** are validated for risk stratification in upper GI bleeding but **data are lacking to guide their use in LGIB**.

### Surgery
- The role of surgery is **much more limited than previously reported**: surgery for acute LGIB fell from **22.6% to 16.6%** between 1988–1997 and 1998–2006 in a 1112-patient cohort, and in a nationwide prospective UK cohort of 2528 consecutive patients **only 0.2% ultimately required surgery**.
- Emergency surgery for refractory LGIB (retrospective, 1614 patients): **30-day mortality 12.2%**. Older age, functional dependence and higher ASA score raised the odds of short-term mortality; a **laparoscopic approach and higher hematocrit at surgery** were associated with lower mortality.
- Rare scenarios that might prompt emergent laparotomy as the initial modality: **aortocolonic fistula, bleeding Meckel's diverticulum**.

### Nuclear medicine and sustainability
- CT availability and performance have **reduced the need for nuclear medicine evaluation**; scintigraphy is now largely limited to settings where CT or endoscopy are unavailable or inconclusive.
- **Sustainability best practices** stated by the ASGE Sustainable Endoscopy Task Force: avoid low-yield or duplicative testing (**e.g. forgo CT in stable LGIB** and involve gastroenterology early); reduce admissions, procedures and length of stay for low-risk patients using the available risk-prediction tools; and aim for **definitive endoscopic therapy at the index procedure** to avoid repeat procedures, prolonged hospitalization and readmission.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Supersedes [[asge-2014-lgib]]** (same society, newer publication) and is newer than [[acg-2023-lgib]]. Where the earlier documents urge urgent colonoscopy within 24 hours for high-risk patients, this guideline's position is that **timing is not the criterion** — stability and ability to tolerate a full preparation are. Any page asserting a 24-hour rule should carry this.
- **The panel declines to define "stable."** It rejects a strict criterion, a validated score, transfusion requirement, and ongoing clinical signs of bleeding as the discriminator, leaving the stable/unstable split to clinical judgment. This is an explicit non-definition, not an omission.
- **Open — generalizability of the diverticular recommendations.** All supporting studies are Asian, with far more right-sided lesions than in Western practice, and colonoscope-loadable banding devices are **not readily available in North America**; the panel restricts banding to right-sided lesions and to experienced providers for that reason.
- **Open — the CT-first mortality signal.** OR 12.64 for 30-day mortality is large but drawn entirely from retrospective cohorts with evident selection bias; the panel explicitly declines to treat it as causal.
- **Open — Doppler probes in LGIB**, over-the-scope clipping as primary therapy, banding learning curves, and capsule endoscopy indications are all named as evidence gaps requiring prospective study.
