## Summary

A dedicated consensus on **EUS-guided gallbladder drainage (EUS-GBD)** from the Interventional Endoscopy and Ultrasound Group (i-EUS), an Italian collaborative network, with multidisciplinary input from gastroenterology, surgery and radiology. Four task forces of four experts each covered **EUS-GBD in acute cholecystitis and fragile patients, technical aspects, postprocedural considerations, and EUS-GBD as a rescue therapy for other indications**. Clinical questions were PICO-framed, evidence was searched to **April 2024** with emphasis on meta-analyses and prospective studies, evidence was graded with **GRADE**, and consensus was defined as **≥80% agreement**; statements falling short were excluded rather than published as non-endorsements.

**Twenty-two statements** were issued across the four domains. The document's defining feature is how one-sided its certainty is: **every statement is conditional except one**. Statement 4 — that **EUS-GBD should be preferred over endoscopic transpapillary gallbladder drainage (ET-GBD) and/or percutaneous gallbladder drainage (PT-GBD) in unfit-for-surgery patients with acute cholecystitis** — is the sole **Strong** recommendation and the only one resting on **moderate**-quality evidence, with **100% agreement**. It rests principally on a single randomized trial (Teoh et al.) showing EUS-GBD with LAMS gave lower adverse-event rates, fewer recurrent cholecystitis episodes, reduced reintervention and fewer unplanned readmissions than PT-GBD, with similar technical and clinical success.

The remaining statements shape practice around that core: drain **moderate-to-severe acute cholecystitis** in unfit patients, do it **early (<48 hours)** when conservative therapy fails, use **LAMS**, prefer **≥15 mm diameter when cholecystoscopy is planned**, **do not routinely dilate the LAMS**, work in a **fluoroscopy-equipped room**, and skip **routine antibiotic prophylaxis** and **routine post-procedural medical therapy**. Several statements are explicitly negative or permissive rather than directive, and the panel repeatedly downgraded certainty because the comparisons available were indirect — a point it makes about its own output in the discussion.

### Why EUS-GBD is preferred (Statement 4)

- **The randomized evidence:** Teoh et al. — EUS-GBD with LAMS vs PT-GBD gave **lower adverse-event rates, fewer episodes of recurrent cholecystitis, reduced reintervention and fewer unplanned readmissions**, with **similar technical and clinical success**.
- A separate **multicentre RCT in 142 high-risk patients was terminated early** for significantly higher major complications with PT-GBD: **65% vs 12%** in the surgery group (RR 0.19, 95% CI 0.10–0.37; P<0.001), with **66% requiring reintervention**.
- Meta-analysis of 6 studies: comparable early adverse events but **lower delayed adverse events (OR 0.21, 0.07–0.61; P<0.01)** and **lower overall adverse events (OR 0.43, 0.30–0.61; P<0.01)** with EUS-GBD, plus shorter hospital stay.
- Subgroup limited to **EUS-GBD with LAMS** vs PT-GBD: technical success RR 0.965 (P=0.146), clinical success RR 0.967 (P=0.036), **fewer adverse events RR 0.424 (P<0.001)** and **lower reintervention RR 0.244 (P<0.001)**.
- ⚠ **A countervailing signal the panel reports rather than suppresses:** a pooled analysis by Mohan et al. found higher clinical success for EUS-GBD than ET-GBD and PT-GBD, but an **unexpectedly high all-cause mortality of 26%** vs 16.6% (ET-GBD) and 11.2% (PT-GBD), P=0.001 — attributed to **selection bias**, EUS-GBD being performed in patients with lower overall survival.
- Network meta-analysis across the three modalities: **EUS-GBD had high clinical success and the lowest recurrent cholecystitis**; **ET-GBD had the lowest reintervention, unplanned readmission and mortality**; **PT-GBD had the highest technical success but the highest subsequent interventions and hospitalizations**. **Adverse-event risk was comparable across all three.**

### Surgical-risk stratification (Statement 1)

No score is endorsed over another, but the document names the tools in use and what each was built for:
- **ASA Physical Status** and **P-POSSUM** (Portsmouth Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the enUmeration of Mortality and Morbidity) — morbidity and mortality after surgery. P-POSSUM showed the **highest postoperative predictive accuracy**, with an **optimal cutoff of 25** for identifying high-risk patients (1429 patients).
- **Charlson Comorbidity Index** — survival based on comorbidities. **APACHE** — severity of critical illness.
- **Clinical Frailty Score** — a 9-point scale of overall patient fitness, useful for deciding between laparoscopic cholecystectomy and minimally invasive drainage.
- **Age is an independent risk factor:** patients **>80 years** have significantly higher postoperative complications; elderly (65–79) and very elderly (>80) differ, the latter faring worse.
- **ACME** (Acute Cholecystitis Mortality Estimation) — chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, dementia, age >80, preoperative vasoactive amine use; **92% mortality prediction accuracy** across surgical and non-surgical candidates. **Chole-Risk** — in-hospital and 30-day mortality, major morbidity.
- Tokyo Guidelines and ASA showed **lower predictive accuracy for morbidity** in acute cholecystitis in a 344-patient study.

### Technique and device detail

- **LAMS over the alternatives (Statement 7).** Systematic review of 21 studies (166 patients): similar technical (90%–100%) and clinical success for double-pigtail plastic stents, self-expandable metal stents (SEMS) and LAMS, but **adverse events 18% / 12% / 10%** respectively. Double-pigtail plastic stents raise bile leak and peritonitis risk through small caliber and inadequate fistula sealing; SEMS seal better but **migrate and can injure the contralateral wall**; **antimigratory SEMS improved clinical success (OR 2.16, 1.07–4.36) and reduced adverse events (OR 0.36, 0.14–0.98)**. LAMS add a large-bore covered design with wide flanges and an electrocautery tip permitting **single-step deployment without tract dilation or device exchange**.
- **Diameter (Statement 8).** LAMS span 6–20 mm; 10 and 15 mm are most used. A meta-analysis of 13 studies suggested larger-diameter LAMS reduce stent obstruction from thick bilious or purulent secretions, though it did not analyse diameter specifically. A 102-patient multicentre comparison of **10 mm (n=78) vs 15 mm (n=24)** found high technical success in both with **no significant difference in clinical success or adverse events**. The **DRAC 1** trial and another study recommended **10 mm for small gallbladder stones and 15 mm for larger ones**; ESGE's technical review suggested the same without a specific recommendation. Some experts propose **15–16 mm** to guarantee endoscopic access for advanced interventions — lithotomy, gallbladder clearance, polyp removal.
- **No routine dilation (Statement 9).** LAMS do not reach full caliber immediately; the metal mesh dilates gradually over subsequent days, optimizing drainage. Immediate balloon dilation has been proposed to reduce stone impaction and is routine in other LAMS applications (pancreatic fluid collection drainage, EUS-directed transenteric ERCP), but **no dedicated study has assessed its systematic impact** and the retrospective series reporting high success did not differentiate outcomes by dilation. A long-term adverse-event study found **14% of patients who underwent immediate dilation after EUS-GBD had specific adverse events**, while a case series and a 22-patient prospective study that explicitly did **not** dilate reported technical success 95.5%–100%, clinical success 100%, and adverse events 0%–4.5%.
- **Contracted gallbladder (Statement 12):** saline injection may be attempted to obtain lumen distension.

### Other indications and interfaces

- **Concomitant choledocholithiasis (Statement 6):** choledocholithiasis is present in **~15% of AC cases**. The panel favours a **1-stage procedure (simultaneous ERCP and ET-GBD)** on low-quality evidence — similar efficacy and safety to a 2-stage approach with organizational benefit. General principles cited: cholecystitis should be managed **within 72 hours of admission**; **ET-GBD is the preferred endoscopic alternative**; ERCP is standard for clearing the duct; when **ET-GBD fails, EUS-GBD combined with ERCP** is a valid rescue with success comparable to EUS-GBD alone.
- **Conversion from PT-GBD (Statement 5):** AC recurrence after catheter removal runs **22% to 45%**. Case series converting PT-GBD to EUS-GBD report **100% technical success and up to 33% adverse events with no AC recurrence**; a 21-patient series using plastic or metal stents gave 91% technical success with 15% adverse events. A 238-patient comparative study found **conversion significantly reduced AC recurrence vs conservative management after catheter removal (5% vs 18%, P=0.012)**; the 8 EUS-GBD conversions had **100% technical and clinical success**.
- **Rescue for distal malignant biliary obstruction (Statement 19)** — after ERCP failure, when EUS-guided biliary drainage is not feasible. **Not prophylactically (Statement 20)** even with tumour across the cystic duct orifice.
- **Timing of early drainage (Statement 3):** the evidence is conflicting and indirect — of five studies comparing early vs late PT-GBD, "early" meant within 24 h in three, 48 h in one, 72 h in another; two reported fewer drainage-related complications with early intervention, three a significant reduction in hospital stay, but **two showed no clinical benefit or reduction in morbidity and mortality**. The World Society of Emergency Surgery suggests PT-GBD or EUS-GBD after 48 h in patients with persistent leukocytosis (>15,000/µL) and fever unresponsive to antibiotics at 24–48 h. The panel **downgraded to very low certainty** because no trial addresses EUS-GBD timing specifically.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Agrees with but is stronger than [[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage]].** The AGA clinical practice update positions EUS-GBD as an option in unfit-for-surgery patients; this consensus issues a **Strong recommendation preferring it over both ET-GBD and PT-GBD**, the only strong statement in the document. Being the dedicated, GRADE-assessed document on this exact question, it governs.
- **The mortality signal is unresolved.** Mohan et al.'s pooled **26% all-cause mortality** with EUS-GBD vs 16.6% and 11.2% for the alternatives is reported and attributed to selection bias — EUS-GBD being performed in sicker patients with lower overall survival — but it is **not refuted by comparative data**. The strong recommendation rests on adverse events, recurrence and reintervention, not on mortality.
- **The network meta-analysis does not crown one modality.** EUS-GBD led on recurrent cholecystitis, **ET-GBD on reintervention, readmission and mortality**, PT-GBD on technical success. Adverse-event risk was comparable across all three. The strong recommendation therefore reflects a particular weighting of those outcomes, not uniform superiority.
- **Timing (<48 h) rests on PT-GBD data.** No study specifically addresses the timing of EUS-GBD; the panel extrapolated and downgraded to very low certainty accordingly.
- **No statement on LAMS diameter resolves the stone-size question.** The 10 mm vs 15 mm comparison found no difference in clinical success or adverse events; the ≥15 mm suggestion is conditioned specifically on **planned cholecystoscopy**, not on stone burden, even though DRAC 1 and others tied diameter to stone size.
- **Statements falling below 80% were excluded, not published.** Unlike [[i-eus-2026-eus-gastroenterostomy]], which reports its five non-endorsements, this document does not disclose which questions failed to reach consensus — so the areas of genuine expert disagreement are not visible here.
