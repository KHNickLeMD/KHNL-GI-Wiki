## Summary

Joint SCCM/A.S.P.E.N. guideline for the adult (≥18 years) critically ill patient expected to stay >2 or 3 days in an intensive care unit (ICU) — medical (MICU) or surgical (SICU). Expanded from 2009 to cover organ failure (pulmonary, renal, liver), acute pancreatitis, surgical subsets (trauma, traumatic brain injury [TBI], open abdomen [OA], burns), sepsis, postoperative major surgery, chronic critical illness and critically ill obesity (pp 159–160). Definitions (p 161): **nutrition therapy** = enteral nutrition (EN) by enteral access device and/or parenteral nutrition (PN) by central venous access; **standard therapy (STD)** = intravenous (IV) fluids, no EN or PN, advance to oral diet as tolerated.

Core framework: **nutrition risk** (nutritional risk screening [NRS 2002] or NUTRIC score) decides who needs early, aggressive therapy. High-risk patients get early EN within 24–48 h advanced to >80% of goal within 48–72 h; low-risk, well-nourished patients need no specialized therapy in week 1 and should not get PN in the first 7 days. EN is preferred over PN; gastric feeding is acceptable for most, with post-pyloric feeding for high aspiration risk or gastric intolerance.

Operational thrust: feeding protocols, no routine gastric residual volume (GRV) monitoring, aspiration-reduction bundle, high-dose protein (1.2–2.0 g/kg/d), standard polymeric formula, immune-modulating formula only in selected surgical/TBI/trauma populations, glucose target 140 or 150–180 mg/dL, and disease-specific chapters (pulmonary, renal, hepatic, pancreatitis, surgery, sepsis, obesity, end of life).

Recommendations are lettered by section (A–R) and numbered within section (A1, A3a, …). **95 recommendations** in 18 sections. A 6-item bedside bundle is given in Table 2; the guideline's summary and GRADE tables are published in its online appendix.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Table 2 — Bundle statements (p 163)

- Assess on ICU admission for nutrition risk; calculate energy **and** protein requirements to set goals
- Start EN within 24–48 h of onset of critical illness and ICU admission; increase to goals over the first week
- Reduce aspiration risk / improve gastric tolerance: prokinetic, continuous infusion, chlorhexidine mouthwash, elevate head of bed, divert feeding lower in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract
- Implement EN protocols with institution-specific strategies to promote delivery
- Do not use GRVs as part of routine care to monitor ICU patients on EN
- Start PN early when EN is not feasible or sufficient in high-risk or poorly nourished patients

### A. Nutrition Assessment (pp 161–164)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | We suggest determining nutrition risk (eg, NRS 2002, NUTRIC score) on **all** ICU admissions for whom volitional intake is anticipated to be insufficient. High nutrition risk identifies those most likely to benefit from early EN | Based on expert consensus |
| A2 | We suggest nutrition assessment include comorbid conditions, GI tract function and aspiration risk. We suggest **not** using traditional nutrition indicators or surrogate markers (not validated in critical care) | Based on expert consensus |
| A3a | We suggest indirect calorimetry (IC) to determine energy requirements, when available and in the absence of variables that affect measurement accuracy | Very Low |
| A3b | Without IC, we suggest a published predictive equation or a simplistic weight-based equation (**25–30 kcal/kg/d**) to determine energy requirements (obesity: section Q) | Based on expert consensus |
| A4 | We suggest ongoing evaluation of adequacy of protein provision | Based on expert consensus |

- Risk thresholds (A1 rationale): "at risk" = NRS 2002 **>3**; "high risk" = NRS 2002 **≥5** or NUTRIC **≥5** (if interleukin-6 not included; otherwise **>6**). All hospitalized patients need an initial nutrition screen within **48 h** of admission
- Traditional serum proteins (albumin, prealbumin, transferrin, retinol-binding protein) reflect the acute-phase response, not nutrition status (A2 rationale); calcitonin, C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-1, tumor necrosis factor, interleukin-6 and citrulline remain investigational
- Predictive equations: accuracy **40%–75%** vs IC; less accurate in obese and underweight patients; after aggressive volume resuscitation or with edema/anasarca use dry or usual body weight; account for dextrose-containing fluids and propofol (A3 rationale)
- Re-evaluate energy expenditure **more than once per week** (A3 rationale)
- Protein monitoring by weight-based equations (eg, **1.2–2.0 g/kg/d**) when nitrogen balance is unavailable; serum proteins not valid for protein adequacy (A4 rationale)

### B. Initiate EN (pp 164–168)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| B1 | We **recommend** early EN within **24–48 h** in the critically ill patient unable to maintain volitional intake | Very Low |
| B2 | We suggest EN over PN in critically ill patients who require nutrition support therapy | Low to Very Low |
| B3 | We suggest that in most MICU and SICU patients, GI contractility should be evaluated when starting EN, but overt signs of contractility (bowel sounds, flatus) are **not** required before starting EN | Based on expert consensus |
| B4a | We **recommend** diverting the level of infusion lower in the GI tract in patients at high aspiration risk (section D4) or with intolerance to gastric EN | Moderate to High |
| B4b | We suggest it is acceptable to start EN in the stomach in most critically ill patients | Based on expert consensus |
| B5 | We suggest withholding EN during hemodynamic compromise or instability until fully resuscitated and/or stable; initiation/reinitiation may be considered with caution in patients undergoing withdrawal of vasopressor support | Based on expert consensus |

- Early vs delayed EN meta-analysis (21 RCTs): mortality RR **0.70** (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.49–1.00; P = .05; Figure 1); infectious morbidity RR **0.74** (0.58–0.93; Figure 2) (B1 rationale)
- EN vs PN (12 studies, 618 patients): infections RR **0.56** (0.39–0.79; Figure 3); ICU length of stay (LOS) shorter (weighted mean difference −0.82 days); no difference in hospital LOS or mortality (B2 rationale)
- GI dysfunction occurs in **30%–70%** of ICU patients (B3 rationale)
- Small bowel vs gastric (B4 rationale): nutrient delivery +11.06% (Figure 4); pneumonia RR **0.75** (0.60–0.93; Figure 5); no difference in mortality or LOS. If small-bowel access is not feasible in a timely way, gastric EN beats delaying
- Withhold EN when (B5 rationale): mean arterial pressure **<50 mm Hg**; catecholamines (norepinephrine, phenylephrine, epinephrine, dopamine) being initiated; or escalating doses needed to maintain stability. EN may be given with caution on chronic, stable low-dose vasopressors. On vasopressors, abdominal distention, rising nasogastric output or GRVs, less stool/flatus, hypoactive bowel sounds, or rising metabolic acidosis/base deficit = possible early gut ischemia → hold EN

### C. Dosing of EN (pp 168–170)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | We suggest that patients at **low** nutrition risk with normal baseline nutrition status and low disease severity (eg, NRS 2002 **≤3** or NUTRIC **≤5**) who cannot maintain volitional intake do **not** require specialized nutrition therapy over the first week in the ICU | Based on expert consensus |
| C2 | We **recommend** either trophic or full EN for patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)/acute lung injury (ALI) and those expected to need mechanical ventilation **≥72 h** — similar outcomes over the first week | High |
| C3 | We suggest patients at **high** nutrition risk (eg, NRS 2002 **≥5** or NUTRIC **≥5**, without interleukin-6) or severely malnourished be advanced toward goal as quickly as tolerated over **24–48 h**, monitoring for refeeding syndrome. Aim for **>80%** of estimated or calculated goal energy and protein within **48–72 h** to achieve the clinical benefit of EN over the first week | Based on expert consensus |
| C4 | We suggest sufficient (high-dose) protein: requirements expected at **1.2–2.0 g/kg actual body weight/d**, likely higher in burn or multitrauma patients (sections M and P) | Very Low |

- Trophic feeds: usually **10–20 mL/h or 10–20 kcal/h** (C3 rationale); in the ARDS/ALI trials **10–20 kcal/h or up to 500 kcal/d for up to 6 days** (C2 rationale); trophic EN → less GI intolerance in week 1, similar ventilator-free days, ICU-free days, 60-day mortality and infections. Larger trial underdelivered protein (0.6–0.8 g/kg/d); high-risk patients were excluded
- **>50%–65%** of goal energy may be needed to prevent increased intestinal permeability and infection in burn and bone marrow transplant patients and to reduce mortality in high-risk patients (C3 rationale)
- Observational data (C4 rationale): achieving protein **1.3 g/kg** plus energy targets → 50% lower 28-day mortality; energy targets alone (0.8 g/kg protein) did not. Nonprotein calorie:nitrogen ratio (NPC:N) **70:1–100:1** and nitrogen balance are of limited value in the ICU

### D. Monitoring Tolerance and Adequacy of EN (pp 170–173)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| D1 | We suggest daily monitoring for EN tolerance; avoid inappropriate cessation of EN; minimize nil per os (NPO) orders around diagnostic tests or procedures to limit ileus and inadequate delivery | Based on expert consensus |
| D2a | We suggest GRVs **not** be used as part of routine care to monitor ICU patients on EN | Low (label printed once, after D2b) |
| D2b | Where GRVs are still used, we suggest avoiding holding EN for GRVs **<500 mL** in the absence of other signs of intolerance (section D1) | Low |
| D3a | We **recommend** enteral feeding protocols be designed and implemented to increase the overall percentage of goal calories provided | Moderate to High |
| D3b | We suggest considering a volume-based feeding protocol or a top-down multistrategy protocol | Based on expert consensus |
| D4 | We suggest assessing patients on EN for aspiration risk and proactively taking steps to reduce aspiration and aspiration pneumonia | Based on expert consensus |
| D4a | We **recommend** diverting the level of feeding by post-pyloric access device placement in patients at high aspiration risk (see also B5 as printed) | Moderate to High |
| D4b | We suggest switching high-risk patients, or those intolerant of bolus gastric EN, to continuous infusion | Based on expert consensus |
| D4c | We suggest prokinetics (metoclopramide or erythromycin) in patients at high aspiration risk, where clinically feasible | Low |
| D4d | We suggest nursing directives to reduce aspiration and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). In all intubated ICU patients on EN, elevate the head of bed **30°–45°** and consider chlorhexidine mouthwash **twice a day** | Based on expert consensus |
| D5 | We suggest not using blue food coloring or any coloring agent as a marker of EN aspiration; we also suggest not using glucose oxidase strips as surrogate markers of aspiration | Based on expert consensus (both parts) |
| D6 | We suggest EN not be automatically interrupted for diarrhea; continue feeds while evaluating etiology to determine treatment | Based on expert consensus |

- GI intolerance (D1 rationale): vomiting, abdominal distention, discomfort, high nasogastric output, high GRV, diarrhea, reduced flatus/stool, abnormal abdominal radiographs. Nurses most often cite **200 mL and 250 mL** as GRV thresholds for stopping EN
- Delivery gaps (D1 rationale): fewer than half reach goal; patients get on average ~50% of goal energy; EN ceases in >85% of patients for 8%–20% of infusion time
- GRV evidence (D2 rationale): sensitivity for aspiration **1.5%–4.1%** (GRV range 150–400 mL); raising the cutoff from 50–150 mL to 250–500 mL does not increase regurgitation, aspiration or pneumonia; GRVs **200–500 mL** should raise concern and prompt aspiration-reduction measures, but automatic cessation should not occur below **500 mL** without other signs of intolerance
- Protocols vs none: nosocomial infection RR **0.59** (0.43–0.81; Figure 6) (D3 rationale)
- Aspiration risk factors (D4 rationale): inability to protect the airway, nasoenteric access device, mechanical ventilation, age **>70 years**, reduced consciousness, poor oral care, inadequate nurse:patient ratio, supine position, neurologic deficits, gastroesophageal reflux, transport out of the ICU, bolus intermittent EN
- Post-pyloric (D4a rationale, 13 RCTs): pneumonia RR **0.75** (0.6–0.93); VAP RR **0.72** (0.55–0.93); no difference in mortality, ICU or hospital LOS, ventilation duration, or time to goal
- Prokinetic doses (D4c rationale): erythromycin **3–7 mg/kg/d**; metoclopramide **10 mg 4 times a day** (adjust in declining renal function); oral or IV. Lower GRVs vs control RR 1.87 (Figure 7) without mortality or infection difference. Risks: erythromycin cardiac toxicity, tachyphylaxis, bacterial resistance; metoclopramide tardive dyskinesia (more in elderly); both QT prolongation. Combination therapy raised watery diarrhea (54% vs 26.3%) — not recommended
- Head-of-bed 30°–45° vs supine: pneumonia **23% → 5%** in one study (D4d rationale)
- U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Health Advisory (September 2003) against blue food coloring as an aspiration monitor (D5 rationale)
- Diarrhea definition (D6 rationale): **2–3 liquid stools/day or >250 g liquid stool/day**; incidence 2%–95%. Workup: abdominal exam, stool quantification, stool culture for *Clostridium difficile* (and/or toxin assay), serum electrolytes, medication review; distinguish infectious from osmotic diarrhea

### E. Selection of Appropriate Enteral Formulation (pp 173–175)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| E1 | We suggest a standard polymeric formula when starting EN in the ICU. We suggest avoiding routine use of all specialty formulas in the MICU and disease-specific formulas in the SICU | Based on expert consensus |
| E2 | We suggest immune-modulating enteral formulations (arginine with other agents incl. eicosapentaenoic acid [EPA], docosahexaenoic acid [DHA], glutamine, nucleic acid) **not** be used routinely in the MICU; reserve consideration for TBI and perioperative SICU patients (sections O and M) | Very Low |
| E3 | **We cannot make a recommendation** at this time on routine use of an enteral formulation with an anti-inflammatory lipid profile (eg, omega-3 fish oils, borage oil) and antioxidants in ARDS and severe ALI, given conflicting data | Low to Very Low |
| E4a | We suggest a commercial mixed fiber formula **not** be used routinely and prophylactically to promote bowel regularity or prevent diarrhea | Low |
| E4b | We suggest considering a commercial mixed fiber–containing formula if there is persistent diarrhea. We suggest avoiding both soluble and insoluble fiber in patients at high risk of bowel ischemia or severe dysmotility. We suggest considering small peptide formulations in persistent diarrhea with suspected malabsorption or lack of response to fiber | Based on expert consensus |

- Standard formula = polymeric isotonic or near-isotonic **1- to 1.5-kcal/mL** (E1 rationale). Exception: immune-modulating formula for the postoperative SICU patient (O3). High-fat pulmonary formulas rest on an erroneous rationale (effect seen only with overfeeding). Disease-specific and severely fluid-restricted formulas may be used case by case (eg, electrolyte profile, volume restriction in renal)
- Mixed-fiber formulas: reports of bowel obstruction in surgical and trauma patients receiving insoluble fiber (E4 rationale)

### F. Adjunctive Therapy (pp 175–177)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | We suggest considering a fermentable soluble fiber additive (eg, fructo-oligosaccharides [FOSs], inulin) for routine use in all hemodynamically stable MICU/SICU patients on a standard formula. We suggest **10–20 g** of fermentable soluble fiber in divided doses over **24 h** as adjunctive therapy if there is diarrhea | Based on expert consensus |
| F2 | We suggest that, while studied probiotic species and strains appear safe in general ICU patients, they be used only for select medical and surgical populations in which RCTs document safety and outcome benefit. **We cannot make a recommendation** for routine probiotics across the general ICU population | Low |
| F3 | We suggest a combination of antioxidant vitamins and trace minerals, in doses reported safe in critically ill patients, for patients who require specialized nutrition therapy | Low |
| F4 | We suggest supplemental enteral glutamine **not** be added routinely to an EN regimen in critically ill patients | Moderate |

- Probiotic harms (F2 rationale): fungemia with *Saccharomyces boulardii* and worse outcomes in severe pancreatitis reported; studied probiotics may be considered in selected populations (eg, liver transplantation, trauma, pancreatectomy)
- Antioxidants/trace elements (vitamins E and C; selenium, zinc, copper), 15 trials: overall mortality RR **0.8** (0.7–0.92; Figure 8); no difference in infection, LOS or ventilation; consider renal function (F3 rationale)
- Enteral glutamine, 5 RCTs (558 burn, trauma, mixed ICU patients): no significant effect on mortality, infection or hospital LOS (Figure 9) (F4 rationale)

### G. When to Use PN (pp 177–179)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| G1 | We suggest that in the patient at **low** nutrition risk (eg, NRS 2002 **≤3** or NUTRIC **≤5**), exclusive PN be **withheld over the first 7 days** after ICU admission if the patient cannot maintain volitional intake and early EN is not feasible | Very Low |
| G2 | In the patient at **high** nutrition risk (eg, NRS 2002 **≥5** or NUTRIC **≥5**) or severely malnourished, when EN is not feasible, we suggest exclusive PN **as soon as possible** after ICU admission | Based on expert consensus |
| G3 | We **recommend** that in patients at low **or** high nutrition risk, supplemental PN be considered after **7–10 days** if unable to meet **>60%** of energy and protein requirements by the enteral route alone. Starting supplemental PN before this 7- to 10-day period in patients on some EN does not improve outcomes and may be detrimental | Moderate |

- PN-dependent patients (eg, short bowel) should continue PN on ICU admission unless bacteremia is suspected (G1 rationale)
- Beyond 7 days of STD the committee was concerned about nutritional deterioration, although the cited literature recommends withholding PN for 10–14 days (G1 rationale)
- Malnourished ICU patients: PN vs STD → fewer overall complications (RR 0.52); STD → higher mortality (RR 3.0) (G2 rationale)
- Late (day 8) vs early (day 3) supplemental PN on hypocaloric EN: more likely discharged alive from ICU, shorter ICU LOS, fewer infections (22.8% vs 26.2%) (G3 rationale)

### H. When Indicated, Maximize Efficacy of PN (pp 179–182)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | We suggest protocols and nutrition support teams to maximize PN efficacy and reduce PN-associated risk | Based on expert consensus |
| H2 | We suggest considering hypocaloric PN (**≤20 kcal/kg/d or 80% of estimated energy needs**) with adequate protein (**≥1.2 g protein/kg/d**) in appropriate patients (high risk or severely malnourished) requiring PN, initially over the first week in the ICU | Low |
| H3a | We suggest withholding or limiting soybean oil (SO)–based IV fat emulsion (IVFE) during the first week after starting PN to a maximum of **100 g/wk** (often divided into 2 doses/wk) if there is concern for essential fatty acid deficiency | Very Low |
| H3b | Alternative IVFEs may provide outcome benefit over SO-based IVFEs, but **we cannot make a recommendation** at this time because of lack of availability in the United States. When alternative IVFEs (SMOF [soybean oil, medium-chain triglyceride (MCT), olive oil, fish oil], MCT, olive oil, fish oil) become available in the United States, based on expert opinion, we suggest they be considered in the critically ill patient who is an appropriate PN candidate | Based on expert opinion (as printed) |
| H4 | Based on expert consensus, standardized commercially available PN vs compounded PN admixtures has **no advantage** in clinical outcomes | Based on expert consensus |
| H5 | We **recommend** a target blood glucose of **140 or 150–180 mg/dL** for the general ICU population; ranges for specific populations (postcardiovascular surgery, head trauma) may differ and are beyond the guideline's scope | Moderate |
| H6 | We **recommend** that parenteral glutamine supplementation **not** be used routinely in the critical care setting | Moderate |
| H7 | We suggest reducing PN energy as EN tolerance improves, and discontinuing PN when the patient receives **>60%** of target energy requirements from EN | Based on expert consensus |

- Refeeding risk factors (H1 rationale): alcoholism, weight loss, low body mass index (BMI), prolonged NPO; in these patients advance feeding more slowly, over **3–4 days** to goal
- Hypocaloric PN (H2 rationale): 20 kcal/kg/d vs 25 kcal/kg/d → less infection and shorter LOS in one meta-analysis; hyperglycemia 0% vs 33.1%; once stable, PN energy may be increased to **100%** of estimated requirements
- H3a: only recommendation below the 70% consensus threshold — **64% agreement** (9 for, 5 against) to "withhold or limit" SO-based IVFE to 100 g/wk rather than "withhold". Basis is primarily one 20-year-old trauma trial (Battistella) with acknowledged criticisms (overfeeding by nonprotein-calorie goals, fast lipid infusion); lipid-free PN reduced infectious morbidity (Figure 10)
- Glucose targets (H5 rationale): SCCM recommends **150–180 mg/dL**, A.S.P.E.N. **140–180 mg/dL**. A landmark trial of tight glucose control (**80–110 mg/dL**) with intensive insulin therapy reported benefit, but later trials (e.g., VISEP, stopped early) showed more severe hypoglycemia; NICE-SUGAR (6104 patients, target 80–100 vs <180 mg/dL) → higher 90-day mortality with tight control (27.5% vs 24.9%)
- Parenteral glutamine (H6 rationale): greatest concern at higher doses (**>0.5 g/kg/d**) early in critical illness with multiple-organ failure or vasopressor-requiring shock

### I. Pulmonary Failure (pp 182–183)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| I1 | We suggest **not** using specialty high-fat/low-carbohydrate formulations designed to manipulate the respiratory quotient and reduce CO₂ production in ICU patients with acute respiratory failure (not to be confused with E3) | Very Low |
| I2 | We suggest considering fluid-restricted energy-dense EN formulations for acute respiratory failure (especially with volume overload) | Based on expert consensus |
| I3 | We suggest monitoring serum phosphate closely and replacing phosphate appropriately when needed | Based on expert consensus |

- Avoid total energy above requirements (CO₂ production rises with lipogenesis); avoid rapid IVFE infusion (especially SO-based) in severe pulmonary failure (I1 rationale)
- Energy-dense formula = **1.5–2 kcal/mL** (I2 rationale)
- Hypophosphatemia: moderate = serum phosphorus **≤2.2 mg/dL**, severe = **<1.5 mg/dL**; nearly **30%** of ICU patients; phosphate <0.80 mmol/L linked to weaning failure (I3 rationale)

### J. Renal Failure (pp 183–184)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| J1 | We suggest ICU patients with acute renal failure (ARF) or acute kidney injury (AKI) receive a standard enteral formulation with standard ICU protein (**1.2–2 g/kg actual body weight/d**) and energy (**25–30 kcal/kg/d**). If significant electrolyte abnormalities develop, consider a renal specialty formulation (appropriate electrolyte profile) | Based on expert consensus |
| J2 | We **recommend** that patients on frequent hemodialysis or continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) receive increased protein, up to a maximum of **2.5 g/kg/d**. Protein should **not** be restricted in renal insufficiency as a means to avoid or delay dialysis | Very Low |

- Weight (J1 rationale): usual body weight for normal-weight patients, ideal body weight for obese and critically ill patients
- CRRT amino acid loss **10–15 g/d**; may need at least an additional **0.2 g/kg/d**, totaling up to 2.5 g/kg/d; no advantage above 2.5 g/kg/d (J2 rationale)

### K. Hepatic Failure (pp 184–185)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| K1 | We suggest using dry or usual weight instead of actual weight in predictive equations for energy and protein in cirrhosis and hepatic failure (ascites, intravascular volume depletion, edema, portal hypertension, hypoalbuminemia). We suggest **not** restricting protein in liver failure; use the same recommendations as other critically ill patients (section C4) | Based on expert consensus |
| K2 | We suggest EN be used preferentially when providing nutrition therapy to ICU patients with acute and/or chronic liver disease | Based on expert consensus |
| K3 | We suggest standard enteral formulations in ICU patients with acute and chronic liver disease. There is no evidence of further benefit of branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) formulations on coma grade in the ICU patient with encephalopathy already on first-line luminal-acting antibiotics and lactulose | Based on expert consensus |

- Energy needs in liver disease are best determined by IC; protein restriction may worsen nutrition status and lean mass and reduce ammonia removal (K1 rationale)
- Oral BCAA granules over 12–24 months may slow progression and prolong event-free survival in outpatient trials (K3 rationale)

### L. Acute Pancreatitis (pp 185–188)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| L1a | We suggest the initial nutrition assessment in acute pancreatitis evaluate disease severity to direct therapy; because severity may change quickly, reassess feeding tolerance and need for specialized therapy frequently | Based on expert consensus |
| L1b | We suggest **not** providing specialized nutrition therapy in **mild** acute pancreatitis — advance to oral diet as tolerated. If an unexpected complication develops or the patient fails to advance to oral diet within **7 days**, consider specialized nutrition therapy | Very Low |
| L1c | We suggest that patients with **moderate to severe** acute pancreatitis have a naso-/oroenteric tube placed and EN started at a trophic rate, advanced to goal as fluid resuscitation is completed (within **24–48 h** of admission) | Very Low |
| L2 | We suggest a standard polymeric formula to start EN in **severe** acute pancreatitis. Data are currently insufficient to recommend an immune-enhancing formulation | Very Low |
| L3a | We suggest EN over PN in severe acute pancreatitis requiring nutrition therapy | Low |
| L3b | We suggest EN by either the gastric or the jejunal route in severe acute pancreatitis — no difference in tolerance or clinical outcomes | Low |
| L4 | We suggest that in moderate to severe acute pancreatitis with EN intolerance, measures be taken to improve tolerance | Based on expert consensus |
| L5 | We suggest considering probiotics in severe acute pancreatitis patients receiving early EN | Low |
| L6 | We suggest that in severe acute pancreatitis, when EN is not feasible, PN be considered **after 1 week** from onset of the pancreatitis episode | Based on expert consensus |

**Severity definitions (L1a rationale, p 185)**

| Severity | Definition |
|---|---|
| Mild | No organ failure and no local complications |
| Moderately severe | Transient organ failure lasting **<48 h** and local complications |
| Severe | Persistent organ failure **>48 h** from admission |

- Organ failure = shock (systolic blood pressure **<90 mm Hg**), pulmonary insufficiency (PaO₂/FiO₂ **≤300**), or renal failure (serum creatinine **≥1.9 mg/dL**). Local complications on computed tomography (CT): pseudocyst, abscess, necrosis
- Earlier markers of moderately severe to severe disease: Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Assessment II (APACHE II) score **≥8**, Ranson criteria **>3**, CRP **>150 mg/L**; positive predictive value of high scores, systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) or CT necrosis for progression to severe disease is **<50%**
- Mild disease: ~6% complications, ~0% mortality, 81% advance to oral diet within 7 days; soft/low-fat diet vs clear liquids → shorter hospital LOS (−2.62 days; Figure 11); advancing first to clear liquids and normalization of pancreatic enzymes are **not** required (L1b rationale)
- Failure to start EN for **>72–96 h** after admission in moderate to severe disease risks rapid nutritional deterioration (L1c rationale)
- EN vs PN (L3a rationale, 10 RCTs): infectious morbidity RR 0.46, hospital LOS −3.94 days, surgical intervention RR 0.48, multiple-organ failure odds ratio (OR) 0.306, mortality OR 0.251 (Figures 12–13 show PN vs EN: mortality RR 2.17, infections RR 2.45)
- Measures to improve tolerance (L4 rationale): start EN within the first 48 h to limit ileus, divert feeding more distally, switch to a small-peptide/MCT or nearly fat-free elemental formula, change bolus to continuous infusion
- Probiotics (L5 rationale): a large Dutch multicenter trial (Besselink, 296 patients, multistrain probiotics delivered into the jejunum) showed **increased** mortality (16% vs 6%), multiple-organ failure and need for surgery; later meta-analysis and RCT data favourable. No specific dose or organism can be recommended
- Early PN within 24 h in mild-to-moderate disease caused net harm vs STD in one early trial (L6 rationale)

### N. Sepsis (pp 191–193)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| N1 | We suggest EN within **24–48 h** of diagnosis of severe sepsis/septic shock, as soon as resuscitation is complete and the patient is hemodynamically stable | Based on expert consensus |
| N2 | We suggest **not** using exclusive PN or supplemental PN with EN early in the acute phase of severe sepsis or septic shock, regardless of nutrition risk | Very Low |
| N3 | **We cannot make a recommendation** on selenium, zinc and antioxidant supplementation in sepsis because of conflicting studies | Moderate |
| N4 | We suggest trophic feeding (**10–20 kcal/h or up to 500 kcal/d**) for the initial phase of sepsis, advancing as tolerated after **24–48 h** to **>80%** of target energy over the first week. We suggest **1.2–2 g protein/kg/d** | Based on expert consensus |

- Hemodynamic stability (N1 rationale): adequate perfusion pressure, stable vasoactive doses, stabilized or decreasing lactate and metabolic acidosis, mean arterial pressure **≥60 mm Hg**
- Energy (N4 rationale): IC at baseline with follow-up **every 4 days**; if IC unavailable or FiO₂ **>0.60**, use **25 kcal/kg/d** or published equations

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| O1 | We suggest determining nutrition risk (eg, NRS 2002 or NUTRIC) on all postoperative ICU patients; traditional visceral proteins (serum albumin, prealbumin, transferrin) should **not** be used as markers of nutrition status | Based on expert consensus |
| O2 | We suggest EN when feasible in the postoperative period within **24 h** of surgery — better outcomes than PN or STD | Very Low |
| O3 | We suggest **routine** use of an immune-modulating formula (containing both arginine and fish oils) in the SICU for the postoperative patient who requires EN | Moderate to Low |
| O4 | We suggest enteral feeding for many patients in difficult postoperative situations (prolonged ileus, intestinal anastomosis, OA, vasopressors for hemodynamic support); individualize each case by perceived safety and clinical judgment | Low to Very Low |
| O5 | We suggest that after major upper GI surgery with EN not feasible, PN be started **only if** therapy is anticipated to last **≥7 days**. | O6 | We suggest that on postoperative diet advancement, patients be allowed solid food as tolerated; clear liquids are **not** required as the first meal | Based on expert consensus |

- EN not feasible postoperatively (O2 rationale): continued GI obstruction, bowel discontinuity, increased bowel ischemia risk, ongoing peritonitis. EN may be feasible with high-output fistulas, severe malabsorption, shock or severe sepsis if stable for at least **24–36 h**
- Both fish oil and arginine must be present for benefit (O3 rationale)
- Most ICU patients on a low, stable vasopressor dose may be fed into the stomach with close monitoring (O4 rationale)
- Major upper GI surgery (O5 rationale): esophagectomy, gastrectomy, pancreatectomy, other major reoperative abdominal procedures; benefit of PN seen when given preoperatively for 7–10 days and continued postoperatively; routine immediate postoperative PN → ~10% more complications
- Postoperative nausea ~20% whether advanced to clear liquids or solid meals (O6 rationale)

### Q. Obesity in Critical Illness (pp 196–199)

| # | Recommendation (condensed) | Evidence label |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | We suggest early EN within **24–48 h** of ICU admission for obese patients who cannot sustain volitional intake | Based on expert consensus |
| Q2 | We suggest nutrition assessment of the obese ICU patient focus on biomarkers of metabolic syndrome, comorbidities and level of inflammation, in addition to the parameters for all ICU patients | Based on expert consensus |
| Q3 | We suggest assessment focus on central adiposity, metabolic syndrome, sarcopenia, BMI **>40**, SIRS, or other comorbidities correlating with higher obesity-related cardiovascular and mortality risk | Based on expert consensus |
| Q4 | We suggest high-protein hypocaloric feeding in obese ICU patients to preserve lean body mass, mobilize adipose stores and minimize overfeeding complications | Based on expert consensus |
| Q5 | We suggest that for all obesity classes, EN not exceed **65%–70%** of target energy requirements measured by IC. Without IC, we suggest **11–14 kcal/kg actual body weight/d** for BMI **30–50** and **22–25 kcal/kg ideal body weight/d** for BMI **>50**. We suggest protein from **2.0 g/kg ideal body weight/d** for BMI **30–40** up to **2.5 g/kg ideal body weight/d** for BMI **≥40** | Based on expert consensus |
| Q6 | We suggest, if available, an enteral formula with low caloric density and reduced NPC:N in the obese ICU patient. Lack of outcome data precludes a recommendation on immune-modulating formulas | Based on expert consensus |
| Q7 | We suggest additional monitoring for worsening hyperglycemia, hyperlipidemia, hypercapnia, fluid overload and hepatic fat accumulation in the obese ICU patient on EN | Based on expert consensus |
| Q8 | We suggest the obese ICU patient with prior bariatric surgery receive supplemental **thiamine before** dextrose-containing IV fluids or nutrition therapy; consider evaluation for and treatment of deficiencies of calcium, thiamin, vitamin B₁₂, fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), folate, iron, selenium, zinc and copper | Based on expert consensus |

- Weight-based equations (11–14 kcal/kg actual / 22–25 kcal/kg ideal body weight) approximate **65%–70%** of measured energy expenditure (Q5 rationale)
- Adjusted body weight is not recommended (variable definitions, no validation); use BMI and ideal body weight (Q2, Q5 rationale)
- Mortality curve U-shaped: highest at BMI **>40** and **<25**, lowest at **30–40** (obesity paradox, Q1 rationale); BMI >30 → OR 1.5 for malnutrition
- 2.0 g/kg ideal body weight/d was insufficient for neutral nitrogen balance when BMI >40 (Q5 rationale)
- Bariatric patients (Q8 rationale): daily multivitamin with iron and vitamin B₁₂ plus calcium and vitamin D is encouraged; once normalized, monitor micronutrient levels annually

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Sepsis energy target, internal mismatch:** N4 advances to **>80%** of target energy over week 1, but its own rationale (p 192) says advancing to **60%–70%** of target over the first week "may be most appropriate and optimal"
- **Probiotics:** F2 confines probiotics to populations with RCT-documented safety and declines a general ICU recommendation, citing worse outcomes in severe pancreatitis; L5 nonetheless suggests considering probiotics in severe acute pancreatitis on early EN, despite the Besselink trial's higher mortality (16% vs 6%) cited in the same rationale
- **Pancreatitis EN-vs-PN meta-analysis wording:** the L3a rationale says 9 studies "showed reduced mortality (RR = 2.17 …)" — the RR >1 reflects Figures 12–13 plotting PN vs EN (higher risk with PN), not a reduction with the comparator named first
- **H3a lipid restriction** reached only 64% agreement (below the 70% threshold) and rests mainly on one dated trial
- **Glucose target** is a compromise between societies (SCCM 150–180 vs A.S.P.E.N. 140–180 mg/dL); specific populations deferred to SCCM glycemic guidelines
- **Evidence date:** literature searched only to December 31, 2013; landmark later trials were deliberately excluded (p 159)
