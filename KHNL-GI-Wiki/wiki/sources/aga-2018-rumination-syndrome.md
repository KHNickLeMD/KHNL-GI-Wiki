## Summary

The only document in the corpus that treats rumination syndrome. [[rumination-syndrome]] carried an empty Therapeutics section whose note said both of its sources cover diagnosis only, and sent the reader elsewhere for "diaphragmatic-breathing/behavioral therapy, baclofen, and neuromodulators". **This update supplies all three, plus the technique itself.**

**Diaphragmatic breathing, with or without biofeedback, is first-line in *all* cases** (Best Practice Advice 4) — and the update gives the instructions rather than the label: **inhale slowly through the nose while protruding the abdomen and keeping the chest stationary, exhale slowly through the mouth, aiming for 6–8 respirations per minute, for 15 minutes after every meal**, longer if the sensation of impending rumination persists, and **practised away from meals too**. Patients learn it by putting one hand on the chest and one on the abdomen and allowing only the abdominal hand to move. ⚠ **It need not be a specialist who teaches it** — speech therapists, psychologists, gastroenterologists and other practitioners familiar with the technique all qualify (BPA 5).

**Baclofen 10 mg three times daily is the named next step in refractory patients** (BPA 7), on a randomized cross-over trial in 22 patients (16 with rumination) showing a modest reduction in postprandial flow events and **symptom improvement in 63% on baclofen vs 26% on placebo**. The update is explicit about the limits of everything else: levosulpiride helped 38% but left 48% unchanged and **worsened 14%**; buspirone is "reasonable" on mechanism alone with **no trials in rumination** and anecdotally limited benefit; and **surgery should be avoided** — fundoplication rests on a 5-patient case series, and prior cohorts included patients still ruminating *after* fundoplication.

The diagnostic message is the one a general gastroenterologist most needs: **consider it in anyone with consistent postprandial regurgitation, because these patients are routinely labelled refractory reflux or vomiting** (BPA 1) — and ⚠ **nocturnal regurgitation, dysphagia, nausea, or symptoms away from meals do not exclude it; they only make it less likely** (BPA 2).

## Key Findings / Claims

### The 7 Best Practice Advice statements

**The document numbers them itself and attaches no grades** — this is an Expert Review Clinical Practice Update, so there are no GRADE ratings to report.

| # | Best Practice Advice |
|---|---|
| **1** | Clinicians **strongly should consider rumination syndrome in patients who report consistent postprandial regurgitation.** Such patients often are labelled as having refractory gastroesophageal reflux or vomiting. |
| **2** | **Presence of nocturnal regurgitation, dysphagia, nausea, or symptoms occurring in the absence of meals does not exclude rumination syndrome, but makes the presence of it less likely.** |
| **3** | Clinicians should **diagnose rumination syndrome primarily on the basis of Rome IV criteria after an appropriate medical work-up.** |
| **4** | **Diaphragmatic breathing with or without biofeedback is the first-line therapy in all cases** of rumination syndrome. |
| **5** | Instructions for effective diaphragmatic breathing **can be given by speech therapists, psychologists, gastroenterologists, and other health practitioners familiar with the technique.** |
| **6** | **Objective testing with postprandial high-resolution esophageal impedance manometry can be used to support the diagnosis**, but **expertise and lack of standardized protocols are current limitations.** |

### Clinical features and mechanism

- **Effortless, often repetitive regurgitation of recently ingested food into the mouth**, either re-chewed and reswallowed or expectorated. **Not preceded by nausea or retching**, and **not selective for liquids or solids** — though patients typically describe it as vomiting.
- **Timing: typically 10–15 minutes after a meal.**
- **Mechanism:** a **voluntary but unperceived and unintentional contraction of the abdominal wall musculature** raises intragastric pressure; when it overcomes lower esophageal sphincter pressure, gastric content flows freely into the esophagus and mouth. Three coordinated components — **thoracic suction, abdominal thrust and hiatal opening** — make the manoeuvre brief and smooth enough to pass unnoticed by the patient.
- **Accommodation abnormalities in 50%** of patients; **gastric emptying is normal in the majority**; **anxiety and depression are more common** than in the population.
- Clinical impact ranges from **minor social inconvenience to disabling nutritional impairment**. Epidemiology is essentially unknown.

### Rome IV criteria as the document prints them (Table 1)

- **Persistent or recurrent regurgitation of recently ingested food into the mouth** with subsequent **spitting or remastication and swallowing**.
- **Regurgitation is not preceded by retching.**
- **Criteria fulfilled for the past 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.**
- **Supportive criteria:** effortless events usually not preceded by nausea · regurgitant contains **recognizable food that might have a pleasant taste** · **the process tends to cease when the regurgitated material becomes acidic**.

### Objective testing

- **Postprandial high-resolution impedance manometry (HRIM)** supports the diagnosis (BPA 6); ⚠ **limited by expertise and the absence of standardized protocols**.
- **Electromyography (EMG) of the anterior abdominal wall and intercostal muscles** is **both diagnostic and therapeutic** — regurgitation episodes carry a characteristic pattern: an **abrupt (0.6 s) simultaneous rise** in intercostal activity (thoracic suction) and anterior abdominal wall activity (abdominal compression). ⚠ **Typically available only at select expert centres.**

### Diaphragmatic breathing — the technique

- **Learning it:** one hand on the chest, one on the abdomen; **only the abdominal hand moves outward with inspiration** while the chest stays still.
- **Doing it:** inhale **slowly through the nose** while protruding the abdomen and keeping the chest stationary; exhale **slowly through the mouth**, letting the abdomen retract. **Each inhalation and exhalation slow and complete, aiming for 6–8 respirations per minute.**
- **Dose:** **15 minutes after each meal**, or longer if the sensation of impending rumination remains; **also practised away from meals** to become expert at it.
- **Results:** resolution or improvement in **20%–66%** across uncontrolled studies and case series.
- **It works mechanically two ways** — it **lowers postprandial intragastric pressure** *and* **raises esophagogastric junction pressure**. ⚠ In an observational HRIM-biofeedback study, **rumination episodes restarted promptly once patients resumed their normal breathing pattern**.

### Biofeedback

- EMG-guided: patients watch real-time recordings and are trained to **reduce intercostal and anterior abdominal muscle activity while increasing diaphragmatic activity**, then practise the same exercises at home **5 minutes before and after meals**.
- One study: regurgitation fell from **18 to 8 episodes/day** (*P* < .001) with improved meal tolerance, and **to <1 episode/day by 6 months**.
- **Randomized trial vs sham:** rumination activity fell **74% ± 6%** (29 ± 6 → 7 ± 2 daily events) versus **1% ± 14%** with sham (21 ± 2 → 21 ± 4); *P* = .001.
- ⚠ EMG and HRIM equipment plus the expertise to run biofeedback **are not universally available, so most patients are taught diaphragmatic breathing without it** — which is why BPA 4 permits either.

### Medical therapy

- **Only 2 studies of pharmacotherapy exist in rumination syndrome.**
- **Baclofen** (γ-aminobutyric acid agonist; suppresses transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxations) **10 mg three times daily** — randomized cross-over trial, 22 patients (16 with rumination): modest reduction in postprandial flow events; **symptom improvement in 63% on baclofen vs 26% on placebo**. An open-label 1-week study in 12 adults reduced postprandial rumination events after a 1,000-kcal meal.
- ⚠ **Levosulpiride** (selective dopamine D2-receptor antagonist with prokinetic activity), 21 adults over 8 months with supportive psychotherapy: **38% improved, 48% unchanged, 14% worsened**.
- **Buspirone** (5-HT1A agonist, induces fundic relaxation): superior to placebo in functional dyspepsia but **no trials in rumination syndrome**. A trial is "reasonable" given that **up to 50%** of patients may have impaired fundic relaxation, "although anecdotally the clinical benefit appears to be limited."
- **Chewing gum** — beneficial in **2 case reports**, both pediatric/adolescent.

### Surgery

- ⚠ **"We recommend avoiding surgery as a therapy for rumination syndrome."** A case series of 5 non-responders improved after fundoplication, but **prior studies of rumination patients included people still symptomatic *after* fundoplication**. One case report of subtotal gastrectomy in a severe refractory case. **In the absence of controlled data and given surgical risk, any role in refractory patients remains investigational.**

### Information gaps the document names

Epidemiology (incidence, prevalence, risk factors) · quality-of-life impact · **no validated diagnostic or symptom-measurement tool exists**, unlike most other functional GI disorders · efficacy and **durability** of diaphragmatic breathing beyond one short-term randomized trial · pathophysiology and initiating factors.
