## Summary

The Rome V chapter on anorectal disorders — the document that **prints the actual Rome V diagnostic criteria** for the F-category disorders, which the Rome V process overview ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]) deliberately does not. Seven diagnoses in four groups: **F1** fecal incontinence (FI); **F2** anorectal pain (F2a levator ani syndrome, F2b unspecified/unexplained anorectal pain, F2c proctalgia fugax); **F3** dyssynergic defecation (DD); **F4** anorectal sensory dysfunction disorders (F4a rectal hyposensitivity, F4b rectal hypersensitivity).

The chapter's structural point is that **anorectal disorders split by what the diagnosis requires**: unlike other disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI), **DD and the rectal sensory disorders require symptoms *plus* physiologic testing**, whereas **FI and anorectal pain require symptoms alone**. The F4 disorders are layered diagnoses — they can only be made in a patient who already meets Rome V symptom criteria for FI or DD.

The largest criteria change is in **DD**: Rome IV required abnormalities in **≥2 of 3** test modalities, but inter-test concordance proved limited. Rome V requires **any 1** abnormal test (reduced rectoanal gradient or abnormal pattern on anorectal manometry, abnormal balloon expulsion, or impaired evacuation on defecography), introduces a **probable (1 abnormal test) vs definite (≥2) DD** distinction, broadens the symptom entry criterion to any one difficult-evacuation symptom in ≥25% of bowel movements, and **abolishes the type I–IV dyssynergia subclassification** for diagnostic purposes. FI criteria gain an **operative frequency floor** (≥1 episode/month documented on a 4-week stool diary) in place of the undefined word "recurrent."

Management is dominated by **biofeedback therapy (BT)**, which is the mainstay for DD, the only therapy with randomized support in levator ani syndrome, and the basis of sensory retraining in both rectal sensory disorders. The chapter grades the **diagnostic tests** (A1 through C) but issues **no graded treatment recommendations**.

### F1. Rome V diagnostic criteria for fecal incontinence

FI is defined as the **recurrent uncontrolled passage of fecal material for at least 3 months**. Fecal staining of underwear **is** included; clear mucus secretion and flatus incontinence are **not**.

Must include **all** of the following:

1. **Two or more** episodes of uncontrolled passage of fecal material
2. The individual should report **at least 1 episode of fecal incontinence per month on a 4-week stool diary**

*Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.* For clinical purposes, **2 or more episodes of uncontrolled passage of fecal material in the past 12 months**.

**Changes from Rome IV, and why:**

- **"Recurrent" → "2 or more" episodes.** "Recurrent" was nonspecific: in one study 55% of patients had <1 FI episode per month and 24% had exactly 1 per month. Even infrequent FI significantly impairs quality of life and such patients respond equally well to BT.
- **Developmental age removed** — the chapter addresses FI in adults.
- **Duration increased from 3 to 6 months**, because some patients may not have had an episode in the previous 3 months for lifestyle reasons, and to align with other Rome criteria.
- **A threshold of 1 FI episode per week was rejected for research studies** — up to 50% of patients have fewer than 1 episode per month with significant quality-of-life impairment, so a weekly floor would shrink eligibility.
- The **4-week stool diary** requirement was added for clarity and is affirmed by several randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

**FI phenotype → mechanism (the chapter's history-taking logic):**

| Symptom | Implied mechanism |
|---|---|
| Incontinence for **solid** stool | More severe sphincter weakness than liquid stool alone |
| **Nocturnal** incontinence | Uncommon; often internal anal sphincter (IAS) dysfunction (e.g. diabetes, scleroderma) |
| **Urge** incontinence (strong desire to defecate before leakage) | Reduced squeeze pressure and duration, and/or reduced rectal capacity with rectal hypersensitivity |
| **Passive** incontinence (no awareness of stooling) | Lower resting pressure |

- **Staining** = leakage of small amounts of stool (1–2 tablespoons). **Soiling** = more extensive leakage, which can be specified further (underwear, outer clothing, or furnishings/bedding).
- Severity is determined by **type** (solid, liquid, and/or gas), **quantity**, **frequency**, and **awareness of the urge** before incontinence.
- Anal sphincter weakness is the most frequent abnormality: in older women with FI, ~40% had reduced resting and ~80% reduced squeeze pressure. **Resting pressure is the strongest predictor of future FI after obstetric anal sphincter injury.** Up to one-third of patients with FI have **normal** anal sphincter pressures.
- Risk factors: age, female sex, obstetric trauma, diarrhea, cholecystectomy, diabetes, neurologic and inflammatory diseases, smoking, obesity. **Obstetric anal sphincter injury during vaginal or instrument-assisted delivery is the leading cause of anal sphincter trauma.**

### F2. Rome V diagnostic criteria for the anorectal pain disorders

The three syndromes are distinguished by **pain duration** and **presence or absence of levator ani tenderness on digital rectal examination**.

**F2a. Levator ani syndrome** — must include **all** of:

1. Chronic or recurrent anorectal pain
2. Episodes last **30 minutes or longer**
3. **Tenderness during traction on the puborectalis**
4. Exclusion of other causes of anorectal pain, such as anal fissure, thrombosed hemorrhoids, malignancy, and infectious or inflammatory causes such as inflammatory bowel disease, perianal abscess, fistula, prostatitis, coccygodynia, and gynecologic causes of pelvic pain

*Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.*

**F2b. Unexplained anorectal pain** — symptom criteria for levator ani syndrome **but no tenderness** during traction on the puborectalis muscle.

**F2c. Proctalgia fugax** — must include **all** of:

1. Recurrent episodes of pain localized to the anorectum and **unrelated to defecation**
2. Episodes last **from seconds to minutes**
3. **No anorectal pain between episodes**
4. Exclusion of other causes of anorectal pain, such as anal fissure, thrombosed hemorrhoids, malignancy, and infectious or inflammatory causes such as inflammatory bowel disease, perianal abscess and fistula, prostatitis, coccygodynia, and major structural disorders of the pelvic floor

*Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.*

**Changes from Rome IV, and why (4 changes):** (1) the order of the "other conditions" exclusion list was changed to match the frequency of those disorders; (2) "structural alterations" was replaced with "disorders"; (3) **"posterior traction" was removed**, because traction at other quadrants also evokes pain; (4) the term **"functional" was removed**.

- Levator ani syndrome is **a vague, dull ache or pressure sensation in the rectum, worse sitting than standing or lying down**. Left-sided tenderness was formerly thought more common, but a recent study found digital rectal examination reproduced pain in **1 or more anal quadrants with similar pain severity**. The distinction between levator ani syndrome and unexplained anorectal pain rests **largely on 1 study**, and the chapter's discussion applies to both.
- **85% of patients with levator tenderness had dyssynergia** on manometry (but not constipation) in an RCT of 157 patients with chronic anorectal pain; reversal with biofeedback strongly predicted symptom relief. 34% of patients with levator ani syndrome met criteria for DD. Patients with levator ani syndrome also have **weak anal sphincters and prolonged balloon expulsion time**.
- Proctalgia fugax attacks are **infrequent — fewer than 5 times per year in 51% of patients**; pain is cramping, gnawing, aching or stabbing, ranging from uncomfortable to unbearable; often recalled as nocturnal but can occur anytime, interrupting activities in 50%. **Hypertrophy of the IAS** has been reported in a hereditary form.

### F3. Rome V diagnostic criteria for dyssynergic defecation

DD is **paradoxical contraction or inadequate relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles and/or inadequate propulsive forces during attempted defecation**.

Must include **all** of the following:

1. The patient reports **1 or more symptoms suggestive of difficult evacuation** (excessive straining, use of digital maneuvers to evacuate, sensation of anorectal blockage, and/or feeling of incomplete evacuation) with **at least 25% of bowel movements**, and **may** satisfy diagnostic criteria for chronic constipation or irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)
2. During attempted defecation, features of impaired evacuation demonstrated by **any 1** of the following 3 tests:
   - a. **Reduced rectoanal pressure gradient or abnormal anorectal evacuation pattern** on anorectal manometry
   - b. **Abnormal balloon expulsion test**
   - c. **Impaired rectal evacuation on defecography**

*Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.* Abnormalities are identified by comparing data against **age- and sex-appropriate normal values for that particular manometry system**. Structural abnormalities such as rectocele, rectal mucosal intussusception and/or rectal sensory disorders **may coexist** with DD.

- **Probable vs definite DD: 1 abnormal test = probable DD; ≥2 abnormal tests = definite DD.**

**Changes from Rome IV, and why:**

- **"Functional" removed**, and "defecation disorders" (which includes structural disorders such as rectal prolapse) replaced by the more specific "dyssynergic defecation."
- **Type 3 dyssynergia and electromyography (EMG) removed** — EMG is rarely used clinically.
- **≥2 of 3 abnormal tests → any 1 abnormal test.** Concordance among the tests was limited; it was unclear whether a patient with prolonged balloon expulsion but a normal rectoanal gradient had DD. In 474 constipated patients and 184 healthy participants, high-resolution anorectal manometry, the balloon expulsion test and defecography findings were concordant, and a **reduced rectoanal gradient best predicted prolonged balloon expulsion or reduced rectal evacuation**. Requiring only one test also avoids unnecessary tests and radiation.
- **Symptom spectrum broadened:** patients reporting difficult evacuation who do **not** satisfy criteria for chronic constipation or IBS can now be diagnosed with DD if ≥1 symptom occurs >25% of the time and the physiology criteria are met — recognizing that patients with disordered defecation and **increased stool frequency or loose stools** who meet objective dyssynergia criteria respond to biofeedback.
- **No subclassification of DD.** Precise criteria for inadequate propulsion and paradoxical contraction vary across techniques, do not reliably discriminate healthy people from DD patients, and do not predict response to BT.
- Rome V specifies that **assessment of rectoanal coordination, such as the rectoanal gradient, is the best manometry measure of abnormal evacuation**.

### F4a. Rome V diagnostic criteria for rectal hyposensitivity

Must include **all** of the following:

1. Patients fulfill **Rome V symptom criteria for fecal incontinence or dyssynergic defecation**
2. Demonstration of rectal hyposensitivity on rectal balloon distention sensory testing — **2 or more threshold values** (volume/pressure) of rectal sensation (first sensation, desire to defecate, urgency to defecate, or maximum tolerable sensation) **greater than 2 SDs above the upper limit of the normal range**, using either a simple balloon (anorectal manometry) or rectal barostat
3. **No evidence of structural disease** on colonoscopy/barium enema or computed tomography, or **metabolic abnormalities**

*Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.* With a latex/simple manometry balloon, rectal sensory thresholds can be affected by **rectal compliance** — consider this when interpreting results.

### F4b. Rome V diagnostic criteria for rectal hypersensitivity

Must include **all** of the following:

1. Patients fulfill **Rome V symptom criteria for fecal incontinence or dyssynergic defecation**
2. Demonstration of rectal hypersensitivity on rectal balloon distention sensory testing — **1 or more threshold values** (volume/pressure) of rectal sensation (desire to defecate, urgency to defecate, maximum tolerable sensation, or pain) **less than 2 SDs below the lower limit of the normal range**, using either a simple balloon (anorectal manometry) or rectal barostat
3. **No history of rectal excision** and no evidence of structural disease, **including full-thickness rectal prolapse**, on endoscopy or imaging

*Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.* **Thresholds for first or constant sensation should not be used.**

- Note the **asymmetry**: hyposensitivity needs **2 or more** abnormal thresholds and may use first sensation; hypersensitivity needs only **1** and must not use first sensation (the first-sensation threshold is less reliable).
- **Hypersensitivity to the maximum tolerated threshold (or pain) is the most clinically relevant phenotype.**
- One study defined hypersensitivity as ≥2 thresholds below 2 SDs of healthy participants: **first ≤20 mL, desire to defecate ≤80 mL, urgency to defecate or maximum tolerable volume ≤150 mL**.
- Rectal hyposensitivity may be **secondary to** an enlarged rectum and/or increased rectal compliance, opioids, Parkinson disease, spinal cord injury, diabetes, or prolonged stool retention. **Electrolyte and renal metabolic dysfunction should be excluded before diagnosis.**
- On digital rectal examination, **if stool is present in the rectum and the patient is unaware, hyposensitivity should be suspected.**

### Graded diagnostic-test recommendations (Table 2)

The chapter's **only** graded statements. Grades: **A1** = excellent evidence in favor of the test based on high specificity, sensitivity, accuracy and positive predictive values; **B2** = good evidence with some evidence on those measures; **B3** = fair evidence with some evidence on those measures; **C** = poor evidence with some evidence on those measures.

**Fecal incontinence:**

| Test | Evidence | Grade | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anorectal manometry | Good | **A1** | Facilitates diagnosis of FI, DD and rectal sensory disorders |
| Translumbosacral anorectal magnetic stimulation | Good | **B2** | Identifies neuropathy in FI, levator ani syndrome, spinal cord injury, mixed FI and constipation |
| Anal ultrasound | Good | **B2** | Not widely available; rigid probe useful, endoscopic anal ultrasound not reliable |
| Magnetic resonance (MR) defecography | Good | **B2** | Open magnet and seated assessment ideal but not widely available |
| Needle EMG | Fair | **B3** | Research laboratories only; painful |
| Surface EMG | Fair | **B3** | Largely used for biofeedback |
| Pudendal nerve terminal motor latency | Fair | **B3** | Conflicting recommendations; rarely used clinically |

**Constipation with dyssynergic defecation:**

| Test | Evidence | Grade | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anorectal manometry | Excellent | **A1** | Identifies DD, weak or hypertonic sphincters/puborectalis, rectal hypo-/hypersensitivity, rectal compliance, Hirschsprung disease |
| Balloon expulsion test | Excellent | **A1** | Identifies DD and impaired evacuation |
| Fluoroscopic defecography | Good | **B2** | Performed seated; helps diagnose DD when manometry/balloon expulsion are inconclusive |

### Operative test thresholds

- **Balloon expulsion test:** patient attempts to expel a water-filled rectal balloon with either **50 mL of water** or inflated until the individual's defecatory desire, **in privacy and seated on a commode**. Normal expulsion times range from **20 seconds to 2 minutes**, although a **1-minute cutoff is recommended**. Reproducible in 90% of individuals across days.
- **Rectoanal gradient (RAG):** among healthy people with a normal balloon expulsion time, the 10th–90th percentile range for the RAG in the left lateral position on high-resolution anorectal manometry is **–70 to 12 mm Hg in women** and **–128 to 1 mm Hg in men**. The **RAG is abnormal only when it falls below the lower limit of normal** for sex and (in women) age. An abnormal RAG measured with the Manoscan catheter is **36% sensitive and 85% specific** for predicting a prolonged balloon expulsion time.
- **Pre-test probability of reduced rectal evacuation** by RAG + balloon expulsion result: **14%** when both normal, **45%** when either abnormal, **75%** when both abnormal.
- An abnormal balloon expulsion test and an abnormal gradient are **81%** and **86% specific**, respectively, for predicting reduced rectal evacuation — which is why **defecography may not be required**.
- **The 4 historical dyssynergia types** (from an early study of 35 constipated patients): **type I** adequate propulsion (≥45 mm Hg) with paradoxical anal contraction; **type II** inadequate propulsion (<45 mm Hg) with paradoxical contraction or insufficient relaxation; **type III** adequate propulsion (≥45 mm Hg) with absent/insufficient anal relaxation (<20%); **type IV** inadequate propulsion (<45 mm Hg) with inadequate relaxation (<20%). A controlled study found **87% of healthy participants had "abnormal" patterns** suggestive of DD; interobserver agreement is substantial for types I and IV, moderate for normal/dyssynergic patterns, fair for types II and III. **By themselves, patterns may have limited utility** — hence no Rome V subclassification.
- **Position matters:** rectoanal pressures and the RAG during evacuation are **greater seated than left lateral**, and seated high-resolution manometry is more useful for distinguishing healthy individuals from DD patients. Ideally attempted defecation is performed **seated**, though catheter displacement makes this challenging. In practice manometry is generally performed **left lateral**.
- Up to **two-thirds of patients with DD also have slow colonic transit** — so slow colonic transit **does not** remove the need for anorectal testing.

### Treatment — fecal incontinence

- **Primary goal: optimize stool form and restore normal bowel habits**, especially with loose stools.
- **Diet/fiber:** low-lactose or low-FODMAP (fermentable oligo-, di-, monosaccharides and polyols) diets, and **psyllium — but not gum arabic or carboxymethylcellulose** — improved FI vs placebo in a controlled study.
- **Drugs:** loperamide or diphenoxylate-atropine for diarrhea without fecal impaction (loperamide also **increases IAS tone**). Amitriptyline may improve FI via anticholinergic effects. Cholestyramine benefits patients with diarrhea and FI. **Clonidine alone was not beneficial over placebo** in an RCT; **oral clonidine 0.1 mg twice daily with colesevelam 1875 mg twice daily** improved stool form, FI episodes for semi-formed stools, and FI severity.
- **Transanal therapies:** suppositories (glycerine, bisacodyl), low-volume enemas and irrigation may help in FI with abnormal evacuation, but studies are lacking. In older adults, **lactulose 10 mL twice daily plus weekly enemas** improved FI. Transanal irrigation has RCT support in **neurogenic bowel/spinal cord injury, spina bifida, and anterior resection syndrome**, but **more than one-third discontinue within 1 year**.
- **Devices:** in the pivotal anal insert device study, 77% of 73 completers and 62% of 91 intention-to-treat participants reported ≥50% reduction in FI frequency. A US Food and Drug Administration–approved **vaginal bowel control device** (inflatable balloon compressing the rectum): of 110 women, 65 were successfully fitted and 79% achieved ≥50% reduction at 1 month.
- **Biofeedback:** patients are taught to selectively contract the external anal sphincter and puborectalis to improve strength and duration of anal squeeze, and to correct dyssynergia when present; reinforcement via balloon distention with progressively smaller volumes. Multiple RCTs show efficacy. **More than half maintain benefit at a 7-year median follow-up.** **Patients failing initial therapy rarely respond later and should be referred for alternative therapies.** Intact baseline sensation and improved sensory discrimination predicted greater success.
- **Sacral nerve stimulation:** 2-staged, with permanent implantation **contingent on response to a 2- to 4-week test period**. In the pivotal open-label US multicenter study, 90% of 120 patients received permanent stimulation; at 5 years, 36% achieved complete continence and 89% were deemed a therapeutic success. In another 5-year follow-up (n = 101), 42.6% had a favorable outcome, and **younger age, urge FI and 6-month response predicted success**. **No high-quality, multicenter, sham-controlled RCT has confirmed efficacy**, mechanisms remain uncertain, and sham-controlled trials showed clinically meaningful improvement with sham stimulation.
- **Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation has not shown significant benefit over sham** in multiple RCTs including a large trial; it may be combined with biofeedback in coexistent FI and obstructive defecation.
- **Translumbosacral neuromodulation therapy (TNT):** repetitive magnetic stimulation of the lumbar and sacral plexus. In a dose-ranging RCT of 1 vs 5 vs 15 Hz, **1 Hz was superior with 91% responders** (>50% reduction in FI episodes); anal neuropathy, squeeze pressure, rectal capacity and quality of life improved. A sham-controlled study further confirmed efficacy.
- **Sphincter bulking:** five RCTs evaluated 10 materials in 382 patients. In the pivotal trial (n = 206), **dextranomer in stabilized hyaluronic acid achieved 52% response vs 31% sham (number needed to treat 4.4)**; adverse events mostly mild (proctalgia, bleeding) with rare abscesses; quality-of-life improvement was not significant. At 2 years outcomes were comparable with BT, and a recent large multicenter RCT found dextranomer and BT both ~29% responder rate. **Best responders: shorter FI duration, obstetric cause, no prior therapy.**
- **SphinKeeper:** 10 self-expandable polyacrylonitrile prostheses (**20 mm × 1.8 mm**) implanted into the intersphincteric space; **prosthetic displacement 14%, anorectal pain 71%**.
- **Intrarectal botulinum A toxin** reduced FI plus urgency episodes in a randomized placebo-controlled study; deserves further evaluation, especially for urge FI.
- **Overlapping anterior sphincteroplasty:** up to 60%–85% improved short-term, but **failure rates ~50% at 40–60 months**.
- **Colostomy remains a last resort** for severe FI and improves quality of life.

### Treatment — anorectal pain

- **Levator ani syndrome:** in an RCT, **87% of patients with levator tenderness reported adequate pain relief after BT vs 45% after electrical stimulation and 22% after massage**. **Patients without tenderness did not benefit from any therapy.** Electrogalvanic stimulation may be considered if BT is unavailable. An open-label TNT study reported significant improvement in anorectal pain and lumbosacral neuropathy.
- **Botulinum A toxin** (intra-anal or intra-levator): retrospective studies suggested improvement, but **1 RCT found no benefit over placebo**.
- **Ineffective in levator ani syndrome:** muscle relaxants, diazepam, sacral nerve stimulation, sitz baths, radiofrequency, pudendal infiltration, transgluteal decompression, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), and laparoscopic decompression.
- **Proctalgia fugax** has been described as "harmless, unpleasant, and incurable." **Reassurance is often sufficient**; frequent or severe attacks may require therapy. In an RCT, **inhaled salbutamol shortened the duration of severe and prolonged attacks** vs placebo. Clonidine, amyl nitrite, nitroglycerine, neuromodulators and behavioral therapies have been suggested, but **no comparative studies exist**.

### Treatment — dyssynergic defecation

- Management begins with **explanation, education and lifestyle measures**.
- **BT is the mainstay** — it restores bowel function by improving rectoanal coordination. **A multicomponent BT is preferred.** Large RCTs confirm efficacy short- and long-term; BT is **superior to sham feedback, pelvic floor exercises, laxatives and muscle relaxant drugs**.
- **BT is ineffective for isolated slow-transit constipation.**
- Two RCTs found surgery superior to BT, but one included patients whose symptoms were due to **structural** pelvic floor abnormalities and the other used a **suboptimal biofeedback protocol**.
- **Predictors of favorable BT outcome:** anal digitation, hard stools, greater patient motivation, **undergoing ≥5 sessions**, and prolonged balloon expulsion time.
- **Home-based BT achieves success rates comparable with office BT (68% vs 70%) at lower cost.**
- **Pelvic floor physical therapy** is a widely used alternative, although evidence and standardization are lacking and it **differs significantly from BT**.
- Barriers to BT: labor-intensiveness, lack of dedicated equipment and standardized protocol, limited therapist availability, poor insurance coverage.

### Treatment — rectal sensory disorders

- **Hyposensitivity:** because it may coexist with stool retention, improving rectal emptying with laxatives, enemas, suppositories or BT for dyssynergia/FI may help. **Sensory BT is particularly useful** — repeated balloon inflations starting at a volume that induces urge, then **reducing by 5–10 mL with each inflation**; if a sensation is not perceived the volume is increased slightly. In an RCT a **barostat-assisted sensory training technique was superior to syringe-assisted training** in patients with ≥2 abnormal sensory thresholds. **Rectal electrical stimulation also enhanced sensation, whereas biofeedback without sensory training did not.**
- **Hypersensitivity:** possible therapies include hypnotherapy, psychotherapy, secretagogues, antispasmodics, tricyclic antidepressants, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, α2 agonists (e.g. clonidine), nitrates, and pregabalin. **Sensory adaptation therapy (SAT)** = intermittent barostat-mediated phasic distentions in **1- to 2-mm Hg increments**, starting at the desire-to-defecate threshold and continuing until pain is reported on 2 consecutive inflations; **sessions repeated at 1- to 2-week intervals for 6 sessions over 3 months**. In an RCT, **SAT but not escitalopram significantly improved hypersensitivity and bowel symptoms**; both improved quality of life and psychological profiles; 80% of SAT pain responders were also hypersensitivity responders, and **SAT was better tolerated**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Pain-duration cut-point for levator ani syndrome.** This chapter prints **"30 minutes or longer"** (F2a criterion 2). The wiki's [[proctalgia-syndromes]] page carried **≥20 minutes**, attributed to [[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]. The criteria chapter is the document that prints the criteria, so **30 minutes wins the page**; the 20-minute figure is removed.
- **Symptom-duration footnote.** Every F-category criteria box in this chapter carries *"Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis."* The Rome V process overview ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]) describes a general move to an **8-week** duration with an explicit exemption for infrequent-episode disorders including proctalgia fugax. The two Rome V chapters are the same tier and year; the anorectal chapter is the more specific document for these diagnoses, so **its 3-month/6-month footnote asserts the F-category pages**.
- **Internal tension in the FI criteria.** The justification text says the duration "was increased from **3 to 6 months**," while the definition sentence reads "for at least **3 months**" and the criteria footnote reads "last **3 months** with symptom onset at least **6 months** before diagnosis." The footnote is the operative rule; the wiki states it as printed.
