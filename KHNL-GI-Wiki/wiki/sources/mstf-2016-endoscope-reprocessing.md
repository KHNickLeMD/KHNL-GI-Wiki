## Summary

The **Multi-Society Task Force (MSTF) reprocessing guideline**, third iteration after 2003 and 2011, and the document that **sets the 41 reprocessing recommendations a United States endoscopy unit is held to**. Drafted by a subcommittee of the ASGE Quality Assurance in Endoscopy Committee, then redistributed for endorsement; the **2016 endorsing organizations** are ASGE, AASLD, ACG, AGA, APIC, ASCRS, SHEA, SAGES and SGNA.

The update was prompted by the duodenoscope-transmitted multidrug-resistant organism outbreaks of 2012–2015, which occurred **despite apparently appropriate cleaning and high-level disinfection**, and by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) communications that followed. It retains the expanded detail on cleaning and drying introduced in 2011 and adds guidance specific to **endoscope models with movable elevators at the distal tip** — duodenoscopes and linear ultrasound endoscopes.

Recommendations are graded with the **CDC category system**, not GRADE: **IA** (strongly recommended, well-designed experimental/clinical/epidemiologic studies), **IB** (strongly recommended, some studies plus strong theoretical rationale), **IC** (required by state or federal regulation — absence of an IC recommendation does not imply absence of state regulation), **II** (recommended, suggestive studies or theoretical rationale), and **NR** (no recommendation — unresolved issue, insufficient evidence or no consensus).

The document is explicit about its own limits. It covers **high-level disinfection (HLD) of flexible GI endoscopes only** — not sterilization for extraluminal applications such as natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery or intraoperative endoscopy through open or laparoscopic access, and not cystoscopes, bronchoscopes or rigid/semirigid endoscopes. It also states that **neither HLD nor extreme application of HLD processes can achieve the needs of sterile environments**, and that the FDA has acknowledged flexible endoscopes **cannot be sterilized with available high-level disinfectants** — hence the long-standing term "high-level disinfectant/sterilant" should **no longer imply the ability to sterilize endoscopes**.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Spaulding classification and level of disinfection

Three categories of medical device, by the risk of infection involved with their use:

| Category | Definition | Required processing | GI examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Enters normally **sterile tissue or the vascular system** | **Sterilization** — destruction of all microbial life | Endoscopes used in sterile settings (laparoscopic endoscopy); endoscopic accessories such as biopsy forceps and sphincterotomes |
| **Semicritical** | Contacts **intact mucous membranes**, does not ordinarily penetrate sterile tissue | **At least high-level disinfection (HLD)** — destruction of all vegetative microorganisms, mycobacteria, small or nonlipid viruses, medium or lipid viruses, fungal spores, and **some, but not all, bacterial spores** | **GI endoscopes** |
| **Noncritical** | Does not ordinarily touch the patient, or touches only intact skin | **Low-level disinfection** | Stethoscopes, patient carts |

### Pathogen transmission — the background the recommendations respond to

- **>20 million GI endoscopic procedures** are performed annually in the United States; patient outcomes are not routinely tracked, and reports of pathogen transmission are **rare**.
- A historical review of 265 articles (1966–1992) attributed **281 transmission episodes** to GI endoscopy. In every instance transmission was associated with **a breach of accepted cleaning and disinfection guidelines, an unacceptable liquid chemical germicide, improper drying, or defective equipment**.
- CDC review of its investigation log **1980–2002 found no outbreaks of infection associated with GI endoscopy**. MAUDE database review 1990–2002 revealed **7 possible episodes**.
- Compliance is imperfect: a 2009 CDC pilot audit of **68 ambulatory surgical centers** found **non-uniform compliance in 28%**. A 2004 SGNA survey found compliance with published standards inconsistent and widely variable.
- **Duodenoscope transmission:** patient-to-patient transmission of carbapenem-resistant *Enterobacteriaceae* or other multidrug-resistant organisms during [[ercp|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]] **despite appropriate and optimal reprocessing**, reported by **at least 8 major US medical centers**, with similar outbreaks in Europe. **10 to 12 outbreaks and at least 60 clinical infections in the United States, 25 or more outbreaks and over 250 infections with more than 20 deaths worldwide** in the 3 years before publication. **>1000 potentially exposed patients** advised to undergo screening cultures; **at least 100 believed to harbor silent carriage**. Transmission is attributed to **persistent contamination at the elevator region and/or the elevator cable**, and has occurred with **instrument designs from all major duodenoscope manufacturers**.
- **Nonendoscopic transmission** — unrelated to the endoscope itself — has caused several hepatitis C outbreaks through improper handling of intravenous sedation tubing, multidose vials and reuse of needles or syringes.

### The 41 recommendations

*Condensed from the document, which numbers them 1–41 and assigns each a CDC category. Numbering and categories are the document's own; wording is simplified where the original is long, with every operative qualifier kept.*

| # | Recommendation | Category |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | All healthcare personnel in the endoscopy suite should be trained in and comply with standard infection prevention and control recommendations, including those protecting both patients and healthcare workers | **IA** |
| 2 | **Preclean at the point of use**, before bioburden has an opportunity to dry and before comprehensive decontamination. Wipe the exterior with appropriate detergent solution and aspirate a large volume of detergent solution through the air/water and biopsy channels | **IB** |
| 3 | After point-of-use precleaning, **transport the soiled endoscope to the reprocessing area before remaining soil has an opportunity to dry**. Contain it so as not to expose staff, patients or the environment — an open container suffices for transport to an immediately adjacent reprocessing room, but **fully enclosed and labeled containers or bags** are required for transport through corridors used by others | **II** |
| 4 | **Perform pressure/leak testing after each use and before formal reprocessing**, according to manufacturer guidelines | **IB** |
| 5 | Before manual or automated HLD, **meticulously clean the entire endoscope** — valves, channels, connectors and all detachable parts — **using only model-specific cleaning devices (such as brushes) designed for the endoscope model being cleaned**. **Manual cleaning should occur within the manufacturer's recommended time frame**; if delayed beyond it, follow the manufacturer's directions for delayed processing. Maintain strict compliance with manufacturer guidance, particularly for endoscopes with movable mechanisms such as the **distal tip elevator on duodenoscopes and linear ultrasound endoscopes**. Disconnect and disassemble endoscope components (air/water and suction valves) and **completely immerse** endoscope and components in a compatible detergent. **Flush and brush all accessible channels** to remove organic and other residues. **Repeatedly actuate the valves during cleaning** to facilitate access to all surfaces. Clean external surfaces and components with a soft cloth, sponge or brushes | **IB** |
| 6 | Use **brushes appropriate for the size** of the endoscope channel, parts, connectors and orifices — bristles should contact all surfaces. Brushes must be appropriately sized for the part being brushed and **approved for that use by the endoscope manufacturer**. Cleaning items should be **disposable, or thoroughly cleaned and disinfected between uses** | **II** |
| 7 | **Discard enzymatic detergents after each use** — they are not microbicidal and will not retard microbial growth | **IB** |
| 8 | **Reusable accessories that break the mucosal barrier** (e.g. biopsy forceps, other cutting instruments) should be **mechanically cleaned and then sterilized between each patient use — HLD is not appropriate**. Reprocessing of single-use items should **not** be performed except according to FDA guidance | **IA** |
| 9 | **Ultrasonic cleaning** of reusable accessories and endoscope components may be used to remove soil and organic material from hard-to-clean areas | **II** |
| 10 | Endoscopes and accessories contacting mucous membranes are **semicritical** and should receive **at least HLD after each patient use** | **IA** |
| 11 | New high-level disinfectants and agent-specific machines continue to appear. **Use a high-level disinfectant and compatible reprocessing machine cleared by the FDA for their respective HLD claims**; obtain information from the FDA website and independent peer-reviewed publications | **IA** |
| 12 | **Exposure time and temperature vary among FDA-cleared high-level disinfectants — follow the FDA-cleared label claims for HLD** | **IA** |
| 13 | **Select a liquid disinfectant or sterilization technology that is compatible with the endoscope.** Avoid using a specific high-level disinfectant or sterilization technology on an endoscope if the manufacturer warns against its use because of functional damage, with or without cosmetic damage | **IC** |
| 14 | The disinfectant field is dynamic and new products appear. Persons or committees responsible for selecting disinfectants for GI endoscope reprocessing should be **guided by FDA clearance of these products and by information in the scientific literature** | **II** |
| 15 | **Completely immerse the endoscope and its components in the HLD solution and ensure all channels are perfused.** **Nonimmersible GI endoscopes should not be used** | **IB** |
| 16 | HLD can be performed in an **automated endoscope reprocessor (AER)** or manually. **Use of an AER is advisable and should be adopted when feasible.** When an AER is used, ensure the endoscope and its components **can be effectively reprocessed in that AER** — for example, **the elevator wire channel of duodenoscopes is not effectively disinfected by most AERs, and this step should be performed manually**. Obtain and review FDA-cleared model-specific reprocessing protocols from both the endoscope and AER manufacturers and check for compatibility | **IB** |
| 17 | If an AER is used, **place the endoscope and components in the reprocessor and attach all channel connectors according to the AER and endoscope manufacturers' instructions** to ensure exposure of all internal surfaces to the high-level disinfectant. **Only approved connectors should be used** | **IB** |
| 18 | **If an AER cycle is interrupted, HLD or sterilization cannot be ensured — therefore the cycle should be repeated** | **II** |
| 19 | Because design flaws have compromised AER effectiveness and can also involve endoscopes, infection prevention staff should **routinely review and document their attention to FDA advisories, manufacturer alerts and the scientific literature** for reports of endoscope and AER deficiencies that may lead to infection | **II** |
| 20 | After HLD, **rinse the endoscope and flush the channels with sterile or filtered water** to remove the disinfectant solution. **Discard the rinse water after each use or cycle.** **Flush the channels with 70% to 90% ethyl or isopropyl alcohol and dry using filtered forced air.** The final drying steps greatly reduce the risk of remaining pathogens and the possibility of recontamination by waterborne microorganisms. Some organizations stipulate use of "instrument air," further characterized relative to humidity, vapors, and so on | **IA** |
| 21 | **Visually inspect both endoscopes and reusable accessories frequently** in the course of their use and reprocessing — before, during and after use, as well as during and after cleaning and before HLD. **Manual cleaning of complex endoscope components, such as elevators, requires optimal lighting and may be facilitated by magnification.** Damaged endoscopes and accessories should be removed from use for repair or disposal | **II** |
| 22 | **Store the endoscope hanging in a vertical position to facilitate drying**, with caps, valves and other detachable components removed as per manufacturer instructions | **II** |
| 23 | Endoscopes should be **stored in a manner that will protect them from contamination**. In the absence of data linking infection outbreaks to transport or storage, and given the limited data on this issue, **equipment and practices for storage and for transport should be addressed at the facility level**, in conjunction with the infection prevention department or consultants | **II** |
| 24 | **Beyond the reprocessing steps discussed in these recommendations, no validated methods for additional duodenoscope reprocessing currently exist.** However, units should review and consider the feasibility and appropriateness for their practice of using **1 or more of the additional modalities *suggested* by the FDA** for duodenoscopes: intermittent or per-procedure culture surveillance of reprocessing outcomes, sterilization with ethylene oxide gas, repeat application of standard HLD, or use of a liquid chemical germicide | **II** |
| 25 | **Although reuse of endoscopes within 21 days of HLD appears to be safe, the data are insufficient to proffer a maximal outer duration for use of appropriately cleaned, reprocessed, dried and stored flexible endoscopes.** This interval remains poorly defined and warrants further study; some organizations advise shorter intervals | **NR** |
| 26 | **Use HLD or sterilize the water bottle** (used for cleaning the lens and irrigation during the procedure) **and its connecting tube at least daily**. **Sterile water should be used to fill the water bottle** | **IB** |
| 27 | **Maintain a log for each procedure** recording the patient's name and medical record number (if available), the procedure, the **serial number or other identifier of the endoscope (and AER, if used)**, the date and type of the procedure, and **the name of the person performing the cleaning and HLD/sterilization process** — to assist in an outbreak investigation. Logs for transmission identification and reporting should include **identifiers and use of specific loaner endoscopes** that may be added to local inventories on a temporary basis | **II** |
| 28 | **Perform routine testing of the liquid HLD to ensure at least the minimum effective concentration of the active ingredient. Check the solution at the beginning of each day of use** (or more frequently per manufacturer guidelines) **and document the results. If the chemical indicator shows the concentration is less than the minimal effective concentration, discard the solution** | **IA** |
| 29 | **Discard the liquid HLD at the end of its reuse life** (which may be single use), **regardless of the minimal effective concentration.** If additional liquid HLD is added to an AER (or basin, if manually disinfected), the reuse life should be determined by the **first use/activation of the original solution** — that is, **"topping off" a liquid HLD pool does not extend its reuse life** | **IB** |
| 30 | Facilities where endoscopes are used and disinfected should be **designed to provide a safe environment for healthcare workers and patients**. **Eyewash stations should be available to reprocessing staff using caustic chemicals, and should not be placed on sinks used for washing or soaking soiled endoscopes.** Air exchange equipment (ventilation system and exhaust hoods) should minimize exposure of all persons to potentially toxic vapors (e.g. glutaraldehyde); vapor concentration should **not exceed allowable limits** (e.g. ACGIH, OSHA). Organic vapor respirators are **not intended for routine use and are not a substitute** for adequate ventilation, vapor recovery systems and work practice controls | **IB and IC** |
| 31 | **Reprocessing facilities should be designed with attention to the optimal flow of personnel, endoscopes and devices to avoid contamination between entering dirty instruments and reprocessed clean instruments.** **Reprocessing of endoscopes (other than immediate precleaning) should not be performed in patient care areas** because of the risk of patient exposure to contaminated surfaces and devices | **IC and II** |
| 32 | Personnel assigned to reprocess endoscopes should receive **device-specific reprocessing instructions** (endoscope and/or AER manufacturer, as needed) to ensure proper cleaning and HLD or sterilization. **Competency testing of personnel who reprocess endoscopes should be performed and documented on a regular basis** — at commencement of use, **at least annually**, any time a breach is identified, when a major technique or new endoscope or reprocessing equipment is introduced, and in the context of local quality control efforts. Training and competency testing should **include recognition of excessive wear or damage to instruments**. **Temporary personnel should not be allowed to reprocess endoscopes until competency has been established** | **IA** |
| 33 | All personnel using chemicals should be **educated about the biologic and chemical hazards** present while performing procedures that use disinfectants, and should have **competency documented** with regard to use of the specific HLD or sterilization agents used in their practice setting | **IC** |
| 34 | **Personal protective equipment** (gloves, gowns, eyewear, respiratory protection devices) should be **readily available and should be used, as appropriate**, to protect workers from exposure to chemicals, blood or other potentially infectious material | **IC** |
| 35 | **Healthcare facilities should ensure that users can readily identify whether and when an endoscope has been reprocessed** | **II** |
| 36 | **The use of routine environmental microbiologic testing of endoscopes for quality assurance has not been established but warrants further study** | **NR** |
| 37 | **If environmental microbiologic testing is performed, standard microbiologic techniques per CDC guidance should be used** | **II** |
| 38 | **Reprocessing of nonendoscopic devices, accessories and attachments should adhere to manufacturers' recommendations** | **IC and II** |
| 39 | **Standard infection prevention practices for aseptic administration of medications**, including injectable agents and sedation and analgesia, **should be used** | **IC** |
| 40 | **In the event of an outbreak caused by a suspected infectious or chemical etiology, environmental sampling should be performed according to standard outbreak investigation protocols** | **IA** |
| 41 | **Endoscopy-related infections should be reported to all of the following:** (a) the persons responsible for infection control at the institution, with notification of the referring physician and potentially affected patients as appropriate; (b) **the appropriate public health agency** (state or local health department as required by state law or regulation); (c) **the FDA**, via Medical Device Reports submitted through MedWatch, which can be reviewed on the FDA's MAUDE database; (d) **the manufacturer(s)** of the endoscope, disinfectant/sterilant, and AER (if used) | **IB and IC** |

### Unresolved issues the document names explicitly

These are flagged in a dedicated section as lacking the data to support a recommendation:

- **Shelf life / "hang time"** — the interval of storage after which endoscopes should be reprocessed before use. Available data suggest contamination during appropriate storage for **7 to 21 days** is negligible, unassociated with duration, predominantly on the **exterior** of instruments, and involves **common skin organisms rather than significant pathogens**. One study cultured endoscopes at **0, 7, 14 and 21 days** — nonpathogenic organisms in 29 of 96 cultures, with no relationship to time frame, location or endoscope type, and only 4 potential pathogens in low titers, also unrelated to time. Another cultured colonoscopes at **3 and 5 days and 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 weeks** with no medically significant growth. **Reuse within 21 days, and perhaps even 56 days, appears to be safe**, but the data are insufficient to set a maximum. **Where instruments are used near otherwise sterile regions — the biliary tree, pancreas, peritoneal space — reprocessing within this interval *before use* may be advisable**; that means reprocessing before reuse, **not** reprocessing stored endoscopes at fixed intervals without scheduled reuse. **SGNA currently espouses a maximum storage interval without reprocessing of 7 days**; APIC is silent on reprocessing intervals; **AORN recommends a facility-level multidisciplinary risk assessment** to set the maximum.
- **Optimal frequency for replacing (1) clean water bottles and tubing for insufflation of air and lens wash water, and (2) waste vacuum canisters and suction tubing** — not determined. FDA nonbinding draft guidance stipulates that the **most distal device or tubing nearest the source of contamination, and the accompanying exposed antibackflow valves, require replacement or reprocessing before reuse** — so for air/water (lens wash) channels the **air/water valve needs replacing per procedure, but the water bottle feeding the channel can be changed daily**. **One-way valves for inline high-volume water flushing require replacement on a daily basis. Suction valves require replacement per procedure.** The interval for exchange of vacuum tubing and waste canisters is incompletely addressed. **AORN recommends water and irrigation bottles be high-level disinfected or sterilized at least daily.**
- **Microbiologic testing of endoscopes after reprocessing, during storage, or before use** — not advised in current US standards, though surveillance culturing as a quality-assurance measure is advised in several international guidelines. The **American Society for Microbiology has advised against** performance of endoscope cultures by hospital clinical diagnostic laboratories, out of concern that recipients of negative cultures will **misinterpret them as evidence of sterility**; they advise such cultures be submitted to reference laboratories licensed to perform environmental cultures. The **CDC methodology was designed for outbreak investigation and is not validated for quality surveillance of reprocessing adequacy or confirmation of sterility**; in May 2015 the FDA advisory panel concluded the CDC interim surveillance guidance "is not sufficient in the current form to be implemented by healthcare facilities as a best practice" and that "more data and validation testing is needed before a surveillance program should be implemented." **Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) residue testing** is the most widely studied alternative indicator of cleaning adequacy before HLD; methodology and threshold values are **not standardized** and results **do not closely correlate with terminal HLD outcome**, but they do appear to correlate with clearance of bioburden by precleaning and manual cleaning — so ATP testing **may be useful for training, competency testing and spot surveillance of the cleaning steps before HLD**.
- **"Culture and quarantine"** — one major US medical center resolved an ongoing duodenoscope transmission outbreak by culturing after reprocessing of every instrument, storing them for **48 hours** pending return of cultures demonstrating absence of pathogenic organisms before reuse. Whether this is practically or financially feasible for all centers **remains to be determined**.
- **Relatively new technologies for automated washing and HLD with limited or no prior manual washing or brushing** have been FDA-cleared on the basis of laboratory testing and limited clinical use. For duodenoscopes the **FDA currently advises that "the AER cleaning cycle can only be used as a supplement to thorough manual cleaning according to the duodenoscope manufacturer's instructions."**
- **Endoscope durability and longevity** are incompletely understood. One study correlated **greater surface adhesion of bacteria with simulated repetitive use equivalent to a number of years of high-volume use**. Two reports of duodenoscope contamination were related to **unexpected wear and/or vendor-identified need for repair, even in fully functional instruments that passed local user assessment of function**.
- **Optimal methods for cleaning and disinfection, or sterilization, of duodenoscopes** remain incompletely defined. The FDA Advisory Panel concluded that **"duodenoscopes and AERs do not provide a reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness"**; although some panelists held that HLD is adequate when done properly, most believed duodenoscopes should be **reclassified as critical devices** in the Spaulding classification, shifting expectations toward sterilization. **No modality is efficacious, efficient and cost-effective for duodenoscope sterilization today.** At present the only low-temperature sterilization technique available is **prolonged exposure to ethylene oxide** — costly, inefficient, associated with potential toxicity to personnel, **cannot sterilize residual gross soil**, warrants concern about endoscope durability, is not widely available, and **is not FDA-approved for reprocessing endoscopes**.

### Practical notes the document records

- **FDA-cleared labels for HLD with greater than 2% glutaraldehyde at 25°C advise contact times ranging from 20 to 90 minutes** depending on the product, while multiple scientific studies and professional organizations support the efficacy of **greater than 2% glutaraldehyde at 20 minutes at 20°C**. The document presents this as an example of FDA labeling or manufacturer guidance lagging behind evolving data; where an alternative practice is demonstrated optimal by several well-designed scientific studies and endorsed by multiple professional societies, **it can be considered for use by an organization**.
- **In October 2015 the FDA mandated that all 3 duodenoscope manufacturers prepare postmarket surveillance studies** evaluating whether and how often end users can appropriately complete cleaning and reprocessing instructions, and how often they accomplish complete clearance of contaminating organisms when standard techniques are used.
- **Users should always refer to FDA labeling and manufacturers' instructions for device-specific reprocessing guidance.** Accrediting bodies will typically survey for performance in accordance with this guidance.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No contradiction with [[aga-2017-mstf-endoscope-reprocessing-commentary]]** — the commentary is a commentary *on this document*, and the two agree. Where the commentary paraphrased, this guideline's own wording now governs the page.
- ⚠ **One wording difference worth noting.** The commentary states that MSTF 2016 recommendation 5 requires manual cleaning **"within 60 minutes of endoscope use"** with the interval monitored. **This document's recommendation 5 says "within the manufacturer's recommended time frame"** and directs the reader to the manufacturer's delayed-processing instructions if that interval is exceeded; it names no number. The 60-minute figure is the commentary's. The page carries the guideline's wording as the rule and attributes the 60 minutes to the commentary.
- **Competency interval conflict, also noted on the page:** this guideline requires competency testing **at least annually** (recommendation 32); the AGA commentary records that the AGA has encouraged **semi-annual** demonstration. Both stand — the guideline is the floor.
- **The document grades 2 of its 41 recommendations NR** — shelf life (25) and routine environmental microbiologic testing for quality assurance (36). Neither is a recommendation, and neither should be rendered as one.
- **Out of scope by the document's own statement:** sterilization for extraluminal applications (natural orifice transluminal endoscopic surgery, intraoperative endoscopy through open or laparoscopic access), and reprocessing of affiliated flexible, rigid or semirigid endoscopes used in other procedures such as cystoscopy or bronchoscopy.
- **Dated in one respect:** the duodenoscope-design discussion predates disposable-cap and single-use duodenoscope designs. [[aga-2017-mstf-endoscope-reprocessing-commentary]] already carries the 2016–2017 disposable-endoscope data the page uses.
