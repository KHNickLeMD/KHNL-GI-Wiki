## Summary

- Introductory article of the Rome V compendium: definition and classification of disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI), the new **Rome Clinical Criteria**, key changes since Rome IV (2016), the biopsychosocial model, and a general care approach
- **Does not print the full diagnostic criteria** (symptom items, frequency, duration) for individual DGBI — those are in the Rome V chapters/book. The only criteria thresholds it states are the *changes*: irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) pain/discomfort frequency, fecal incontinence episode count, dyssynergic defecation test count
- Process: 7 years (2019–2026); 144 investigators/clinicians, 27 countries, 25 committees; consensus used when evidence was insufficient
- Classification: **34 adult and 22 pediatric DGBI** (Table 2); adults in six domains A–F; pediatrics reorganized by anatomy (G upper, H lower/biliary) instead of age
- Management: biopsychosocial; therapeutic patient–provider relationship (12 steps); treatment intensity matched to symptom severity (mild / moderate / severe; Table 4 IBS profile)

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definition (Table 1)

- DGBI = disorders with gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms related to any combination of: motility disturbance, visceral hypersensitivity, altered mucosal and immune function, altered gut microbiota, altered central nervous system (CNS) processing
- "DGBI" introduced in Rome IV (2016) to replace "functional gastrointestinal disorder" (FGID); both acceptable then. Rome V: FGID should no longer be used; "functional" removed from diagnoses where an alternative existed (some names keep it)

### Table 3 — Rome Clinical Criteria

| Criterion | Description |
|---|---|
| Qualitative symptom criteria | The qualitative features of the Rome V diagnostic criteria must be met |
| Bothersomeness | Symptoms bothersome enough to seek care or interfere with daily activity and quality of life |
| Frequency | Lower than the traditional criteria threshold is permitted |
| Duration | Rome V 6-month duration not required; **8 weeks suggested** to exclude other diagnoses. Exceptions: clinician satisfied that evaluation excludes other disorders, or infrequent-episode disorders (e.g., cyclic vomiting syndrome, proctalgia fugax) |

- Rationale — limitations of standard research criteria in practice:
  - **Subdiagnostic group:** global study (>50,000 people): 41.4% met Rome criteria for ≥1 DGBI, 33.4% had no GI symptoms; the remaining ~one-fourth had GI symptoms without a Rome diagnosis — with poorer health-related quality of life and more anxiety, depression, health care use, and life/work impairment than people without GI symptoms
  - **Multi-region symptom patterns:** current Rome criteria do not include entities spanning >1 anatomic area; may be considered overlapping DGBI
  - **Overlap:** 5931 adults, 3 countries — 35% met criteria for 1 DGBI; >one-third of these had ≥1 other DGBI in a different region; more overlaps → worse quality of life, more treatments and abdominal surgeries
- When organic disease is sufficiently ruled out, qualitative features are met, and patient and provider judge symptoms bothersome → frequency and duration criteria can be eliminated or reduced
- Rome V diagnostic algorithms (3rd edition book) and Multidimensional Clinical Profile (4th edition) are separate publications

### Changes for Rome V

**New adult diagnoses**

- **Inability to belch syndrome (B3c)** ("retrograde cricopharyngeal dysfunction"): common in practice, variable presentation; diagnosis *can be confirmed* by esophageal impedance manometry with sparkling-water provocation; successful treatment with botulinum toxin injection in the upper esophageal sphincter has been shown
- **Abdominal migraine (D2):** described in children (Rome pediatric criteria since Rome II), increasingly recognized in adults; placed in centrally mediated pain (episodic pain, symptom-free intervals, no bowel dysfunction)
- **Anorectal sensory dysfunction (F4):** F4a rectal hyposensitivity → decreased urge, straining, digital maneuvers; F4b rectal hypersensitivity → increased urge, prolonged or frequent toilet time; rectal sensitivity testing aids diagnosis; treated with biofeedback and balloon sensory training

**Pediatric** — reclassified from age (neonate/toddler ≤4 y; child/adolescent ≥5 y) to anatomic regions (G upper, H lower/biliary)

**Chapter changes**

- Patient Experience, Gender, Age, Race, and Social Determinants of Health chapter replaces Age, Gender, and Women's Health (adds lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer [LGBTQ] content, life stages, stigma, trauma, income inequality)
- **Esophageal:** Rome and Lyon consensuses synchronized to define gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and esophageal DGBI; mean nocturnal baseline impedance introduced as a mucosal-permeability metric; uses Chicago Classification v4.0 criteria for esophagogastric junction outflow obstruction; functional lumen imaging probe may uncover abnormalities missed by manometry
- **Gastroduodenal:** PDS postprandial pattern defined more explicitly; hierarchical stepwise algorithm separates PDS from epigastric pain syndrome and nausea/vomiting disorders; cyclic vomiting syndrome and cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome — timing of symptoms and intervals specified from recent cohort studies; rumination, supragastric and gastric belching kept; inability to belch added
- **Bowel — IBS:**
  1. Abdominal **discomfort** re-included (removed in Rome IV) — some patients, in some countries, have discomfort without pain
  2. Pain or discomfort **≥3 days per month in the last 3 months** (Rome IV: 1 day per week during the previous 3 months) — the higher Rome IV pain threshold dropped IBS prevalence from ~10% to 4%, selecting a more severe population than seen in practice
  3. Pain and discomfort **should not be continuous** — separates IBS from centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome (continuous pain)
- **Bowel — renamings:** "chronic constipation" replaces "functional constipation"; "functional abdominal bloating" replaces "functional abdominal bloating and distention" (bloating is the DGBI symptom; distention adds no diagnostic value); "unclassified bowel disorders" replaces "unspecified bowel disorders"
- **Centrally mediated GI pain:** abdominal migraine added; centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome split into 2 subcategories, both with continuous pain:
  - Category A (Rome IV entity): pain not associated with physiological events
  - Category B: pain modified by physiological events (worse after eating, relief with defecation) but still continuous — ~5× more common than A in epidemiologic and clinical studies; same diagnostic and treatment approach
- **Gallbladder and SOD:**
  - Typical biliary pain definition clarified and simplified
  - Dysfunctional gallbladder disorder: typical biliary pain is specific enough that cholescintigraphy to identify low gallbladder ejection fraction is no longer recommended; typical biliary pain strongly outperforms cholescintigraphy for predicting response to surgery
  - Watchful waiting rather than surgery recommended to reduce unneeded cholecystectomy **when symptoms are transient**
  - Biliary and pancreatic SOD: sphincter manometry removed from the supportive criteria (lack of sensitivity and reproducibility)
- **Anorectal:**
  1. Fecal incontinence: "**two or more episodes** of uncontrolled passage of fecal material" replaces "recurrent uncontrolled passage" (threshold frequency)
  2. "Functional defecation disorders" category removed — too broad; can include structural abnormalities (rectal prolapse, perineal descent)
  3. Dyssynergic defecation listed separately: requires **difficult evacuation symptoms** (e.g., straining, digital maneuvers) **plus 1 of 3 abnormal tests** (balloon expulsion, manometry, imaging) — Rome IV required 2 tests; electromyography (EMG) dropped (infrequent use); diagnosis relies on specific manometric patterns of abnormal evacuation incl. the rectoanal gradient
  4. Rectal hypo- and hypersensitivity added

### Biopsychosocial model (Figure 1)

- Early life (genetics, culture, environment — trauma, infection, parental behaviors) → psychosocial factors (life stress, personality trait, psychological state, coping/cognition, social support) and physiology (motility, sensation, immune dysfunction/inflammation, altered microflora, food/diet), linked via the brain–gut axis → DGBI presentation (symptoms, severity, comorbidity, behaviors) ↔ outcome (health care use, daily function, quality of life, costs); all bidirectional
- Childhood *Salmonella* infection can be an IBS risk factor in adulthood; genetic component (family/twin studies) — polygenic, not Mendelian
- Psychosocial disturbance is **not required for diagnosis**, but modifies physiology, illness experience, treatment choice, outcome. Four observations:
  1. Stress exacerbates GI symptoms and may contribute to DGBI onset (e.g., postinfection IBS or dyspepsia)
  2. Psychological distress and comorbidity strongly associated with DGBI — global study >54,000: psychological distress and somatic symptom severity in 37.5%; **4.45× higher odds** of ≥1 DGBI; DGBI + distress → more health care/medication use, worse quality of life
  3. DGBI has psychosocial consequences (illness itself is a stressor)
  4. Distress and maladaptive cognitions (catastrophizing, hypervigilance) perpetuate symptoms → visceral anxiety, lower sensation thresholds — the target of brain–gut behavioral treatments

### Physiology and brain–gut axis

- **Motility:** dysmotility can cause vomiting, diarrhea, constipation, pain, incontinence; greater motility response to stressors in DGBI; only partly correlates with symptoms
- **Visceral hypersensitivity:** lower pain threshold to balloon distention (visceral hyperalgesia), increased sensitivity to normal function (allodynia), sensitization with repeated distention
- **Immune/barrier:** after postinfection IBS and dyspepsia research — increased permeability, low-grade mast cell activation, cytokines → visceral hypersensitivity; barrier dysfunction a potential target
- **Microbiome:** reduced fecal diversity in IBS; modest effect of probiotics, variable effect of microbiota transplant, more benefit from periodic antibiotics in IBS; duodenal microbiota and *Helicobacter pylori* implicated in dyspeptic symptoms
- **Food/diet:** low fermentable oligo-, di-, monosaccharides and polyols (FODMAP) or gluten restriction may help some patients; no single diet fits all; food is a well-established trigger in PDS
- **Brain–gut axis:** bidirectional CNS–enteric nervous system (ENS) connection (autonomic, neuroendocrine, neurohumoral; also microbiota and barrier); descending "gate control" pain modulation impaired in DGBI; serotonin and noradrenaline are the targets of tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) and serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs); anterior cingulate cortex dysfunction in IBS; autonomic dysfunction (vagal/sympathetic); vagal stimulation devices emerging for IBS and functional dyspepsia

### Approach to care

**12 steps to enhance the therapeutic relationship**

1. Improve patient satisfaction and engage the patient (nonverbal communication, partner-like interaction)
2. Nondirective, nonjudgmental, patient-centered history
3. Find the immediate reason for the visit — new/worsening factors, fear of serious disease, stressors, psychiatric comorbidity, functional impairment, "hidden agenda" (e.g., narcotic or laxative abuse, litigation, disability claims)
4. Careful physical exam and cost-efficient investigation
5. Determine what the patient understands of the illness and their concerns
6. Elicit the patient's "illness schema," then explain the disorder accounting for their beliefs
7. Respond realistically to expectations for improvement
8. Link stressors and symptoms when consistent with the patient's beliefs
9. Set consistent limits
10. Involve the patient in treatment
11. Make recommendations consistent with patient interests
12. Establish an ongoing relationship (with you or primary care)

- Five evidence-based connection practices: prepare in advance; listen intently; agree on what matters most; connect with the patient's story; explore emotional cues

**Severity as a guide to treatment** (general guidance for IBS; may apply to other DGBI) — see Table 4 below

| Severity | Profile | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild** (~40%) | Mostly primary care; no significant functional or psychological impairment; pain minimal/mild; quality of life good | Education (IBS is a genuine disorder of an over-responsive GI system); reassurance — not perfunctory, not before needed tests; identify and reduce offending foods/medications, consider dietitian referral |
| **Moderate** (~30–35%) | Primary/secondary care; intermittent disruption; link to diet, travel, stress; more distress | Symptom diary 1–3 weeks; pharmacotherapy for distressing/impairing episodes by predominant symptom — as-needed (e.g., antispasmodic, loperamide) or continuous (e.g., secretagogue, neuromodulator); **brain–gut behavioral treatments recommended for motivated patients with moderate-to-severe symptoms** (cognitive behavior therapy, hypnosis, mindfulness, others) |
| **Severe** (~20–25%) | Referral practices; refractory; high psychosocial comorbidity, chronic impairment, ~10%+ work disability; may resist psychosocial treatment | Ongoing physician relationship with brief repeated visits — act on objective findings not demands; realistic goals (quality of life, not cure); shift responsibility to patient via options; focus on adjustment to chronic illness. **Central neuromodulators** (TCAs, SNRIs; selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs] ancillary — less effective for pain, help anxiety/depression) — recommended for chronic pain with impaired daily function, coexisting major or atypical depression, symptom anxiety, or panic attacks; may help without depression when pain dominates; poor response may reflect underdosing or no titration. Referral to a multidisciplinary DGBI treatment center |

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Criteria not printed:** this article gives only the Rome IV → V *changes*; full symptom items and thresholds for functional dyspepsia, chronic constipation, cyclic vomiting syndrome, cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome, rumination, F4, and others are in the Rome V chapters. Wiki pages carrying older Rome III/IV item lists ([[asge-2014-constipation]], [[aga-2019-opioid-induced-constipation]], [[aga-2024-cvs]], [[aga-2024-chs]]) remain the operative criteria text until the chapters are ingested.
- **Dyssynergic defecation test count:** Rome V needs 1 of 3 abnormal tests plus symptoms; [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders|American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2021]] requires both anorectal manometry and balloon expulsion. Both tier 1; Rome V newer → wiki follows 1-of-3 and notes the ACG rule.
- **IBS criteria:** [[acg-2020-ibs|ACG 2020]] uses Rome IV (pain ≥1 day/week, onset ≥6 months); Rome V lowers frequency to ≥3 days/month, re-adds discomfort, requires non-continuous pain. Rome V newer → governs.
- **Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome:** [[aga-2024-cvs]]/[[aga-2024-chs]] frame it as a cyclic vomiting syndrome subtype; Rome V lists it as a separate adult entity (B2c), still nested under cyclic vomiting syndrome in pediatrics (G3b.i).
- **Brain–gut therapy candidacy:** Rome V recommends brain–gut behavioral treatment for motivated patients with moderate-to-severe symptoms; [[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology|American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) 2018]] holds these therapies are less effective with comorbid psychopathology and that pronounced emotional symptoms be treated first.
- IBS severity strata (Table 4) are a "proposed" profile from a Rome Working Team report; the FBDSI/IBS-SSS item scoring is not reproduced.
