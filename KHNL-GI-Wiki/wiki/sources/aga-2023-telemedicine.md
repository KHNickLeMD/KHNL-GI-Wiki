## Summary

Tier-1 AGA Institute Clinical Practice Update. The page-1 DESCRIPTION states the purpose is "to review the available evidence and provide expert advice regarding best practices for use of telemedicine in gastroenterology and hepatology." METHODS: "This CPU was commissioned and approved by the AGA Institute Clinical Practice Updates Committee (CPUC) and the AGA Governing Board to provide timely guidance on a topic of high clinical importance to the AGA membership and underwent internal peer review through standard procedures of *Gastroenterology*. This expert commentary incorporates important, as well as recently published, studies in this field, and it reflects the experiences of the authors who are active gastroenterologists and hepatologists with extensive experience using telemedicine in clinical practice."

**Format note — no numbered Best Practice Advice.** This is a CPU *Commentary*, not a Best Practice Advice CPU or Expert Review. The document contains **no numbered recommendations and no GRADE or evidence grades of any kind**. Its operative guidance is carried by two tables (Table 1, appropriate uses of each telemedicine format; Table 2, best practices for telemedicine), one bulleted documentation list, and the narrative under four question headings. Scope is **outpatient** gastroenterology; non-telemedicine forms of telehealth are explicitly out of scope.

Content covers: definitions (Federation of State Medical Boards vs Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services); patient and provider preferences, including race-, age-, income-, and insurance-based disparities in uptake of video vs telephone visits; comparative outcomes data suggesting telemedicine suits stable chronic disease better than new acute presentations; which gastroenterology patients are best and least suited to virtual visits; and the regulatory, licensure, documentation, billing, trainee-supervision, and clinic-workflow infrastructure required to deliver it.

The authors conclude telemedicine is "an integral part of outpatient gastrointestinal care in the United States," accepted by patients and providers, with limited data suggesting it does not produce inferior outcomes versus face-to-face care but "may be better suited for chronic disease management." They call for further study of long-term outcomes and of the optimal in-person-to-telemedicine ratio by disease state.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definitions
- **Telemedicine** (Federation of State Medical Boards): "the practice of medicine using electronic communication, information technology, or other means between a physician in one location and a patient in another location, with or without an intervening health care provider."
- **Telemedicine for reimbursement** (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services): "two-way, real time interactive communication between the patient and the physician or practitioner at [a] distant site that includes, at minimum, audio and video equipment."
- **Telehealth** (broader parent term): "use of electronic information and telecommunication technologies to support and promote long-distance clinical health care, patient and professional health-related education, public health, and health administration." Telemedicine is one form of telehealth.
- Figure 1 lists six formats of virtual care around the gastroenterology provider: e-consults, telemedicine group visits, traditional in-person care, inpatient virtual care, telemedicine, remote patient monitoring.

### Patient and provider preferences
- Large national US survey, March 2021 (n = 2080): most patients (67%) reported ongoing interest and willingness to use video visits.
- 2020 survey of gastroenterology telemedicine patients: >80% indicated their medical concerns were addressed and that they would be willing to participate in telemedicine visits in the future for their gastrointestinal care.
- Australian study in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD): high satisfaction with telehealth appointments; **significantly decreased absenteeism with telehealth vs face-to-face visits (20.9% vs 60.4%)**.
- Survey of 509 gastroenterologists from geographically diverse community settings: high satisfaction; **>70% of providers expected telemedicine to remain part of their future care**.

### Disparities in access (explicit CPU position)
- Preference for video visits varies: younger adults, individuals with higher income, and Hispanic and Latino patients "may be more likely than older adults, individuals with lower income, and non-Hispanic White or Black or African Americans to prefer video visits."
- Within gastroenterology populations (IBD, chronic liver disease): "older patients, Black and African American patients, and patients with Medicaid and Medicare insurance are more likely to complete a telephone visit compared with a video visit."
- "Racial and socioeconomic disparities in access to telemedicine, in particular to video visits, should be recognized and barriers to accessing telemedicine should be examined and addressed. When feasible, ambulatory practices, institutions, and health systems should provide technical solutions and individualized patient support to reduce these barriers to accessing telemedicine."

### Outcomes, virtual vs in person
- Large cohort of commercially insured US individuals, first year of COVID-19 (Hatef et al): impact differed by reason for visit — **new acute condition** presenting via telemedicine → *more* likely to require a follow-up in-person visit or emergency department encounter vs face-to-face; **chronic condition** via telemedicine → *less* likely to require subsequent hospitalization. Interpreted as telemedicine being better suited to chronic stable conditions than acute illness.
- Liver transplantation: veterans evaluated via telemedicine had reduced time from referral to hepatologist evaluation and to transplant listing. Randomized controlled trial of 106 liver transplant recipients in a telemedicine-based home management program → lower 90-day readmission rate, improved physical function, and better general health vs standard of care.
- IBD: a small study found quality-of-care metrics did not differ after initiation of a telemedicine program. **Meta-analysis of 17 randomized controlled trials (n = 2571)**: patients with IBD enrolled in a telemedicine program had higher IBD-specific quality of life than standard of care, with fewer clinic visits per patient. **No differences** in remission rates, disease activity, or self-efficacy. The quality-of-life benefit was considerably different in adolescents but not adults, suggesting further work is needed to target telemedicine to the appropriate population.

### Patient selection
- **Excellent candidates:** patients with stable chronic digestive diseases, "including but not limited to, gastroesophageal reflux, irritable bowel syndrome, chronic constipation, chronic liver disease, chronic pancreatitis, and IBD."
- Telemedicine is "often appropriate" when a change in therapy is being contemplated for any chronic illness and the patient requests an additional visit for more information.
- Patients in remote locations are ideal candidates **provided they have good internet broadband access for video visits**. Scotland clinic, 2016–2017: telemedicine was appropriate for **99% of patients with IBD**, with **>90% eligible for further telemedicine visits**; in this **88-patient cohort, >44,000 miles of travel and 1100 hours of time were saved**.
- Telemedicine has been used readily to provide mental health services among IBD patients, and can help with complex care coordination such as pediatric-to-adult IBD transition — a proof-of-concept study blending in-person and telemedicine visits achieved successful transition to adult care after 20 weeks with **86% retention**.
- **Poor candidates for audiovisual visits:** patients with limited ability to access or use technology (smartphone or other device) or with poor to no broadband internet access — "although they may be candidates for audio-only visits." The CPU acknowledges this same population may have difficulty accessing in-person visits as well.
- **Better served in person:** patients with moderate to severe symptoms and/or a physical symptom that requires an examination. Many practices also require new patients to be seen in person first, to integrate multidisciplinary care and establish better rapport.
- **Optimal ratio** of telemedicine to in-person visits depends on practice setting (regulated vs unregulated space), geography (rural vs suburban vs urban), and complexity of care. Hospital-based ambulatory clinics will likely prefer more in-person visits due to loss of facility fees; rural locations will likely shift toward more telemedicine. The CPU acknowledges "what is 'best practice' in one practice or setting may be different from other practices or settings" — some practices continue to offer telehealth for new patients without inferior care.
- In-person visits remain valuable to examine the patient, obtain vital signs, facilitate multidisciplinary care, and offer point-of-care diagnostics and vaccinations — "however, as remote diagnostics evolve, including the ability for remote physical examinations, even those advantages of in-person visits will dissipate." A joint gastroenterology society statement emphasized the practicality of a comprehensive virtual physical examination.

### Regulatory, reimbursement, and documentation
- **Main barrier to virtual care:** "the requirement in many states to be licensed in the state that the patient resides." These requirements were eased early in COVID-19 and have **returned in most states** as the pandemic eased. Some practices may support clinicians obtaining licenses in surrounding states; the CPU states "some regulatory compromise is needed so that providers are not required to maintain licenses in multiple states."
- **Reimbursement:** during COVID-19 the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services reimbursed audiovisual telehealth visits at the same rate as in-person visits, and most commercial payers mirrored this. "The level of reimbursement for audio-only visits is variable and unlikely to remain on par with in-person and telemedicine visits going forward."
- **Documentation** requirements are similar to in-person visits, with several additional components suggested. Verbatim list of important items to document:
  - "patient consent to telemedicine;"
  - "method of telemedicine (secure 2-way interactive video connection or phone call);"
  - "patient location;"
  - "provider location;"
  - "listing all clinical participants' roles and action; and"
  - "other individuals present at the visit."
- Additional best practices named in the text: verifying the patient is in a private setting; conducting the visit in a private office with a closed door; quality of the audiovisual connection; establishing a backup method to conclude the visit if the initial method fails; documentation of the quality of the visit.
- **Billing:** variable by payer. In general should include the same CPT code used for in-person visits (eg, 99202–5 or 99211–15) with a specific Place of Service code and telehealth CPT modifier (eg, 95, GQ or GT) based on payer specifications. **Billing based on time requires that total face-to-face time with the patient is included in visit documentation.** The **2021 coding changes eliminated the requirement to document physical examination findings to support coding levels**, further facilitating reimbursement for telemedicine visits.

### Trainees and clinic workflow
- Trainee use depends on physical location: **trainee in the same location as the provider** → the provider can enter the physical room and complete the telemedicine visit with the trainee. **Trainee and provider in distant locations** → the provider can discuss the case using a separate platform (eg, FaceTime) before entering the virtual room with the patient. Some institutions require a modified teaching physician statement for telemedicine encounters with a trainee.
- **Pre-visit contact by office staff** is recommended: troubleshoot technical issues and confirm the patient can connect; set up assistive services such as an interpreter; complete standard practices such as medication review — preserving uninterrupted, focused provider time during the visit.
- One author's practice created a **"virtual care clinic"** with remote staff — a full complement of trained nursing staff supporting telemedicine visits, communicating with providers via instant messaging, updating medical history and completing medication reconciliation. Stated benefits: in-person clinical staff are not distracted/burdened by remote care, maximal room utilization for brick-and-mortar clinics, and expansion of clinical practice without new space.
- A telemedicine clinic can incorporate other providers (dietitians, psychologists, psychiatrists) who already use telemedicine routinely; **"however, most practices are not able to integrate other providers synchronously into the telemedicine visit."**

### Conclusions (verbatim position)
- "Limited data suggest that telemedicine does not result in inferior patient outcomes compared with care delivered face to face but may be better suited for chronic disease management."
- "Additional studies are needed to verify long-term outcomes associated with telemedicine compared with in-person visits and to determine the optimal ratio of in-person vs telemedicine visits for various disease states."

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **No contradictions with existing wiki content.** No other ingested source addresses telemedicine, visit-modality selection, licensure, or telehealth billing.
- **No graded or numbered advice.** Unlike most AGA CPUs on this wiki, this Commentary issues no numbered Best Practice Advice statements and assigns no evidence grades — every claim is expert commentary supported by cited observational studies, surveys, and one meta-analysis. Weight accordingly against any future graded guideline on virtual care.
- **Open questions the CPU names explicitly:** long-term outcomes of telemedicine vs in-person care; the optimal ratio of in-person to telemedicine visits by disease state; why the IBD quality-of-life benefit appeared in adolescents but not adults.
- **Time-sensitive content.** Licensure and reimbursement statements describe the post-COVID-19 US regulatory landscape as of late 2022; the CPU itself anticipates change ("unlikely to remain on par… going forward", "some regulatory compromise is needed").
- The CPU does not define a threshold for "moderate to severe symptoms" that should trigger an in-person visit, nor does it quantify the broadband speed required for a video visit.
