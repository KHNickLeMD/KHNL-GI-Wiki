## Summary

The derivation paper for the **Stapfer classification** of duodenal and periampullary perforation after endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). A retrospective chart review of **1,413 ERCP procedures** performed at Los Angeles County/USC Medical Center between June 1993 and June 1998 identified **14 periduodenal perforations (1.0%)**. The authors' aim was explicitly to settle a management dispute: some groups advocated routine nonsurgical management, others mandatory surgical exploration, and those favouring a selective approach had **not elaborated distinct criteria** for choosing. The paper's contribution is the classification plus a list of surgical indications.

The central observation is that **mechanism of injury correlates with radiologic findings, and the two considered together are the best guide to surgery.** That yields four types in descending order of severity, keyed to anatomic location and mechanism: type I (duodenal wall, scope-induced), type II (peri-Vaterian, sphincterotomy-induced), type III (distal bile duct, wire/basket-induced) and type IV (retroperitoneal air alone, which the authors argue is **not a true perforation** at all).

Equally important is what the paper found *useless* for triage. **Fever and leukocytosis alone were explicitly not considered justification for surgery**; leukocytosis was present in 9 of 10 patients tested regardless of outcome. **Abdominal examination was not helpful in the first few hours**, and because these injuries are often retroperitoneal, a benign examination does **not** exclude the need for surgery. Early peritonitis should dictate surgery; peritonitis appearing late was a marker of a poor outcome rather than a trigger. A further warning: **3 of 14 patients (21%) thought at ERCP to have a duodenal diverticulum were later found to have large perforations.**

Outcomes drive the urgency. Eight patients were managed nonsurgically at the outset; **five succeeded** (median length of stay 17 days) and **three failed (38%)**, requiring salvage operations with a mean of 3.6 reoperations each, a **median length of stay of 111 days**, and one death. Six patients went to primary surgery (all within 4 hours) with one death and a median stay of 14 days. The authors note that in two of the three failures, antibiotics and nasogastric suction **"produced no significant improvement and possibly delayed definitive management."**

⚠ **Evidence quality is low.** This is a retrospective, single-institution series of **14 patients** from the 1990s, predating modern endoscopic closure devices entirely — there is no over-the-scope clip, no endoscopic suturing and no covered stent anywhere in it. Its durable contribution is the **classification vocabulary**, which is still in use; its surgical thresholds should be read against current endoscopic-closure guidance.

## Key Findings / Claims

**The Stapfer classification (Figure 1) — four types in descending order of severity, by mechanism and anatomic location:**

| Type | Location | Mechanism | Typical size / character | Authors' management |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | Lateral or **medial** duodenal wall | The **endoscope** | Large, **remote from the ampulla**; large persistent retroperitoneal or intraperitoneal contrast leak | **Immediate surgery in all** |
| **II** | **Peri-Vaterian** (periampullary) | Sphincterotomy | Varied severity but usually **more discrete**; often seals spontaneously | Nonsurgical acceptable if minimal/sealed leak and no fluid collection; **43%** needed surgery |
| **III** | **Distal bile duct** | **Wire or basket** instrumentation near an obstructing entity (e.g. a stone) | Often **small** | Nonsurgical acceptable on the same conditions as type II |
| **IV** | **Retroperitoneal air alone** | Probably **compressed air** used to maintain luminal patency | **Not a true perforation** | **No surgical intervention**; no additional treatment or workup if the abdominal examination is normal and no extravasation is seen at ERCP |

- **Type I is the only type the authors send to surgery on the classification alone.** Types II–IV are triaged on the radiographic and clinical findings below.
- **Types II and III tend to seal spontaneously** and therefore lend themselves to nonsurgical management — but surgical indications can still exist for them (fluid collections, retained stones or hardware).
- **Nonsurgical management succeeded in 5 patients with type II or III lesions (62%).**

**Surgical indications after ERCP-related duodenal perforation (Table 4) — any one of five:**

1. **Large extravasation of contrast at ERCP**, defined as **incomplete dissipation of contrast after 1 minute** on follow-up plain film. If extravasation is *small* (**complete dissipation after 1 minute**), perform an upper gastrointestinal contrast study (UGI) at **2 to 8 hours**; **if that shows extravasation, explore surgically.**
2. **Any follow-up computed tomography (CT) scan showing a retroperitoneal or peritoneal fluid collection** consistent with perforation — **not** with pancreatitis.
3. **Documented ERCP perforation with cholelithiasis, choledocholithiasis, or retained hardware** (e.g. retained stones, basket, wire).
4. **Massive subcutaneous emphysema after ERCP** with what appears at endoscopy to be a **large duodenal diverticulum**.
5. **Failure of nonsurgical management.**

**The two contrast definitions that carry the whole scheme:**

- **Large / persistent leak = extravasated contrast still present after 1 minute.**
- **Minimal leak = extravasated contrast completely disappears within 60 seconds.**

**Surveillance protocol when a conservative approach is chosen:**

- **Repeat the UGI within 8 hours** to confirm the initial impression.
- **Double-contrast CT at 8 hours and at 48 hours** — confirm the leak remains sealed and exclude developing fluid collections.
- **Repeat regularly until clinical recovery appears certain.** The authors stress the decision to avoid surgery is **dynamic** and should be re-evaluated "whenever the clinical circumstances demonstrate even the slightest untoward development."
- **Type II, III or IV injury with retained stones and unrelieved biliary obstruction, or with foreign bodies, should be explored** even absent other indications.

**What does *not* decide management:**

- **Fever and leukocytosis alone were not considered justification for surgery.** Leukocytosis was present in 9/10 patients in whom a white cell count was obtained; 4 had temperatures >101 °F, 4 had low-grade fevers, 6 were afebrile.
- **Abdominal examination was not helpful within the first few hours.** 10/14 (71%) had only mild tenderness at presentation. Because the injuries are often retroperitoneal, **a negative abdominal examination should not exclude surgery.**
- **Early peritonitis should dictate surgery**; 4/14 (29%) developed generalized peritonitis between 2 and 72 hours (median 6 h). Peritonitis as a *late* finding was associated with poor outcome.

**Diagnosis:**

- **11/14 (78–79%) were made or suspected during the ERCP itself**, usually by a limited contrast study through the endoscope. The remainder were diagnosed by chest radiograph (free air), by massive subcutaneous emphysema with tension pneumothorax during the procedure, or by clinical sepsis 72 hours later.
- **4 patients (28%) underwent a formal gastrograffin UGI** when perforation was suspected but not proven at ERCP — 2 had extensive extravasation, 2 had none.
- ⚠ **3 patients (21%) erroneously thought to have a duodenal diverticulum were later found to have large perforations.**

**Mechanism and risk context:**

- **Cannulation was considered difficult by the endoscopist in 10 of 14 patients.**
- Presumed source of injury: **sphincterotome 6/14** (all 6 had a sphincterotomy), **endoscope 5/14**, **guidewire 3/14**.
- Background rates quoted by the authors: ERCP-related perforation **~1%** of patients; the injury carries a **death rate of 16%–18%**; ERCP major complication rate approaches **10%** and procedural death rate **1.0%–1.5%**.

**Outcomes by management route:**

| Route | n | Deaths | Median length of stay |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nonsurgical, successful** | 5 | 0 | **17 days** (range 7–38) |
| **Nonsurgical, failed → salvage surgery** | 3 (**38%** of the 8) | 1 | **111 days** |
| **Primary surgery** (all operated <4 h) | 6 | 1 | **14 days** |

- **Failed nonsurgical management carried high rates of death, complications and reoperation** (mean **3.6** reoperations per patient) — the paper's main argument for aggressive early triage.
- **None of the 6 primary-surgery patients required reoperation for duodenal leakage.** Two developed a retroperitoneal abscess (one needing open drainage).
- **The magnitude of the operation was proportionate to the degree of injury.** Type I lesions and nonsurgical failures often required **pyloric exclusion, gastrojejunostomy, tube duodenostomy and extensive drainage or débridement**; patients operated for type II/III indications needed only **stone or foreign-body removal, biliary diversion and periduodenal drainage**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No contradiction with [[aga-2021-gi-perforations-endoscopic-management|AGA 2021]], but a 21-year gap in therapeutic options, and AGA 2021 governs management.** AGA 2021 is both newer and higher-priority (clinical practice update vs retrospective series), and its size-keyed endoscopic closure rule stands. Stapfer's "all type I to immediate surgery" predates over-the-scope clips, endoscopic suturing and covered self-expanding metal stents; AGA 2021 instead advises **urgent surgical consultation for type 1 >3 cm while the feasibility of endoscopic closure is assessed**, and reports **88%–100%** clip closure for duodenal defects <13 mm. The wiki asserts AGA's position for management and keeps Stapfer for **typing** and for the radiographic triage criteria AGA does not supply.
- **Type IV is a contested entity.** The authors argue retroperitoneal air alone is not a true perforation and needs nothing. This aligns with the **14% (7/50)** asymptomatic post-ERCP retroperitoneal air figure already on [[endoscopic-management-of-perforation]], but the same page records that type 2 injuries can still progress to retroperitoneal abscess, peritonitis and death — so the benign reading applies to **air alone with a normal examination and no extravasation**, not to retroperitoneal air generally.
- **n=14 is the whole evidence base for a classification in routine use.** The per-type management rules rest on 1–6 patients each (type I: 5; type II: 6; type III: 3 by the published tables). No prospective validation is presented, and the authors revised their own surgical indications *during* the study after analysing their nonsurgical failures.
- **Figures 1–5 are raster images with no text layer.** Figure 1 is the classification schematic (and the paper notes **type IV is not shown** in it); Figure 2 is an endoscopic view of a type I perforation; Figures 3 and 4 are plain films of a large (type I) and a minimal (type III) leak; Figure 5 is a periduodenal fluid collection on CT.
