## Summary

American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) Standards of Practice guideline on endoscopic evaluation and management of **ampullary** (major duodenal papilla) and **nonampullary duodenal adenomas**. Both can be sporadic or arise in genetic syndromes (familial adenomatous polyposis [FAP], Peutz-Jeghers) and both carry malignant potential. Endoscopic resection has become a viable alternative to surgery (pancreaticoduodenectomy/Whipple or transduodenal ampullectomy) in selected cases; although not directly compared, local surgical excision appears to have a higher adverse-event rate than endoscopy.

Pre-treatment evaluation: a **side-viewing duodenoscope** is generally needed to visualize the papilla; biopsy of suspicious lesions is required before resection to confirm adenoma and exclude cancer (sample away from the pancreatic duct orifice to avoid pancreatitis; ≥6 specimens or sampling within 10 days of sphincterotomy reduces false-negatives, which range 16–60% for carcinoma). **Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)** is recommended for large ampullary lesions or duodenal polyps with features concerning for malignancy before resection, and is superior to computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)/transabdominal ultrasound (US) for tumor (T) staging; intraductal US is discussed alongside EUS but is not named in any recommendation. **Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)** with biliary and pancreatic duct evaluation is performed at the time of papillectomy to assess intraductal extension; <1 cm of ductal extension does not necessarily preclude endoscopic therapy.

Endoscopic resection of the papilla is termed **endoscopic papillectomy** (true "ampullectomy" is a surgical term requiring ductal reimplantation). En bloc snare resection with electrocautery is preferred when the lesion can be fully ensnared (shorter time, complete pathology, minimizes later residual neoplasia within submucosal fibrosis); piecemeal resection is used for lesions >2 cm. Submucosal injection, chromoendoscopy, sphincterotomy, and ablative therapies (argon plasma coagulation [APC] most common, 50–60 W) are adjuncts. **Prophylactic pancreatic duct stenting plus rectal indomethacin** reduce post-procedure pancreatitis. Papillectomy success is 46–92% with recurrence up to 33%; all patients require post-resection surveillance.

For **nonampullary duodenal polyps**, the approach mirrors right-colon polypectomy given the thin duodenal wall; endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) techniques have been described. **Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) of duodenal adenomas, especially beyond the duodenal bulb, is not recommended** (high perforation risk; 23% and 35% in two series). Lesions with >33% circumferential involvement should be considered for surgery. Patients with sporadic ampullary or duodenal adenomas have higher colorectal neoplasia risk and should be offered screening colonoscopy. Search covered 1980–December 2013; recommendations graded by Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) evidence quality.

## Key Findings / Claims

- **Surgical morbidity/mortality:** Whipple/transduodenal ampullectomy associated with anastomotic dehiscence/fistula in up to 9%/14% and mortality 1–9%. Although not directly compared, local surgical excision adverse-event rate (29%) appears higher than that of endoscopy (no endoscopic rate given).
- **Ampullary biopsy:** diagnostic (adenoma + carcinoma) rates 45–80%; false-negative for carcinoma 16–60%. Minimize false-negatives with ≥6 specimens or biopsy within 10 days of sphincterotomy; orient forceps parallel to folds to reduce fibrosis.
- **Features suggesting malignancy / surgical referral:** firmness, ulceration, friability, **non-lifting of the periampullary component with submucosal injection** (failure to lift = strongest predictor of malignancy in one study). Many authors avoid endoscopic resection for lesions ≥4 cm. Lesions with high-grade dysplasia usually warrant therapy. Endoscopic removal of ampullary adenocarcinoma has been described but cannot be endorsed for routine management.
- **Imaging:** EUS superior to CT/MRI/transabdominal US for T staging; MRI superior to EUS for nodal staging; intraductal US may visualize the tumor better than EUS but can overstage. Some experts propose that lesions <1 cm **or** those without obvious signs of malignancy (ulceration, induration, bleeding) do not require US evaluation before endoscopic removal.
- **ERCP/intraductal extension:** <1 cm of common bile duct (CBD) or pancreatic duct (PD) extension does not preclude endoscopic therapy; balloon dilation/catheters can expose intraductal tissue for resection.
- **Papillectomy technique:** en bloc snare + electrocautery preferred when lesion fully ensnarable; piecemeal for >2 cm or residual tissue. No proven superiority of snare type, current type, or snare orientation. Larger-channel therapeutic duodenoscope eases instrument passage. Retrieve specimens immediately; glucagon may reduce peristalsis; CO₂ insufflation advantageous if perforation.
- **Post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP) prophylaxis:** prophylactic PD stenting reduces PEP (one small randomized controlled trial [RCT], n=19, of stenting after papillectomy positive); rectal indomethacin + temporary PD stenting lower PEP risk/severity in high-risk populations. Prophylactic biliary stenting not routinely recommended unless inadequate biliary drainage or microperforation concern.
- **Ablation:** APC (50–60 W) most common for residual/recurrent superficial tissue; PD stent usually placed before ablating near the PD orifice; ablated tissue is not available for pathology — biopsy suspicious areas before ablation.
- **Outcomes:** papillectomy clinical success 46–92%; recurrence up to 33%. Predictors of long-term success: age >48 y, lesion ≤24 mm, male sex, absence of polyposis syndrome (e.g., FAP). Intraductal growth → lower cure, more surgery.
- **Post-papillectomy:** hospitalization should be considered.
- **Surveillance:** all patients after endoscopic removal enter surveillance (Rec 5); some experts advocate endoscopy with photographic documentation and biopsy of the scar, typically 1–6 months after the index procedure, then every 3–12 months for ≥2 years; endpoints not established.
- **Nonampullary duodenal polyps:** EMR techniques described (approach like right colon given thin wall); submucosal injection cushion for flat lesions; lack of lift suggests malignancy/prior manipulation. **ESD of duodenal adenomas, especially beyond the bulb, not recommended** (perforation 23% and 35%; same Japanese group reported no perforations with EMR). Cold-snare polypectomy described for lesions >1 cm to avoid cautery injury. >33% circumferential involvement → consider surgery. Giant (>3 cm) hemicircumferential laterally spreading tumors (LSTs) have higher adverse-event (bleeding) rates (26.3% vs 3.2% for <3 cm). EUS role unclear but may affect management of polyps >2 cm. Duodenal adenoma surveillance intervals: no recommendation possible (limited data) — individualize.
- **Colorectal cancer (CRC) risk:** sporadic ampullary/duodenal adenomas are associated with increased colorectal neoplasia → offer screening colonoscopy.
