## Summary

Commentary on patient-to-patient transmission of infection during endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) infections after ERCP reached the awareness of the medical community in 2014, when the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) published findings from a suburban Chicago case cluster. Reports of similar outbreaks at several major medical centers across the United States in early 2015 prompted the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) to issue safety alerts and convene an FDA expert panel; the CDC and industry provided guidance on safe clinical care in the absence of definitive solutions. In 2016, a US Senate committee investigative report culminated in a coordinated FDA/industry recall of duodenoscopes for refurbishment and replacement of suspect parts. The authors' position is that, despite progress in awareness and understanding, "the currently proposed solutions remain cumbersome and unproven."

The mechanistic argument is a bioburden-margin argument. Under the Spaulding classification, endoscopes are "semicritical" devices that contact mucous membranes and are therefore suitable for high-level disinfection (HLD) rather than sterilization. Sterile devices are held to a Sterility Assurance Level of 10⁻⁶ — fewer than 1 organism in 10⁶ reprocessed instruments — and a similar standard holds for HLD, with the exception of some tolerant spores. Endoscopes, however, harbor 10⁹–10¹² organisms at procedure completion. Bedside precleaning reduces this by 10³ (1000-fold) and manual washing by a further 10⁶, leaving the final 10⁶–10⁹ reduction to liquid chemical germicides during HLD. The margin is therefore narrow and depends on optimal reprocessing of every instrument every day. Duodenoscopes are the worst case: complex instruments with tight crevices and mechanical joints (the elevator region and cable channels) that are repeatedly exposed to high infectious bioburden, where biofilm can aggregate and permit otherwise susceptible organisms to defy HLD.

Transmission is not new (Pseudomonas aeruginosa outbreaks after ERCP with side-viewing duodenoscopes date to the 1980s, clustering in the first patient of the day after overnight storage, and were largely eliminated by alcohol flushing and forced-air perfusion of channels after HLD), but interpatient transmission became visible again only when highly resistant species prompted source investigations with sophisticated bacterial typing. A single-center study using universal culturing of reprocessed endoscopes found that **2% of patient-ready instruments harbored persistent enteric pathogens**. Contaminated endoscopes can transmit antibiotic-sensitive as well as highly resistant causes of post-ERCP cholangitis, and can produce chronic colonization of the gastrointestinal tract with infectious complications appearing weeks to months later in unrelated organ systems.

**The document is ungraded and contains no numbered recommendations.** It is a Clinical Practice Update *Commentary*: there are no Best Practice Advice statements, no recommendation numbering, no strength ratings, and no GRADE or other evidence-quality assessment. Its operative content is two tables ("Current Understandings"; "Opportunities for Reducing Risks of Transmission of Infection Through ERCP") plus narrative relays of advice issued by other bodies — the FDA, the CDC, and an AGA press release — which the authors reproduce and critique rather than restate as their own graded recommendations.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Recommendation scheme

- **No numbered recommendations, no Best Practice Advice statements, no evidence grades or strength ratings.** The authors give no description of a formal evidence-review method. What follows is captured as the document presents it: advice attributed to the FDA, the CDC, and the AGA, and the authors' own narrative appraisal.

### Table 1 — Current Understandings (verbatim)

**Regarding CRE**
- "Increasingly prevalent in the United States."
- "Transmitted despite apparent optimal HLD."
- "Not inherently more resistant to HLD agents and process."
- "Patients with multiple comorbidities or immunosuppression at greatest risk of infection and death."
- "Many patients develop silent carriage, with risk of future infection or transmission."
- "Clinical infections often removed in time (months) and organ location from exposure."
- "Mortality of clinical infection is significant (≤50%)."

**Regarding CRE and ERCP outbreaks**
- "4 US sites published in medical literature."
- ">5 more sites publicly disclosed."
- "Overall, ≥25 outbreaks of MDRO after duodenoscope use."
- "1 cluster before 2010, most exposures 2012-2014."
- "≥250 clinical infections and 20+ deaths worldwide."
- ">1000 patients notified for screening; estimated >100 silent carriage."
- "Some transmissions confirmed by endoscope culture, others negative."
- "Occurrence with all 3 endoscope manufacturers and multiple designs."
- "Occurrence with multiple automated endoscope reprocessors, but one with greater prevalence."
- "Attributed to persistent contamination at elevator region and/or cable channels."

*(MDRO = multidrug-resistant organism. Table adapted by the authors from the US Senate report and from Petersen BT, Gastrointest Endosc 2015.)*

### Why duodenoscopes are prone to persistent contamination

- Spaulding classification: endoscopes are **semicritical** devices (contact mucous membranes) → HLD, not sterilization.
- Sterility Assurance Level for sterile devices = **10⁻⁶** (<1 organism in 10⁶ reprocessed instruments); a similar standard holds for HLD except for some tolerant spores.
- Bioburden ledger: **10⁹–10¹²** organisms at procedure completion → **10³** reduction by bedside precleaning → further **10⁶** by manual washing → final **10⁶–10⁹** reduction must come from liquid chemical germicides during HLD.
- Lapses in reprocessing → chronic colonization by adherent biofilm that cannot be eradicated by repeated, thorough cleaning and HLD; biofilm aggregation lets otherwise susceptible organisms defy HLD.
- Challenge is greatest for complex instruments with tight crevices and mechanical joints repeatedly exposed to high infectious bioburden — duodenoscopes and some ultrasound endoscopes.
- Durability matters: inert surfaces exposed to significant wear become more susceptible to contamination and less amenable to easy clearance of bioburden.

### Reprocessing steps the authors identify as most effective

- **Routine postprocedure manual cleaning is most effective** — wiping the exterior, flushing the channels, and **brushing the elevator lever immediately after use and before the surfaces have become dried**.
- Disinfecting agent used at **proper concentration, temperature, and duration**, with proper functioning of automated endoscope reprocessing equipment.
- **Biofilm can only develop in a moist environment** → routine flushing of the channels with **alcohol as a desiccant**, followed by **forced air drying** and **dry storage**, are "equally important steps."

### FDA advice relayed by the authors (lettered as in the document)

- "(a) enhanced oversight, training, and competency assurance for front line reprocessing staff"
- "(b) assiduous attention to precleaning and cleaning steps before usual automated HLD"
- "(c) adherence to manufacturer's instructions for use, including use of singular proprietary cleaning brushes used by the manufacturers in their validation studies for reprocessing"
- "(d) new emphasis on record keeping regarding all of these varied measures"

### AGA short-term recommendations relayed by the authors

Attributed to an AGA press release ("getting to zero": first meeting of regulators, endoscope manufacturers and gastroenterologists). "Specific short term recommendations from the American Gastroenterological Association include":
- "surveillance of all patients who have had a procedure using an elevator channel endoscope"
- "tracking of each elevator channel endoscope by patient and device serial number"
- "periodic collection of cultures from all elevator equipped endoscopes currently in use within a practice"

**Competency testing interval:** most technical tasks in health care involve **annual** competency testing; the AGA is cited as one of the groups encouraging more frequent confirmation of performance, "suggest[ing] requiring demonstration of competency **semi-annually**." Certification of endoscope reprocessing staff has been proposed but not adopted beyond that required in the operating room setting.

### FDA supplemental measures (May 2015 panel) — lettered as in the document

In May 2015 a majority of the FDA's independent advisory panel felt that current reprocessing standards for existing duodenoscope designs were insufficient for ensuring patient safety. The FDA subsequently encouraged facilities to consider optional supplemental measures:
- "(a) use of surveillance endoscope cultures"
- "(b) double reprocessing cycles between patients"
- "(c) ethylene oxide (ETO) sterilization"
- "(d) use of a liquid chemical sterilant processing system"

### Appraisal of each supplemental measure

| Measure | Operative detail from the document | Authors' appraisal |
|---|---|---|
| **Surveillance cultures** | CDC has proposed techniques analogous to its outbreak-investigation methods: sterile flushes of the biopsy channel, plus both swabbing and swirling immersion of the elevator region at the tip. CDC advises units **quarantine cultured endoscopes away from use until negative results return after 2–3 days** | Expensive, challenging to perform well, difficult to interpret (many nonsignificant organisms). CDC regimen is **not validated for confirmation of sterility, or even post-HLD clearance of pathogens** — the American Microbiology Association and some clinical laboratories have declined to process what they interpret as "environmental" cultures. Culturing is imperfect: contaminated endoscopes may harbor **culture-negative but biologically viable residue** |
| **Culture-and-quarantine, per-procedure** | One group used this per procedure to break a cycle of contamination on some instruments | May substantially curtail instrument availability or require a considerable increase in duodenoscope inventory |
| **Intermittent culturing** | Culture a subset of instruments **at the end of every work week**, as suggested by the CDC, to identify gross breaches in the process | Reduces the expense of additional cultures |
| **Double reprocessing** | Routine sequential duplication of the manual cleaning and HLD steps: **washing → HLD → washing → HLD**, without quarantine. Derived from the culture-quarantine experience, in which repeat reprocessing of culture-positive duodenoscopes reduced the culture-positive rate of pathogenic bacteria **by one additional log (~2% to 0.2%)** | "Perhaps the most simple and readily adopted additional measure to consider"; **not validated**; staffing, space, and equipment considerations are challenging impediments |
| **Ethylene oxide (ETO) sterilization** | Manufacturers provide guidance; some have provided validated time and temperature conditions for their instruments | **ETO has not been subjected to FDA review for this purpose** and some manufacturers do not approve it. Informal experience suggests routine ETO may stiffen or otherwise alter functional aspects of flexible endoscopes. ETO has fallen out of favor in hospitals owing to the extensive controls required to avoid **carcinogenic and teratogenic** risks; few enterprises are equipped. Instrument turnaround may be **17–48 hours**; sterilization, transport, and increased inventory costs are significant. Wide adoption unlikely |
| **Liquid chemical sterilant (e.g., peracetic acid)** | Automated reprocessing with a liquid chemical sterilant; touted as a sterilant for devices that do not require terminal point-of-use sterility | **Has yet to be validated and approved as a chemical sterilant for duodenoscopes.** Given transmissions occurring after appropriate treatment with other liquid chemical germicides, it is not intuitively clear that an alternative liquid-based process will overcome inaccessible crevices in these complex instruments |

### Table 2 — Opportunities for Reducing Risks of Transmission of Infection Through ERCP (verbatim)

**Improved QA [quality assurance] of reprocessing**
- "Optimal training, oversight, and competency evaluation of existing processes."
- "Surveillance testing of bioburden (ATP) for QA of washing processes before HLD."
- "Selective culture and quarantine after HLD after use in MDRO (+) patient."
- "Per procedure culture and quarantine after HLD."
- "Surveillance culture for QA of HLD process."

**Alternative approaches to reprocessing**
- "Double cycles of washing and HLD after each procedure."
- "Selective ETO sterilization, intermittently (for biofilm) or after use in MDRO (+) patient."
- "Per procedure ETO sterilization after HLD."
- "Peracetic acid liquid sterilization."

**Identification of high-risk endoscopes**
- "Routine anal swab in selected or all ERCP patients for detection of CRE (via PCR) or CRE and other MDROs (via culture and sensitivity) to elect alternative or enhanced reprocessing."
- "Scheduled endoscope maintenance, upgrade, parts replacements."
- "Scheduled endoscope retirement."

**New technologies**
- "Alternative duodenoscope designs with: Enhanced access for cleaning (removable tips); Tolerance to high temperature autoclaving; and Single-use disposable components."
- "Alternative low temperature sterilization technologies."
- "Adjunctive tools for enhanced precleaning, washing, and storage."

*(ATP = adenosine triphosphate; PCR = polymerase chain reaction. Table adapted by the authors from Petersen BT, Gastrointest Endosc 2015.)*

### Other limiting strategies appraised

- **Prophylactic antibiotics:** "have only been demonstrated useful and are deemed appropriate in those procedures with an expectation of persisting obstruction, such as intervention for primary sclerosing cholangitis, hilar tumors, duct leaks, or fluid collections."
- **Preprocedure patient screening for MDRO/CRE:** would enable subsequent intensified reprocessing of instruments potentially at risk of transmitting these more serious infections. Testing of anal swabs by PCR is a potentially efficient mechanism for avoiding the delayed turnaround of culture techniques, but **does not address the risk of transmission of common antibiotic-sensitive organisms**.
- **Real-time testing of reprocessing outcome "remains elusive."** A variety of tests for biologic contaminants are useful for evaluating intermediate steps, but **none correlate well with terminal culture negativity**. **ATP testing after manual washing is the best studied and most easily used** (a 5-minute assessment of test swabs or solutions); it may prove most useful for training and intermittent surveillance of washing performance.

### Industry, design, and scale of the problem

- All **3 manufacturers (Olympus, Pentax, Fujinon)** have validated current reprocessing outcomes in **test environments**; the FDA has ruled that **post-market studies of reprocessing in clinical settings are expected**, but results "will not be forthcoming for several years."
- Instrument redesign should emphasize performance enhancements for both endoscopist and reprocessing staff, and features that **reduce the impact of inevitable human error**.
- Automated endoscope reprocessor manufacturers should promote systems that **monitor and record the integrity of their functionality**; recent events highlight the opportunity for **preventive maintenance including scheduled instrument inspection for material degradation and biofilm accumulation**.
- **Scale:** "With >650,000 ERCPs performed in the United States annually, even the lowest reported defect rate of 0.7%, will expose 4500 patients to a preventable risk."
- Infectious cross-contamination also occurs from other hospital or institutional sources (outbreaks of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae in intensive care units and chronic nursing facilities), but **transmission through the environment is generally far lower than iatrogenic exposure**, and investigations strongly suggest the endoscope is the transmission source in most outbreaks of post-ERCP multidrug-resistant infections.
- **Near-term imperative:** "strict adherence to FDA/CDC recommendations, maintenance of measurable unit-based quality control, and surveillance for infectious complications associated with endoscopic procedures." Parallel physician responsibilities: "judicious indications and thorough informed consent discussions for all endoscopic procedures."

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No contradiction with existing wiki content.** This is the first ingested source addressing endoscope reprocessing and duodenoscope-transmitted infection.
- **Internal tension the authors surface:** the FDA encourages four supplemental measures, yet the document states that **none is validated** — ETO has not been FDA-reviewed for this purpose, liquid chemical sterilants are not approved for duodenoscopes, the CDC culture regimen is not validated for sterility or post-HLD pathogen clearance, and double reprocessing is extrapolated from a single culture-quarantine experience. The authors therefore endorse the measures as options to consider rather than as standards.
- **Outbreaks occurred despite apparent optimal HLD**, and CRE is "not inherently more resistant to HLD agents and process" — so the failure is mechanical/design-related rather than microbiologic resistance.
- **Unsettled:** whether any real-time test of reprocessing adequacy can replace culture; whether redesigned duodenoscopes will close the gap; and the clinical-setting post-market reprocessing data the FDA has ordered, which were not available at publication.
- **Later guidance is not in this document.** The 2017 commentary on the Multi-Society Task Force reprocessing guidelines ([[aga-2017-mstf-endoscope-reprocessing-commentary]]) supplies the reprocessing recommendation numbers, the training interval, the manual-cleaning time limit, the cost-effectiveness threshold for culture-and-quarantine, and the disposable-endoscope development that this 2016 commentary predates.
