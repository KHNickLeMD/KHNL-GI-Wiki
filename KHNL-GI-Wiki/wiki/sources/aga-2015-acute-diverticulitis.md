## Summary

Six-page AGA Institute guideline replacing the 1999 US society practice guideline on diverticular disease, built on an accompanying technical review (Strate LL, Peery AF, Neumann I. *Gastroenterology* 2015;149:1950–1976). It is organized as **11 numbered clinical questions**, each answered by a single boxed recommendation; **the recommendations themselves carry no numbers or labels** — Table 3 of the paper simply lists all 11 in question order with their strength and quality of evidence. Strength is categorized as strong, conditional, or *no recommendation* per GRADE; quality as high/moderate/low/very low.

Scope is deliberately narrow: acute diverticulitis only, defined as **clinically evident macroscopic inflammation of a diverticulum or diverticula**. The guideline explicitly **does not address** symptomatic uncomplicated diverticular disease, diverticular bleeding, segmental colitis associated with diverticulosis, prevention of incident diverticulitis, or the management of complicated disease; surgical and international guidelines are referenced where relevant. Epidemiology given in the introduction: acute diverticulitis occurs in approximately **4% of patients with diverticulosis**; roughly **15%** of those patients will have complicated disease — defined here as **abscess, perforation, fistula, or colonic obstruction** — and **15%–30%** will experience recurrence. It is the third-leading inpatient gastrointestinal diagnosis in the US, costing more than $2 billion annually.

Ten of the 11 recommendations are **conditional**; nine rest on **very low or low** quality evidence. The single **strong** recommendation is *against* mesalamine after acute uncomplicated diverticulitis (moderate quality). Two recommendations overturn long-standing teaching: antibiotics should be used **selectively rather than routinely** in acute uncomplicated diverticulitis, and elective colonic resection should **not** follow an initial uncomplicated episode. Three dietary/behavioral "avoidance" instructions are rejected as unsupported — nuts/seeds/popcorn, and aspirin — while nonaspirin NSAIDs should be avoided if possible. Mesalamine, rifaximin, and probiotics are all recommended against for reducing recurrence.

The conclusion states that the majority of the evidence is of poor quality and most recommendations are therefore conditional, and lists four research priorities: identifying who benefits from antibiotics vs safe withholding; evaluating anti-inflammatories, antibiotics, probiotics, and dietary interventions for symptoms/complications/recurrence; identifying risk factors for recurrent diverticulitis; and quantifying the yield, risks, and timing of colonoscopy after an episode.

### Supporting data and caveats given for each question

- **Antibiotics (Q1):** two recent RCTs and two systematic reviews reported no clear benefit and questioned routine use. **Generalizability caveat stated explicitly** — the studied patients were **inpatients with uncomplicated disease confirmed by CT**, so results should not be generalized to complicated patients (abscess, fistula), those with signs of severe infection or sepsis, immunosuppressed patients, or patients with other significant comorbidities. **Outpatient management without antibiotics had not been studied**, though the guideline would expect those patients to have milder disease and logically equal or better outcomes.
- **Colonoscopy (Q2):** observational cohorts of imaging-proven diverticulitis who subsequently underwent colonoscopy detected **colorectal cancer in 15 cases/1000 patients** and **advanced adenomas in 38 cases/1000 patients**. **Absence of a mass lesion on CT does not exclude an underlying colonic neoplasm.** Alternative non-neoplastic explanations for the index presentation (IBD, ischemic colitis) were infrequently identified or not reported. Increased risk of recurrent diverticulitis or perforation from colonoscopy was a concern but was **not reported as an adverse event** in the available literature. Four factors that may influence the decision to scope: **(1) timing and completeness of a prior colonoscopy, (2) comorbidities, (3) persistent symptoms of abdominal pain or diarrhea, (4) patient preferences.** Risks may be higher in **chronic diverticulitis, acute recurrent diverticulitis, or complicated diverticulitis**. Optimal timing is uncertain; **intervals of 6 to 8 weeks after resolution are commonly followed**.
- **Elective resection (Q3):** approximately **20% of patients with acute uncomplicated diverticulitis experience a recurrent episode in the following 5 years**. Risk of future diverticular complications and need for emergency surgery among patients managed medically without resection is **low (<5%)**. Approximately **10%** of patients managed with elective sigmoid resection after an episode of acute uncomplicated diverticulitis experience **short-term surgical complications** — wound infection, anastomotic leak, cardiovascular/thrombotic events — and these postoperative risks are **increased in patients older than 65 years**. **Long-term complications of abdominal distention, cramping, altered defecation, and fecal incontinence are reported in 25% of patients after elective surgery.** Rates of recurrent diverticulitis appear higher in younger patients and operative risks lower, but data do not support elective surgery in this subgroup presenting with acute uncomplicated disease. Recent retrospective data have also challenged recommendations for elective surgery after **repeated** episodes. Decisions should consider **age, patient-specific factors such as access to medical care for recurrent diverticulitis, immunosuppression, operative comorbidities, and patient preference**.
- **Fiber (Q4):** **no studies address whether dietary or supplemental fiber reduces the risk of recurrent acute diverticulitis.** Risk reduction is extrapolated from a study of patients *without* prior diverticulitis (incident disease); reductions in diverticular complications and surgery rest on a single small case-control study. Benefits of fiber for chronic abdominal pain in diverticulosis are inconsistent. The guideline states the **optimal daily dose of fiber is unknown**, differential benefit of dietary fiber vs fiber supplementation is unknown, and benefit in **recurrent or complicated** diverticulitis is **undefined**. Patient preferences and side effects (e.g. abdominal bloating) should be weighed.
- **Nuts and popcorn (Q5):** data derive from observational studies of **incident** episodes; few data on recurrence. The key study reported **modest relative risks (range 0.55–1.13)** with wide 95% confidence intervals surrounding 1.0.
- **Aspirin (Q6):** observational data indicate a slightly increased risk of any episode of diverticulitis — **RR 1.25 (95% CI 1.05–1.47)** — while the estimate for **complicated** diverticulitis is less certain — **RR 1.13 (95% CI 0.61–2.10)**. Modest protective effects on overall mortality and nonfatal myocardial infarction are in part tempered by a modest increase in GI bleeding. **The value of aspirin in secondary prevention of established coronary artery disease outweighs any increased risk of recurrent diverticular disease;** its value as primary prevention is less certain and requires more individualized decision making.
- **Nonaspirin NSAIDs (Q7):** observational data indicate a **moderately increased** risk of occurrence of any episode of diverticulitis and of complicated diverticulitis; few data on recurrence specifically.
- **Mesalamine (Q8):** **6 studies evaluating more than 1800 patients**; evidence is moderate in quality and more robust than for any other agent in the guideline, and does **not** suggest efficacy in reducing risk of recurrence, resolution of pain, or need for surgery. **Scope limit stated:** the recommendation does **not** apply to patients with multiple recurrent/relapsing disease or symptomatic uncomplicated diverticular disease — those populations were not addressed.
- **Rifaximin (Q9):** one unblinded study, terminated early, reported a numerical reduction in recurrence that was **not statistically significant**; effect remains uncertain, with cost and potential adverse events. **Same scope limit** — does not apply to chronic recurrent symptoms or symptomatic uncomplicated diverticular disease.
- **Probiotics (Q10):** one small trial reported a **non-statistically-significant** reduction in recurrence; another unblinded trial had similar findings. Very low quality plus uncertainty about the microbiome and specific agents/species.
- **Physical activity (Q11):** a large observational study of **47,288 men** indicated a **modest decreased risk of occurrence of any episode** of diverticulitis among those with vigorous activity levels; few data on recurrence.

## Relevance to Wiki

- Second source on [[diverticulitis]], joining the newer [[acg-2026-diverticulitis|ACG 2026 guideline]]. Since ACG 2026 is the newer tier-1 source, it keeps the page on every overlapping claim; AGA 2015 contributes net-new, non-conflicting content:
  - **Elective surgery after an *initial* uncomplicated episode** — a decision ACG 2026 does not cover (it addresses recurrent disease): suggestion against resection, with the recurrence (~20% at 5 years), emergency-surgery (<5%), short-term complication (~10%, higher >65 y) and long-term complication (25%) figures that make the trade-off.
  - **Colonoscopy yield numbers** (CRC 15/1000, advanced adenoma 38/1000 after imaging-proven diverticulitis), the fact that a normal CT does not exclude neoplasm, the four factors modifying the decision to scope, and the higher-risk groups for the procedure.
  - **Aspirin handled separately from nonaspirin NSAIDs**, with the cardiovascular secondary-prevention trade-off.
  - **Fiber supplementation** as an accepted alternative to a fiber-rich diet, plus the explicit statement that the optimal daily dose is unknown.
  - **Vigorous** physical activity specifically, and the size of the cohort behind it.
  - Corroborates ACG 2026 on: selective antibiotics, post-recovery colonoscopy at a 6–8-week interval, not advising nut/seed/popcorn avoidance, and recommending against mesalamine (strong, moderate — same strength and quality in both), rifaximin, and probiotics.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Colonoscopy indication — AGA 2015 is broader than ACG 2026.** AGA 2015 suggests colonoscopy after resolution of **any** acute diverticulitis in appropriate candidates if a high-quality colon examination has not recently been performed, without distinguishing complicated from uncomplicated disease. ACG 2026 splits this: **strong** for complicated disease, and **conditional — only with alarm symptoms or if not current with CRC screening** — after uncomplicated disease. **The page asserts the ACG 2026 position** (newer, same tier). The two agree on the **6–8-week** interval.
- **5-year recurrence after a first uncomplicated episode.** AGA 2015 gives **~20% within 5 years**. ACG 2026 gives a **10-year** risk of a second episode of ≥22%, and in its elective-surgery discussion cites **~60% at 5 years without surgery** — a figure it does not tie to a first uncomplicated episode. The page keeps ACG 2026's figures where they appear and carries AGA 2015's number explicitly labeled as following an *initial* episode; the two are not stated for the same population and the discrepancy is unresolved.
- **Antibiotic evidence base.** AGA 2015 states that outpatient management *without* antibiotics had **not been studied** and cautions against generalizing the inpatient trial data. ACG 2026 goes further and defines an explicit low-risk outpatient nonantibiotic pathway with eligibility criteria, at moderate certainty. **The page asserts ACG 2026's criteria.** Both reach the same direction of recommendation (selective, not routine); certainty rose from low to moderate over the decade.
- **Fiber.** AGA 2015 offers **"a fiber-rich diet *or* fiber supplementation"** as equivalent options. ACG 2026 recommends a healthy dietary pattern and states supplemental fiber is **not a substitute** for it. **The page asserts ACG 2026's framing**, noting AGA 2015 also accepts supplementation.
- **Aspirin.** AGA 2015 suggests **against routinely advising avoidance** of aspirin, on the grounds that cardiovascular secondary-prevention benefit outweighs the diverticular risk (RR 1.25). ACG 2026 suggests avoiding regular NSAID use unless clinically indicated, citing an aspirin HR of 1.25 alongside a nonaspirin HR of 1.72. Practically compatible — ACG 2026's "unless clinically indicated" preserves the aspirin exception — but the emphasis differs, and AGA 2015 is the only one of the two to make aspirin a separate recommendation.
- **Internal to this document:** the aspirin recommendation's quality of evidence is printed as *very low* in the body and **Low** in Table 3; Table 3 also adds "seeds" to the nuts-and-popcorn recommendation that the boxed statement omits.
- **Out of scope in this guideline** — so nothing here contributes to these topics: symptomatic uncomplicated diverticular disease, diverticular bleeding, segmental colitis associated with diverticulosis, prevention of incident diverticulitis, and management of complicated disease. It also gives **no severity classification** (no Hinchey staging), **no antibiotic agents, doses, or durations**, and **no diagnostic imaging criteria**.
