## Summary

The American Cancer Society (ACS) guideline update that **moved the recommended start age for average-risk colorectal cancer (CRC) screening from 50 to 45** — the first US organization to do so, three years ahead of the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) 2021 statement. The ACS Guideline Development Group (GDG) applied the Grades of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework and worked from two evidence sources commissioned for the USPSTF 2016 update — a systematic evidence review and Cancer Intervention and Surveillance Modeling Network (CISNET) microsimulation modelling — plus new modelling the ACS commissioned from the MISCAN and SimCRC groups that incorporated observed increases in CRC incidence in adults under 55.

The ACS uses a **two-level strength vocabulary, not the usual GRADE strong/weak pair: *strong* and *qualified***. A strong recommendation means the GDG judged the benefits of adherence to outweigh the undesirable effects and that most patients would choose the intervention. A **qualified** recommendation means there is clear evidence of benefit (or harm) but less certainty either about the balance of benefits and harms or about patients' values and preferences, which could lead different individuals to different decisions. The document is explicit that the age-45 start is **qualified** while regular screening from 50 is **strong** — the distinction is the thin direct evidence under age 50, not doubt about the disease burden.

The rationale for age 45 is disease burden plus modelling rather than trial evidence. CRC incidence rose 51% in adults younger than 50 between 1994 and 2014, and mortality rose 11% from 2005 to 2015; Siegel et al attributed the rise to a birth-cohort effect that will carry forward with age. Adults born around 1990 have twice the risk of colon cancer and four times the risk of rectal cancer of adults born around 1950. The commissioned MISCAN/SimCRC analysis found colonoscopy every 10 years from 45 to 75 gave **6.2% more life-years gained for 17% more colonoscopies per 1000 adults** than the same strategy from 50 to 75, and that under the increased-risk scenario the models concurred on a 10-year interval.

The guideline deliberately declines to rank its six test options, instead presenting them as a menu to be chosen from by patient preference and test availability, on the evidence that offering a choice improves adherence more than offering colonoscopy alone. It is emphatic on one point: a **positive noncolonoscopy test is not a screen until a timely colonoscopy is done**, that colonoscopy is part of the screening process rather than a "diagnostic" exam, and that **repeating a positive stool-based test to decide whether to proceed to colonoscopy is not an appropriate screening strategy**.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Recommendations (Table 1) — ACS's own strength labels, verbatim force

The document presents its recommendations as an unnumbered list; **the numbering below is the wiki's**.

1. **Adults aged 45 y and older with an average risk of CRC should undergo regular screening** with either a high-sensitivity stool-based test or a structural (visual) examination, depending on patient preference and test availability. As part of the screening process, **all positive results on noncolonoscopy screening tests should be followed up with timely colonoscopy**. *(Overall recommendation; the two age components are graded separately below.)*
2. **The recommendation to begin screening at age 45 y is a *qualified recommendation*.**
3. **The recommendation for regular screening in adults aged 50 y and older is a *strong recommendation*.**
4. **Average-risk adults in good health with a life expectancy of greater than 10 y should continue CRC screening through the age of 75 y** — *qualified recommendation*.
5. **Clinicians should individualize CRC screening decisions for individuals aged 76 through 85 y**, based on patient preferences, life expectancy, health status, and prior screening history — *qualified recommendation*.
6. **Clinicians should discourage individuals over age 85 y from continuing CRC screening** — *qualified recommendation*.

**Average risk** is defined in the Table 1 footnote as persons **without** a personal history of CRC or confirmed/suspected hereditary CRC syndrome (such as familial adenomatous polyposis or Lynch syndrome), **without** a personal history of adenomatous polyps, **without** a history of abdominal or pelvic radiation for a previous cancer, **without** a personal history of inflammatory bowel disease, and **without** an increased risk due to a family history of CRC.

### Options for CRC screening (Table 1) — six tests, no ranking

| Class | Test | Interval |
|---|---|---|
| Stool-based | Fecal immunochemical test (FIT) | Every 1 y |
| Stool-based | High-sensitivity, guaiac-based fecal occult blood test (HSgFOBT) | Every 1 y |
| Stool-based | Multitarget stool DNA (mt-sDNA) | Every 3 y |
| Structural (visual) | Colonoscopy | Every 10 y |
| Structural (visual) | Computed tomography (CT) colonography | Every 5 y |
| Structural (visual) | Flexible sigmoidoscopy (FS) | Every 5 y |

- The Table 3 footnote states the ACS "considers these options as acceptable choices for CRC screening in average-risk adults" and that individuals **should be given information on the characteristics, accuracy, costs, and potential harms** of available and accessible tests to make an informed decision on which to choose.
- **mt-sDNA interval is "every 3 y (as per manufacturer)"** — the guideline attributes the interval to the manufacturer, not to its own evidence review.

### Test-selection considerations (Table 3) — condensed

- **FIT:** indirect mortality evidence from guaiac RCTs; equivalent or superior performance vs HSgFOBT; done at home, **many brands require only a single sample**, **no diet or medication restrictions**. Limitations: high nonadherence to annual testing without reminder systems; less effective for advanced adenoma detection; few available tests have published peer-reviewed performance data; test performance varies by version and brand.
- **HSgFOBT:** good RCT evidence for incidence and mortality reduction. **Requires multiple samples and dietary and medication restriction**; higher false-positive rate than FIT, leading to more colonoscopies; **low-sensitivity gFOBT versions are not recommended** for CRC screening.
- **mt-sDNA:** can be done at home; a **new test with limited data on screening outcomes** whose performance needs monitoring over time. One large manufacturer-funded trial showed improved sensitivity for cancer and advanced adenomas but **poorer specificity compared with FIT**; **higher false-positive rate than FIT**; uncertainty in managing a positive result followed by a negative colonoscopy. Most expensive stool-based test.
- **Colonoscopy:** non-RCT evidence of incidence and mortality reduction; extrapolated from FS RCTs; **offers both early detection and prevention of CRC through polypectomy**. Limitations: risk of bowel perforation/bleeding and cardiopulmonary complications of anesthesia; performance depends on adequacy of bowel preparation, cecal intubation rate, withdrawal time, and adenoma detection rate; limited collection of quality data in many settings; **level of adherence to the 10-y interval is unknown**; lower sensitivity for neoplasia in the proximal colon. Requires full bowel cleansing, time off work, and a chaperone if sedation is used. Most expensive test, but currently reimbursable for those with insurance coverage; **polypectomy and anesthesia may be subject to out-of-pocket costs**.
- **CT colonography:** extrapolated from FS RCTs; sensitivity and specificity for cancer and advanced adenomas comparable to colonoscopy. Limitations: **incidental extracolonic findings with unclear benefit-burden balance**; low-dose radiation exposure. Requires full bowel cleansing; **colonoscopy is required if the test is positive, and if same-day colonoscopy is not possible a second bowel cleansing is needed**. Relatively expensive and **not covered by Medicare at this time**.
- **Flexible sigmoidoscopy:** **best evidence among structural examinations** for reducing mortality and incidence. Limitations: **does not examine the proximal colon**; concerns about lack of quality standards, limited availability, failure to achieve a complete examination. Pain and discomfort; requires an enema beforehand; abnormal findings require a second endoscopic procedure (colonoscopy).

### Age to begin — the evidence behind the qualified age-45 recommendation

- **Burden:** CRC incidence rose **51% in adults younger than 50 from 1994 to 2014**; mortality rose **11% from 2005 to 2015**. Rectal cancer incidence doubled, from 2.6 to 5.2 per 100,000 in adults aged 20–49 between 1991 and 2014.
- Adults born **around 1990 have twice the risk of colon cancer and four times the risk of rectal cancer** of adults born around 1950.
- **Incidence by band:** 45–49 y = **31.4 per 100,000**; 50–54 y = **58.4 per 100,000**. The guideline notes the 50–54 figure is inflated by lead time from screening uptake (45.3% of adults 50–54 reported recent colonoscopy or FS in 2015 vs ~17.8% of adults 40–49), so the true underlying risk at 45–49 is **likely closer to that at 50–54 than the crude rates suggest**. The **annual percentage increase** in incidence at 40–49 y (**1.3%**) is more than twice that at 50–54 y (**0.5%**).
- **Prevalence of polyps ≥9 mm in adults younger than 50 was 4.2% in Whites and 6.2% in Blacks**, similar to 5.3% and 6.1% in adults aged 50–59 (Lieberman et al) — i.e. comparable prevention potential.
- **Of all CRC deaths 2010–2014, a similar proportion were diagnosed at 45–49 y (5.1%) as at 50–54 y (7.6%)**; 10% of premature mortality measured by years of potential life lost is attributable to diagnoses at 45–49 y vs 13% at 50–54 y.
- **Modelling (Table 2, per 1000 screened over a lifetime, increased-risk scenario):** colonoscopy every 10 y at 45–75 gives **429 life-years gained (LYG) for 5646 colonoscopies**, vs **404 LYG for 4836 colonoscopies** at 50–75 — **6.2% more LYG for 17% more colonoscopies**. Model-recommendable strategies starting at 45: colonoscopy every 10 y, annual FIT, CT colonography every 5 y, FS every 5 y. **No strategy starting at 50 was model-recommendable** under the increased-risk scenario.
- **Starting at 40 was evaluated and rejected:** a small LYG gain (438 vs 429 per 1000) with the **same number of deaths averted (37)** and an efficiency ratio above the model-recommended threshold; incidence at 40–44 y is **17.6 per 100,000** vs 31.4 at 45–49.
- **Why *qualified*:** there are **no RCT data on screening below age 50** — most CRC screening RCTs enrolled from age 50, and the three European gFOBT trials that enrolled from 45 were underpowered for age-subgroup analysis and did not report age-specific outcomes. The GDG acknowledged the **absolute benefit expected at 45–49 is lower than in older screened age groups** but judged the tradeoff favorable.
- The GDG also noted colonoscopy **harms are lower in younger than older adults**, that **US colonoscopy capacity should accommodate the increase**, and that an earlier start may **reduce disparities** in groups with higher disease burden (Black, Alaska Native, some American Indian populations). Incidence in Asian and Hispanic adults is similar to Whites, so **the age-45 start is intended to apply to all groups**.

### Age to stop — what drives the three age bands

- **Through 75 y:** RCT evidence supports screening to 75 — the Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian (PLCO) FS trial enrolled to age 74, all but one gFOBT trial enrolled to at least 75, and the US gFOBT trial enrolled through 80.
- **Screening benefit generally requires a minimum 10-year life expectancy.** From the gFOBT RCT data, the **lag time to CRC screening benefit is estimated at 10 years** (a combination of early detection, realized sooner, and prevention, realized after 10 years).
- **76–85 y, individualize:** focus on **healthy individuals with no or few comorbidities expected to live at least 10 years**; yield is higher in those **not up to date** with screening. If colonoscopy risk is a concern, **noncolonoscopy options may be preferable**. Patient preference should weigh heavily given the paucity of evidence.
- **Harms rise with age:** in a Medicare cohort of beneficiaries aged 70–79 with no diagnostic or surveillance colonoscopy in the prior 5 y, the **absolute 8-year CRC risk fell 16% with colonoscopy at ages 70–74 (2.19% vs 2.62%)** but risk reduction was **notably less at 75–79 (2.84% vs 2.97%)**. Adverse events from colonoscopy were **nearly twice as high at 75–79 (10.3 per 1000) as at 70–74 (5.6 per 1000)**.
- **Over 85 y:** competing mortality risks and complication risks are sufficiently high that **potential harms outweigh potential benefits**; health care professionals **should not offer screening**. The guideline allows that exceptional circumstances exist (excellent health, never screened, strongly desires testing) but says screening should **in general be discouraged**.

### Follow-up of a positive noncolonoscopy test

- The whole menu is **premised on timely colonoscopy after any positive noncolonoscopy test**; that colonoscopy is **an integral part of the screening process, not a "diagnostic" colonoscopy**, and the screen is **not complete until it is performed**.
- **Repeating a positive stool-based test to decide whether to proceed to colonoscopy is explicitly not an appropriate screening strategy.**
- **Delay data (retrospective cohort, 70,124 patients with a positive FIT):** no significant difference in CRC risk with colonoscopy performed **as late as 7 to 9 months** after a positive FIT. After a delay of **10 months or more**, there was a **48% greater risk of CRC**, and the risk of **stage III or IV disease was double** that of colonoscopy within the first 9 months. At **12 months or more**, odds ratio **2.25 for any cancer** and **3.22 for advanced-stage disease**.
- Timely follow-up is poor in practice: more than half of patients in some settings fail to complete colonoscopy within 12 months of a positive stool test; completion at 12 months varied **58% to 83%** across four US health systems.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Start age vs [[acg-2021-crc-screening|ACG 2021]] and [[uspstf-2021-crc-screening|USPSTF 2021]]:** no conflict in direction — all three screen from 45 — but the **strength differs**. ACS calls age 45 *qualified*; USPSTF 2021 grades it **B**; ACG 2021 calls it **Conditional / Very low**. ACS is the oldest of the three; where the page states a strength for the 45–49 band, the newer statements govern and ACS is shown alongside for comparison.
- **mt-sDNA interval:** ACS gives **every 3 y, attributed to the manufacturer**. USPSTF 2021 gives **1–3 y**. The page already resolves this to 3 y per ACG/USMSTF; ACS corroborates.
- **Flexible sigmoidoscopy interval:** ACS gives **every 5 y** only; USMSTF/USPSTF also allow **10 y combined with annual FIT**. ACS does not list the combination strategy. Newer sources govern; the ACS omission is not a recommendation against.
- **Delay after a positive stool test vs [[aga-2022-noninvasive-crc-screening|AGA 2022]]:** ACS's cohort found **no significant excess CRC risk out to 7–9 months**, with the inflection at **≥10 months**. AGA 2022 sets the operating threshold at **>6 months** and makes it a program quality target (colonoscopy performed in ≥95% within 6 months). AGA 2022 is newer and **governs the page**; the ACS figures are shown as where measurable harm begins, not as permission to wait. Not a true contradiction — a conservative target set below an observed harm inflection — but the two numbers read as conflicting if printed side by side without this note.
- **Colon capsule and blood-based tests** are absent from the ACS menu entirely — the 2018 document predates them as screening options and says nothing either way, so it neither supports nor contradicts the USPSTF exclusion already on the page.
- **Stopping age vs [[aga-2026-surveillance-older-adults|AGA 2026]]:** both require a ~10-year life expectancy and both say age alone is insufficient, but AGA 2026 is far more specific (named prognostic indices, post-normal-screen risk figures, FIT as the preferred noninvasive test in older adults). AGA 2026 is newer and governs the detail; ACS supplies the harms-by-age-band Medicare cohort figures AGA does not print.
