## Summary

The first tier-1 source in the wiki to address **drug chemoprevention of colorectal neoplasia** as its primary subject. It evaluates the seven agents that have been tested in randomized trials — aspirin, non-aspirin NSAIDs, metformin, calcium, vitamin D, folic acid, and statins — and issues **9 Best Practice Advice statements**, of which only **four are positive**: low-dose aspirin in a narrowly defined average-risk group (BPA 1), aspirin after CRC (BPA 2), and metformin in type 2 diabetes for prevention (BPA 4) and for mortality after CRC (BPA 5). The remaining five are explicit **"should not use"** statements — non-aspirin NSAIDs, calcium, vitamin D, folic acid, and statins.

The decisive clinical fact is **latency**. Aspirin's benefit on CRC incidence and mortality is **not present in the first decade**: pooled analysis of 4 trials (n = 69,535) found no difference in CRC incidence 0–12 years after starting aspirin (RR 0.99), while 3 of those trials (n = 47,464) showed a **40% reduction 10–19 years after initiation** (RR 0.60). That is why BPA 1 restricts aspirin chemoprevention to patients **under 70 with a life expectancy of at least 10 years** — a patient who will not live through the latency window gets the bleeding risk without the benefit.

The counterweight is bleeding, and it is quantified: aspirin at any dose raises major GI bleeding (OR 1.59) and intracranial bleeding (OR 1.34), **the risk does not fall meaningfully at doses ≤100 mg daily**, and **GI bleeding risk is greater still above age 70**. The ASPREE trial (n = 19,114, 96% aged ≥70) found *higher* CRC mortality in the aspirin arm (HR 1.77) at a median 4.7 years. Hence the CPU aligns with USPSTF and adds a cardiovascular gate — a **10-year CVD risk of at least 10%** — so that aspirin is only started in people who would plausibly benefit on cardiovascular grounds anyway, with CRC reduction as the long-latency bonus.

Several negative statements carry a **harm signal, not just an absence of benefit**, and these are the ones most likely to surprise: calcium supplementation was associated with an **increased delayed risk of sessile serrated lesions (RR 2.66)**, the calcium + vitamin D combination with **RR 3.82 for SSLs 6–10 years after supplementation began**, and folic acid with **increased advanced and multiple adenomas**, increased non-colorectal (particularly prostate) cancers, and a possible excess of **right-sided serrated lesions**.

## Key Findings / Claims

### The 9 Best Practice Advice statements (verbatim, from Table 1)

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 1:** In individuals at average risk for CRC who are (1) younger than 70 years with a life expectancy of at least 10 years, (2) have a 10-year cardiovascular disease risk of at least 10%,ᵃ and (3) not at high risk for bleeding, clinicians **should** use low-dose aspirin to reduce CRC incidence and mortality.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 2:** In individuals with a history of CRC, clinicians **should consider** using aspirin to prevent recurrent colorectal neoplasia.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 3:** In individuals at average risk for CRC, clinicians **should not** use non-aspirin NSAIDs to prevent colorectal neoplasia because of substantial risk of cardiovascular and gastrointestinal adverse events.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 4:** In individuals with type 2 diabetes, clinicians **may consider** using metformin to prevent colorectal neoplasia.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 5:** In individuals with CRC and type 2 diabetes, clinicians **may consider** using metformin to reduce mortality.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 6:** Clinicians **should not** use calcium or vitamin D (alone or together) to prevent colorectal neoplasia.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 7:** Clinicians **should not** use folic acid to prevent colorectal neoplasia.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 8:** In individuals at average risk for CRC, clinicians **should not** use statins to prevent colorectal neoplasia.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 9:** In individuals with history of CRC, clinicians **should not** use statins to reduce mortality.

ᵃ **Table 1 footnote (verbatim):** *"Cardiovascular disease risk calculator available at http://tools.acc.org/ASCVD-Risk-Estimator/."*

### BPA 1 — the three criteria are a conjunction, and each is operative

All three must hold before starting aspirin for chemoprevention:

| # | Criterion | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Age <70 years AND life expectancy ≥10 years** | Benefit does not appear until **10–19 years** after initiation. GI bleeding risk is **greater above age 70**. ASPREE (96% aged ≥70) found *higher* CRC mortality with aspirin (HR 1.77; 1.02–3.06) at median 4.7 y follow-up — without an increase in CRC incidence |
| 2 | **10-year cardiovascular disease risk ≥10%** | Benefit "has only been consistently demonstrated in adults younger than 70 years who are **at risk of cardiovascular disease**"; consistent with USPSTF. Use the ACC ASCVD Risk Estimator |
| 3 | **Not at high risk for bleeding** | Aspirin at **any** dose: major GI bleeding **OR 1.59 (1.32–1.91)**, intracranial bleeding **OR 1.34 (1.07–1.70)** |

⚠ **Dose does not rescue the bleeding risk.** *"The risk did not change substantially when aspirin dose was 100 mg daily or less."* ASCEND (n = 15,480) found 100 mg daily increased any major bleeding (RR 1.29; 1.09–1.52) over 7 years.

### Aspirin — efficacy data

- **CRC mortality:** pooled data from 4 CVD-prevention trials (n = 14,033), aspirin **75–1200 mg daily**, reduced **20-year cumulative CRC mortality by 33%** (RR 0.67; 0.52–0.86). Protection appeared **only at 10–20 years** of follow-up (HR 0.51; 0.35–0.74) and **not before** (HR 0.79; 0.49–1.26). Duration **>5 years** may give greater protection; dose effect unclear.
- **CRC incidence:** no difference at **0–12 years** (RR 0.99; 0.85–1.15, n = 69,535); **40% reduction at 10–19 years** (RR 0.60; 0.47–0.76, n = 47,464); 24% reduction in 20-year cumulative incidence (HR 0.76; 0.63–0.94). **Effect does not appear to differ by dose**; longer duration may reduce risk.
- **Adenoma incidence (observational, Nurses' Health Study, n = 27,077):** 6–14 tablets of 325 mg aspirin weekly → **28% fewer distal adenomas** (RR 0.72; 0.61–0.85); >14 tablets weekly → **51% fewer** (RR 0.49; 0.36–0.65).
- **Adenoma recurrence with prior adenoma:** meta-analysis of 2 trials, **aspirin 81–160 mg/d → RR 0.83 (0.71–0.96)**; **no reduction in advanced neoplasia** (RR 0.83; 0.44–1.58). **No reduction at doses ≥300 mg daily** in meta-analysis of all 3 trials. seAFOod (n = 709, 300 mg aspirin ± EPA, prior high-risk adenoma): no difference in adenoma recurrence (RR 0.99) at 1 year, though mean adenomas per participant was lower (IRR 0.78; 0.68–0.90).
- **Adenoma recurrence with prior CRC:** US trial (n = 517), **325 mg daily → RR 0.65 (0.46–0.91)** at 1 year, **no difference in advanced adenomas**. Japanese trial (n = 311, 25% with CRC), **100 mg daily over 2 years → OR 0.60 (0.36–0.98)**.
- **Survival after CRC diagnosis:** meta-analysis found **no** association with CRC-specific survival (HR 0.77; 0.52–1.14) but **improved overall survival** (HR 0.84; 0.75–0.94) — benefit *"appeared restricted to individuals with PIK3CA-mutant and COX-2 expressing tumors."*
- **Sessile serrated lesions:** evidence sparse. Meta-analysis of 6 observational studies — protective against serrated polyps generally (RR 0.81; 0.67–0.99) but **not specifically SSLs**. seAFOod secondary analysis: decreased SSL incidence (IRR 0.46; 0.25–0.87).

### Non-aspirin NSAIDs (BPA 3 — do not use)

- **The efficacy is real; the harm is why the answer is no.** Meta-analysis of 23 observational studies (n = 1,286,773): **26% lower CRC risk** (OR 0.74; 0.68–0.81) — but on subgroup analysis protective **only in women** (OR 0.67; 0.53–0.85), **not men** (OR 0.86; 0.70–1.06).
- 3 trials (n = 6166) of celecoxib or rofecoxib for 3 years: **34–45% lower adenoma recurrence**; **44–57% lower advanced adenoma recurrence**. Higher celecoxib dose gave greater protection in the trial using two dosing strengths.
- **Protection is lost on stopping**, and patients *"may even be at increased risk"* of adenoma recurrence afterward.
- **Harms:** two COX-2-inhibitor adenoma trials were **terminated early for increased cardiovascular events**. GI toxicity (peptic ulcers, bleeding) is the main risk of nonselective NSAIDs and is **dose-dependent**.
- Rofecoxib in stage II/III CRC (VICTOR, n = 2434): no effect on CRC-specific mortality (HR 0.98), overall survival (HR 0.97), or recurrence (HR 0.89) — trial terminated early on the worldwide rofecoxib withdrawal.
- Insufficient evidence for non-aspirin NSAIDs and SSL incidence.

### Metformin (BPA 4, 5 — *may* consider, diabetes only)

- **CRC incidence:** meta-analysis of 14 observational studies (n = 459,607) — **27% lower CRC incidence** in type 2 diabetes vs non-users including users of other treatments (OR 0.73; 0.62–0.86). Contrast: a diabetes trial vs rosiglitazone/glyburide found no difference, but was **underpowered**.
- **Mortality with CRC + type 2 diabetes:** meta-analysis of 17 observational studies (n = 269,417) — cancer-specific survival HR 0.75 (0.59–0.94), overall survival HR 0.69 (0.61–0.77). ⚠ The CPU itself cautions these are *"susceptible to confounding by diabetes severity and time-related biases."*
- **Adenoma:** meta-analysis of 5 observational studies (n = 19,730) — **20% lower adenoma risk** in type 2 diabetes (OR 0.80; 0.71–0.90). 2016 Japanese RCT (n = 151) in **nondiabetic** patients with prior adenoma/polyp, **metformin 250 mg vs placebo, 1 year → 40% lower adenoma recurrence** (RR 0.60; 0.39–0.92). Larger, longer studies needed before generalizing to average-risk individuals. Insufficient evidence for SSLs.
- **Harms:** generally well tolerated; diarrhea, nausea, vomiting. In the Japanese trial **11% had a mild adverse event, no serious events**.

### Calcium and vitamin D (BPA 6 — do not use) — note the harm signal

- **Calcium, CRC incidence (observational):** Nurses' Health Study + Health Professionals Follow-up Study 2016 analysis — higher calcium intake, **RR 0.78 (0.65–0.95)**; dose-dependent; **no difference between dietary and supplemental** calcium. On this basis the American Institute for Cancer Research concluded calcium supplements decrease CRC risk.
- **Calcium, adenoma recurrence (trials):** 1999 polyp prevention trial (n = 913), **1200 mg elemental calcium daily → RR 0.85 (0.74–0.98)**; meta-analysis with 2 smaller trials → **~20% reduction** (pooled RR 0.80; 0.68–0.93). But a **more recent, larger randomized study found no benefit** — possibly a reduced effect in obese individuals.
- ⚠ **Calcium supplementation was associated with an increased *delayed* risk of SSLs: RR 2.66 (1.44–4.89).**
- **Vitamin D:** 2011 meta-analysis, highest vs lowest intake, RR 0.88 (0.80–0.96). **Women's Health Initiative** (>36,000 postmenopausal women, **1000 mg elemental calcium + 400 IU cholecalciferol**, mean 7 years): **no effect on CRC incidence** (HR 1.08; 0.86–1.34). US multicenter trial (n = 2259) of **1200 mg elemental calcium daily, 1000 IU cholecalciferol daily, and the combination**: neither vitamin D alone (RR 0.99; 0.89–1.09) nor the combination affected adenoma recurrence.
- ⚠ **Calcium + vitamin D combination was associated with increased SSL risk 6–10 years after supplementation began: RR 3.82 (1.26–11.57).** Vitamin D alone did **not** definitively increase SSL risk (RR 1.31; 0.82–2.10).
- Aspirin + calcium + calcitriol trial (2016) **stopped early** at interim analysis for no benefit on adenoma recurrence (OR 0.95; 0.61–1.48).
- Other calcium harms noted: high intake linked to **increased prostate cancer risk**; excess supplements cause **kidney toxicity, hypercalcemia**, and other metabolic abnormalities.
- **Pending:** the Australian **D-Health Trial** (>21,000 randomized to 5 years of cholecalciferol vs placebo) will report CRC incidence — *"results of this important study are not expected until at least 2025."*

### Folic acid (BPA 7 — do not use) — observational benefit, trial harm

- Observational: higher long-term folate intake associated with lower CRC risk (RR 0.69; 0.51–0.94) and lower adenoma risk; meta-analyses suggest a modest **10–15%** reduction.
- **Supplementation trials are largely negative:** large US multicenter trial (n = 1021) — folic acid did **not** reduce adenoma recurrence (RR 1.04; 0.90–1.20) and was **associated with increased advanced adenomas and multiple adenomas**, plus **increased non-colorectal cancers, particularly prostate**. Long-term follow-up showed **a possible increased risk of serrated polyps, including right-sided serrated lesions**. A UK trial found no effect on adenoma recurrence (RR 1.07; 0.85–1.34).
- Meta-analyses of multiple trials found no clear effect on CRC or adenoma incidence.

### Statins (BPA 8, 9 — do not use for this indication)

- **Mixed and inconsistent throughout.** Korean national database (n = 31,149, 13 y): CRC incidence reduced **44% in men** (HR 0.56; 0.42–0.75) and **36% in women** (HR 0.64; 0.46–0.90). Systematic review of 42 studies: modest reduction (RR 0.90; 0.86–0.95). A systematic review of 40 studies (~8 million individuals) found reduction in cohort (RR 0.91; 0.83–1.00) and case-control (RR 0.92; 0.87–0.98) designs. Other observational studies report **no** protective effect regardless of duration.
- **Mortality after CRC is inconclusive** and observational studies suffer selection bias; in a large SEER-Medicare case-control study, statins started within 6 months of cancer diagnosis were **not** associated with reduced 3-year CRC-related or all-cause mortality.
- Safety: relatively safe — the recommendation against is about **lack of demonstrated benefit for this indication**, not toxicity.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Observational benefit vs randomized harm — the recurring pattern.** Calcium, vitamin D and folic acid all look protective in cohort data and fail or cause harm in trials. The CPU resolves this in favor of the trials. Any wiki text citing the observational figures must carry the trial result alongside.
- **Aspirin dose is genuinely unsettled in this source.** CRC incidence effect *"does not seem to differ by dose"*; adenoma recurrence with prior adenoma benefits at **81–160 mg/d** but **not at ≥300 mg/d**; the trial in patients with prior CRC used **325 mg**. BPA 1 says "low-dose" without printing a milligram figure. **The CPU never states a specific chemopreventive dose — do not supply one.**
- **ASPREE points the other way in the elderly.** Higher CRC mortality with aspirin (HR 1.77) at median 4.7 years without increased incidence. The CPU absorbs this into the age-<70 criterion rather than treating it as a contradiction; recorded here because it is the strongest counter-evidence in the document.
- **Metformin rests almost entirely on observational data** plus one small nondiabetic RCT (n = 151) — hence "may consider," and hence the restriction to patients with diabetes who have an independent indication.
- **Scope exclusions leave real gaps.** This source does not cover chemoprevention in [[lynch-syndrome]], [[familial-adenomatous-polyposis]], other hereditary syndromes, or [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|IBD]] — the populations in which chemoprevention is most often actually discussed. Aspirin in Lynch syndrome (CAPP2) is **not in this source**; do not attribute it here.
- **Age is 2021-vintage.** The D-Health Trial was pending at publication ("not expected until at least 2025"). If a newer CRC-screening or chemoprevention guideline is ingested later, re-check BPA 6 against it.
