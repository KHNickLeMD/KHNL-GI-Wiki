## Summary

A region-by-region account of what mu (μ) opioid receptor agonism does to the gastrointestinal tract, and of the drugs that exploit or reverse it. The organising insight is **differential tolerance**: tolerance to μ opioid effects develops in every gastrointestinal organ **except the colon**, which is why sedation and nausea fade with continued use but **constipation does not**. The proposed mechanism is that β-arrestin-2 downregulation does not occur in the colon, so the receptor recycles to the plasma membrane rather than being internalised.

Clinically the review covers five opioid-attributable presentations — esophageal dysmotility that can be **indistinguishable from primary achalasia** (and specifically mimics type III, less so type II), delayed gastric emptying/gastroparesis, sphincter of Oddi spasm, postoperative ileus, and opioid-induced constipation (OIC) — plus **narcotic bowel syndrome (NBS)**, which the authors are careful to separate from the others as a disorder of *nociception*, independent of the opioid's effects on motility and secretion.

The therapeutic half runs the other direction: opioid pharmacology recruited deliberately. Loperamide and eluxadoline for diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), alvimopan for postoperative ileus, and for OIC a stepped approach — laxatives first, then, when the bowel function index (BFI) shows they have failed, one of the approved agents.

The review is unusually blunt on two safety points: **there is no evidence for μ opioid agonists for the pain of IBS**, and eluxadoline must not be used in patients without a gallbladder after FDA review found increased pancreatitis risk with two reported deaths.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Receptor pharmacology
- Three main receptors — μ, δ (delta), κ (kappa) — G(i/o) protein-coupled; reduce intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate, hyperpolarise neurons, inhibit neurotransmitter release. Net cellular effect inhibitory.
- **μ** — principal mediator of analgesia *and* of sedation, bowel dysfunction, respiratory depression, dependence. Myenteric and submucosal plexuses, central nervous system, spinal cord. Agonists: morphine, trimebutine, loperamide, eluxadoline. Antagonists: naloxone, naltrexone; peripherally acting mu opioid receptor antagonists (PAMORAs) alvimopan, methylnaltrexone, naloxegol.
- **κ** — analgesia, bowel dysfunction, diuresis, sedation; visceral antinociception. Central κ activation causes dose-dependent dysphoria.
- **δ** — mainly central nervous system analgesia; also myenteric neurons. Antagonists: eluxadoline, alvimopan.
- μ and δ are the principal gastrointestinal receptors. **The clinically relevant opioids are μ opioids.**

### Effects by region (Table 1)
| Site | Pharmacologic effect | Potential clinical effect |
|---|---|---|
| Lower esophageal sphincter | Inhibition of relaxation | — |
| Esophagus | Simultaneous contractions | "Achalasia" |
| Gallbladder and biliary tract | Contraction; sphincter of Oddi spasm; decreased secretion | Biliary pain; delayed digestion |
| Gastroduodenum | Inhibition of gastric emptying; increased duodenal motility then quiescence; increased pyloric tone | Anorexia, nausea and vomiting, gastroparesis, postoperative ileus |
| Small bowel | Increased tone/segmentation, transit time, absorption; decreased secretion | Indigestion, bloating, distention, constipation, postoperative ileus |
| Colon | Increased tone/segmentation, transit time, absorption; decreased secretion | Bloating and distention; spasm, cramps, pain; constipation; hard, dry stools |
| Anorectum | Decreased rectal sensitivity; increased internal sphincter tone | Incomplete evacuation; straining constipation |

### Differential tolerance — why constipation persists
- Tolerance to μ opioid effects occurs in all gastrointestinal organs **except the colon**; constipation therefore **does not abate over time**.
- β-arrestin-2 downregulation does not occur in the ileum (→ tolerance to morphine) but does not occur in the colon either, leading to receptor recycling to the plasma membrane and **lack of colonic tolerance**.

### Esophagus
- Acute morphine in gastroesophageal reflux disease: **decreases** transient lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxations, fewer reflux episodes, less time at pH <4. Resting LES pressure decreased in health and in achalasia; swallow-induced LES relaxation significantly decreased.
- **Chronic** opioid use with dysphagia: impaired LES relaxation, high amplitude/velocity, simultaneous esophageal waves; esophagogastric junction outflow obstruction, higher integrated relaxation pressure, lower distal latency. **Some of these are the opposite of the acute effects** — acute and chronic esophageal physiology differ.
- May mimic **type III achalasia**, less so type II. Because patients often cannot stop opioids, distinguishing primary from opioid-induced achalasia is problematic; **treatment outcome is worse** in clear opioid-induced achalasia.

### Gastroparesis
- Among patients evaluated by the National Institutes of Health Gastroparesis Consortium, **42% overall** were on opiates/narcotics; **48%** of those with abdominal discomfort score ≥3 vs **33%** with score <3.
- **Tapentadol** (μ agonist + norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor) delayed gastric emptying comparably to oxycodone, though it does not retard colonic transit.
- **Tramadol** did not retard gastric emptying of solids or liquids (crossover, 12 healthy participants) but showed **40% slower orocecal transit** and significant colonic transit delay.

### Sphincter of Oddi
- Opioid effects on the sphincter are predominantly **myogenic** (preserved in the presence of tetrodotoxin).
- Morphine increases **tonic** pressure; naloxone increases **phasic** pressure; the predominant effect of clinically used opioids is increased **phasic** sphincter of Oddi pressure.
- In post-cholecystectomy patients with suspected sphincter of Oddi dysfunction (SOD), **30% had taken opiate-containing drugs 15–120 minutes before pain onset**.
- **Eluxadoline** — SOD in ~**0.5%** of patients without a gallbladder; confirmed in ~2000 patients with diarrhea-predominant IBS, 10 developing sphincter of Oddi spasm, **most at the higher 100 mg dose**. FDA Adverse Event Reporting System received **99 cases of pancreatitis and 39 of sphincter of Oddi spasm within 10 months** of United States availability. FDA warning (March 15, 2017): **eluxadoline should not be used in patients without a gallbladder** — two deaths in prior-cholecystectomy patients, one from pancreatitis within 1 hour of a single dose, one from sphincter of Oddi spasm after the first dose.

### Narcotic bowel syndrome
- **Definition:** persistent moderate to severe **daily** abdominal pain of **more than 3 months'** duration, in patients requiring **more than 100 mg morphine equivalent per day**; the pain does not respond and **may actually increase in response to escalating doses** of narcotics.
- A disease of **nociception**, independent of opioid effects on gut motility and secretion — this is what separates it from the other opioid gastrointestinal syndromes.
- Prevalence estimated at **4%** (2009 Olmsted County epidemiologic study).
- **Diagnosis suggested by:** a patient on chronic opiates with chronic generalised colicky abdominal pain despite escalating doses, and **worsening of pain with tapering**. May be accompanied by nausea and vomiting. An extensive negative evaluation usually ensues; emergency presentations are common.
- **Five postulated mechanisms:** (1) descending inhibition from the medulla becomes a *facilitator* of pain signals through neuroplastic change; (2) spinal glial cell inflammation via toll-like receptor activation → neuropathic pain; (3) abnormal N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor function at the spinal cord; (4) G-protein coupled receptor activation exciting dorsal root ganglia; (5) abnormalities of central pain processing — in 39 patients with NBS, a high prevalence of psychological traits and traumatic exposures linked to altered brain processing of painful signals.
- **Treatment:** difficult — detoxification with substitution of opioids by non-opiate medications to control pain, anxiety, and withdrawal, **including clonidine**. Best handled through specialists/centres with expertise in opiate dependence. **Recidivism ~50%**; those who remain off narcotics report improvement in pain.

### Opioid-induced constipation
- Prevalence **41%–81%**. In chronic non-cancer pain, 80% have ≥1 adverse event — constipation **41%**, nausea 32%, somnolence 29%.
- **Definition:** change from baseline in bowel habits and defecation patterns after initiating opioid therapy, characterised by any of: reduced spontaneous bowel movement (SBM) frequency (<3/week), worsening straining, sense of incomplete evacuation, harder stool consistency. **Can occur even at low opioid doses and at any time after initiation.**
- **Rome IV diagnostic criteria (Table 3), verbatim:** "1. New, or worsening, symptoms of constipation when initiating, changing, or increasing opioid therapy that must include 2 or more of the following: a. Straining during more than one-fourth (25%) of defecations; b. Lumpy or hard stools (Bristol Stool Form Scale 1–2) more than one-fourth (25%) of defecations; c. Sensation of incomplete evacuation more than one-fourth (25%) of defecations; d. Sensation of anorectal obstruction/blockage more than one-fourth (25%) of defecations; e. Manual maneuvers to facilitate more than one-fourth (25%) of defecations (eg, digital evacuation, support of the pelvic floor); f. Fewer than 3 spontaneous bowel movements per week. 2. Loose stools are rarely present without the use of laxatives."
- **Bowel function index (BFI)** — clinician assessment tool: ease of defecation, feeling of incomplete evacuation, personal judgment of constipation. Each rated **0 to 100** by the patient on the basis of experience over **7 days**.
  - **Reference range for non-constipated patients: 0 to 28.8.**
  - **BFI ≥30 is the recommended criterion** to identify patients on laxatives for whom prescriptions of FDA-approved OIC therapies are justified; **insufficient laxative benefit is evidenced by a BFI score >30 points**.
  - Reassessment of the BFI is useful to monitor improvement.

### OIC treatment sequence and agents
- **Laxatives first-line** in patients with a diagnosis of OIC; European Association for Palliative Care recommends laxatives for prophylaxis or management in cancer patients. Prophylaxis is seldom actually prescribed.
  - Polyethylene glycol **13.81 g daily** and sodium picosulfate **10 mg daily** are more efficacious than lactulose for outpatients with cancer on opioid therapy.
- **If BFI >30 despite laxatives** → PAMORA, oxycodone/naloxone combination, or lubiprostone.
- **Lubiprostone** — bicyclic fatty acid from a prostaglandin E₁ metabolite; stimulates cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator and type 2 chloride channels. Increased SBMs/week and **reduced time to first bowel movement by 50%**. **FDA-approved at 24 µg twice daily** for OIC in non-cancer pain. **Contraindicated in OIC associated with methadone use** — methadone inhibited lubiprostone-stimulated chloride secretion in vitro.
- **Methylnaltrexone** — quaternary N-methyl derivative of naltrexone; methyl group prevents brain entry. 12 mg once daily or every other day shortened time to first rescue-free bowel movement. **FDA-approved as 12 mg subcutaneous injection** for OIC in chronic non-cancer pain. **7 cases of gastric or intestinal perforation** in the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System in the first 18 months after approval (causation not established).
- **Naloxegol** — pegylated naloxone derivative; does not cross the blood–brain barrier, and P-glycoprotein transports it out of the central nervous system. Improved SBMs from the first week even in laxative-inadequate responders; no effect on miosis or withdrawal. Over 52 weeks: most common side effects abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea, headache, flatulence; **no QT/QTc prolongation or serious adverse events**. FDA-approved at **12.5 or 25 mg** orally, with monitoring for adverse cardiovascular events.
- **Oxycodone prolonged release (PR) + naloxone PR** — fixed ratio **2:1**, approved at maximum dose **40 mg and 20 mg** respectively; superior to oxycodone PR alone. Naloxone PR bioavailability **<2%** after hepatic glucuronidation, vs oxycodone bioavailability **80%**, so analgesia is preserved. Decreased BFI by **48.5 units** (0–100 scale), tripled median complete SBMs/week. **13% incidence of severe adverse events.** Naloxone PR reduced mean colonic transit time by **2.1 hours** with oxycodone PR 20 mg/naloxone PR 10 mg vs oxycodone PR 20 mg alone.
- **Alvimopan** — PAMORA approved in the United States for **postoperative ileus after bowel resection**. Meta-analysis of 9 randomised trials, 4075 patients: significantly decreased time to first passage of stool and lowered serious side effects, without compromising opioid analgesia.
- **An improvement in SBM frequency by ≥3 per week** was associated with consistent improvement in patient-reported outcomes.

### Opioid agents used therapeutically in functional disease
- **Loperamide** — synthetic peripheral μ agonist; does not cross the blood–brain barrier. Delays intestinal transit, decreases stool frequency, increases water and ion absorption, improves stool consistency and urgency; may improve anal sphincter tone. **Does not significantly affect pain perception in IBS**, though pain associated with diarrheal attacks may be reduced. **Typical doses: 2 mg after each loose bowel movement (usually <8 mg/day), or 2–4 mg preprandially** in IBS with prominent postprandial diarrhea.
- **Eluxadoline** — μ and κ agonist, δ antagonist, minimal oral bioavailability. At 100 mg and 200 mg improved bowel movement frequency, urgency, global symptoms, IBS Symptom Severity Score, quality of life, adequate relief; pivotal trials **number needed to treat ~8**, though **abdominal pain scores were not significant for the 75 or 100 mg doses**. Pancreatitis and sphincter of Oddi spasm each **0.3%** in controlled trials → exclusions for history of bile duct obstruction, pancreatitis, severe liver impairment, severe constipation, or **>3 alcoholic beverages per day**.
- **μ opioid agonists in IBS:** used in **35% of pain attacks**; diarrhea-predominant IBS patients used opioids in **32%** of attacks vs 20% (constipation-predominant) and 19% (mixed). **"There are no randomized, controlled trials of the use of μ opioid agonists in the treatment of chronic pain in patients with IBS… there is no evidence for use of μ opioid agonists for the pain of IBS."**
- Among non-cancer patients presenting to an emergency department with acute abdominal pain, **~19% (442 of 2354)** were on μ opioid agonists for >3 months; in **21% (93/442)** the indication for the opioid *was* abdominal pain.

### Nausea and emesis
- Primary mechanism is **central** — direct stimulation of the chemoreceptor trigger zone in the area postrema (floor of the fourth ventricle); peripheral delay of gastric emptying contributes.
- Adding a prokinetic (eg, metoclopramide), prochlorperazine, or a 5-HT₃ antagonist is beneficial, especially in postoperative pain control.

### Context
- WHO 3-step analgesic ladder: step 1 non-opioids (aspirin, acetaminophen, diclofenac, ibuprofen); step 2 mild opioids (codeine, tramadol); step 3 strong opioids (morphine, buprenorphine, fentanyl, hydromorphone, methadone, oxycodone).
- Chronic pain = persistent pain >3 months; affects 10%–15% of the general population. **4% of United States adults take chronic opioid therapy**, chiefly for non-cancer pain.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **The published naloxegol dosing frequency is internally inconsistent.** The text states the FDA "approved naloxegol at 12.5 or 25 mg 4 times a day orally", but the trials it cites and tabulates (Webster 2013; Chey 2014) administered naloxegol on a daily basis, and the review reports their outcomes as change in SBMs per week from a daily regimen. The strengths (12.5 and 25 mg) are consistent throughout; **the frequency as printed is not supported by the document's own cited trials**. Entity pages carry the strengths and do not assert the frequency.
- **Acute vs chronic esophageal effects point in opposite directions** — acute morphine reduces transient LES relaxations and LES pressure, while chronic use is associated with impaired LES relaxation and outflow obstruction. The review flags this as genuinely unresolved ("a different esophageal physiology with acute and chronic usage") rather than reconciling it.
- **No numbered or graded recommendations exist in this document** (see the note under Bibliographic Info). Any wiki or external text attributing a numbered AGA Best Practice Advice to this Clinical Practice Update is in error.
- **vs [[aga-2019-opioid-induced-constipation]] (newer, same tier).** The 2019 update supersedes this one on overlapping OIC claims. Notably, AGA 2019 prints no PAMORA doses, whereas this 2017 review does — so the doses here fill a gap rather than conflict.
- **Bristol Stool Form Scale types 1–2** are invoked in the Rome IV criteria, but the scale itself is not printed in this source.
