# Local Postprocedural Cryoprevention Significantly Reduces the Incidence of Post-ERCP Pancreatitis (EUTOPIA)

## Summary

Multicenter Japanese RCT testing whether **irrigating the duodenal papilla with ice water at the end of [[ercp|ERCP]]** ("cryoprevention") reduces [[ercp#Complications|post-ERCP pancreatitis (PEP)]]. The rationale is mechanical: PEP is thought to begin with papillary oedema obstructing pancreatic outflow, and local cooling — the standard of care for acute soft-tissue injury in sports medicine — constricts local vessels, reduces vascular permeability, and blunts oedema formation. The technique requires no drug and no specialized device: 250 mL of ice water, a syringe, and the working channel of the duodenoscope, adding ~2 minutes to the procedure.

Conducted at **8 hospitals in Japan, March 2022–February 2024**, in adults ≥20 years with a **native papilla**. 880 randomized 1:1; 3 ice-water patients withdrew consent after randomization, leaving **877 analyzed (cryoprevention 434, control 443)**. The control arm received **no irrigation of any kind** — not room-temperature water — and **no patient in either arm received NSAIDs**, because rectal NSAIDs are not approved for PEP prophylaxis under Japanese national health insurance and the available indomethacin formulation there is 50 mg rather than the internationally recommended 100 mg.

PEP occurred in **3.2% with cryoprevention vs 6.8% with no irrigation (P = 0.02; RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.26–0.89)** — an absolute risk reduction of 3.6 percentage points, a relative risk reduction of 52.4%, and an **NNT of 28 (95% CI 18–57)**. The reduction was carried by **mild** PEP; the moderate-to-severe difference (0.7% vs 1.4%) was in the same direction but not significant and the trial was underpowered for it. There were no differences in cholangitis, bleeding, or perforation, and **no adverse event was attributed to the ice-water irrigation**. The single death was in the control arm, from severe PEP.

The two limitations that govern how far this transfers: **no arm received rectal NSAIDs** (so the effect of ice water *added to* standard prophylaxis is untested), and **the control arm received no irrigation at all** (so the benefit of cooling cannot be separated from the benefit of irrigating/washing the papilla with any fluid). The authors state both explicitly and call for a three-arm trial — ice water, room-temperature water, no irrigation — and for a comparison of ice water plus rectal NSAIDs against rectal NSAIDs alone.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Population and design**

- **Setting/period:** 8 Japanese hospitals, March 2022 – February 2024. Randomization 1:1 by 24 h/d web system, permuted blocks of varying size, **stratified by center and by ERCP indication (bile duct cannulation vs pancreatic duct cannulation)**.
- **Blinding:** patients blinded to allocation (to avoid biasing the report of abdominal pain); the endoscopist could not be blinded, and separate blinded ERCP/assessing physicians were not feasible at general hospitals — so the physician adjudicating PEP was **not** blinded.
- **Eligible:** age ≥20 with a **native papilla**. **Excluded:** ECOG performance status 4; age <20; predicted papilla inaccessibility (e.g. duodenal obstruction); postoperative reconstructed intestinal tract other than Billroth-I; acute pancreatitis; [[chronic-pancreatitis|chronic pancreatitis]]; [[pancreatic-cancer|pancreatic head cancer]] with occlusion of the main pancreatic duct; inability to consent; patient deemed inappropriate for the trial. (Chronic pancreatitis and obstructing pancreatic head cancer were excluded because they carry a *lower* baseline PEP risk.)
- **Baseline:** median age 74 (range 26–99) vs 75 (23–103); female 43.3% vs 43.1%; indication [[choledocholithiasis|choledocholithiasis]] 71.0% vs 67.5%; high-risk patients 83.9% vs 83.7%; difficult cannulation 55.1% vs 59.4%; endoscopic sphincterotomy 74.9% vs 70.9%; trainee involvement (<200 ERCPs) 63.5% vs 63.2%. No significant baseline differences.

**Intervention — the actual technique**

- Performed **after the ERCP is complete**, with the patient under conscious sedation, side-viewing duodenoscope (Olympus TJF-260V or TJF-Q290V).
- **250 mL of ice water** — water chilled in a refrigerator with **10 small ice cubes** — irrigated toward the papilla **through the working channel with a syringe, in five 50 mL increments**.
- **Aspirate the duodenal fluid after each 50 mL**, then repeat; five irrigate–aspirate cycles in total. The scope is withdrawn **without aspirating the final bolus**.
- Takes **approximately 2 minutes**. **No NSAIDs were administered** in either arm.
- Control arm: **no irrigation** (not a sham/room-temperature irrigation).

**Primary endpoint definition (modified Cotton criteria)**

- **PEP = new abdominal pain within the first 24 h after ERCP *plus* serum amylase or lipase ≥3× the upper limit of normal.** Two deliberate modifications to the original [[ercp#Defining PEP|Cotton criteria]]: enzymes were sampled **within** 24 h (routine Japanese practice is the next morning) rather than strictly *after* 24 h, and the Cotton requirement for **≥2 nights of hospitalization was omitted**, since a 2-night stay is not unusual in Japanese practice regardless of PEP.
- **Severity, by modified Cotton criteria:** **mild** = fasting required 2–3 days; **moderate** = fasting 4–10 days; **severe** = fasting ≥11 days. In addition, necrosis or pseudocyst formation, or percutaneous drainage or surgery, defined **severe**. The authors chose modified Cotton over the Marshall score because the latter "may not be adequately capturing certain clinical features specific to post-ERCP pancreatitis."

**Trial's own high-risk definition (≥1 factor = high risk)**

| Patient-related | Procedure-related |
|---|---|
| Female sex | Precut papillotomy for difficult bile duct cannulation |
| Age <60 years | Endoscopic pancreatic sphincterotomy |
| Clinically suspected [[sphincter-of-oddi-dysfunction\|sphincter of Oddi dysfunction]] | Endoscopic papillary balloon dilation |
| History of [[recurrent-acute-pancreatitis\|recurrent pancreatitis]] | Difficulty in bile duct / pancreatic duct cannulation |
| History of PEP | Contrast irrigation into the pancreatic duct |

- **Difficult cannulation was defined as** contact with the papilla **>5 times**, cannulation time **>5 minutes**, **or** unintended pancreatic duct cannulation **>2 times**.

**Primary and safety outcomes (Table 3)**

| Outcome | Ice water (n = 434) | Control (n = 443) | P | RR (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **PEP (primary)** | **14 (3.2%)** | **30 (6.8%)** | **0.02** | **0.48 (0.26–0.89)** |
| Mild | 11 (2.5%) | 24 (5.4%) | 0.04 | 0.46 (0.23–0.94) |
| Moderate–severe | 3 (0.7%) | 6 (1.4%) | 0.51 | 0.51 (0.13–2.03) |
| Cholangitis | 4 (0.9%) | 5 (1.1%) | 1.00 | 0.82 (0.22–3.02) |
| Bleeding | 6 (1.4%) | 11 (2.5%) | 0.33 | 0.56 (0.21–1.49) |
| Perforation | 4 (0.9%) | 1 (0.2%) | 0.21 | 4.08 (0.46–36.38) |
| Mortality | 0 (0%) | 1 (0.2%) | N/A | N/A |

- 95% CI for the PEP rates themselves (Clopper–Pearson): **3.2% (1.7–5.4)** vs **6.8% (4.6–9.6)**. ARR **3.6 points**; RRR **52.4%**; **NNT 28 (95% CI 18–57)**.
- **No adverse events were attributed to the ice-water irrigation.** The one death: a control patient who had ERCP for CBD stones, failed biliary cannulation, received a pancreatic stent only, developed PEP the next day, then circulatory failure, arrhythmias, duodenal perforation and intra-abdominal abscess, and died on day 14 after declining surgery.

**Subgroups (Figure 2 — exploratory, no interaction test was significant)**

- Benefit was directionally consistent across most subgroups, and reached significance in: **high-risk patients RR 0.49 (0.26–0.92)**; **patients who underwent endoscopic sphincterotomy RR 0.36 (0.15–0.83)**; **age ≥60 RR 0.41 (0.19–0.88)**; **difficult cannulation RR 0.44 (0.22–0.90)**; **men RR 0.38 (0.15–0.97)**; **wire-guided cannulation RR 0.27 (0.08–0.93)**; **CBD as target duct RR 0.48 (0.25–0.95)**.
- **The one subgroup with no signal of benefit: patients who received a prophylactic pancreatic stent (EPS).** With EPS, PEP occurred in **5 of 34** ice-water vs **3 of 29** control (**RR 1.42, 95% CI 0.37–5.45**); without EPS, **9 of 400** vs **27 of 414** (**RR 0.35, 0.16–0.72**). **P for interaction = 0.075.** The authors read this as mechanistically coherent — a pancreatic stent already mitigates papillary oedema, so cooling adds nothing on top of it — and as further support for oedema being central to PEP. It is exploratory and hypothesis-generating.
- Among patients with **intended** biliary cannulation who received a prophylactic pancreatic stent, PEP occurred in 4 of 31 (ice water) vs 3 of 28 (control).

**Power and statistics**

- Powered on an assumed **10% baseline PEP rate**, 2-sided α = 0.05, power 0.8, to detect a **50% relative reduction**: 434 per arm required; 440 per arm targeted to allow for loss/withdrawal.
- Fisher exact test for primary and safety outcomes; 95% CIs by the **Clopper–Pearson method**; Z test for subgroup interaction; JMP v13.0. Missing variables treated as missing data with no special processing. A per-protocol sensitivity analysis was also performed.

**Stated limitations**

1. **No NSAIDs were used in either arm**, which limits generalizability to settings where rectal NSAIDs are standard. The authors defend this as ethically appropriate *in Japan*, where the indication is not covered by national health insurance and rectal NSAIDs are not routinely implemented — but state plainly that the **additive or synergistic effect of ice water plus rectal NSAIDs is the necessary next trial.**
2. **The control arm received no irrigation at all.** Because there was no room-temperature-water arm, **the possibility that irrigation itself — regardless of temperature — produced the benefit cannot be excluded.** The ideal design would have had three arms (ice water / room-temperature water / no irrigation); it was not used because of the extended cooling period, limited resources, and staff burden.
3. **The 250 mL volume has no scientific basis** — it was carried over from the authors' earlier single-center prospective study. A longer cooling duration or a different volume might perform better.
4. **No double blinding** — the physician assessing whether PEP occurred knew the allocation.
5. **The observed control-arm PEP incidence (6.8%) was lower than the assumed 10%**, which may have reduced statistical power; the lower-than-expected baseline is a limitation when interpreting the result.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No contradiction with [[asge-2023-post-ercp-pancreatitis|ASGE 2023]]** — cryoprevention is not an intervention that guideline evaluated. The trial does not test, and therefore does not challenge, rectal NSAIDs, wire-guided cannulation, PD stents, or aggressive hydration.
- **Cooling vs irrigation is unresolved by design.** Without a room-temperature-water comparator, "ice water works" and "washing the papilla works" are both consistent with the data.
- **Unknown on top of standard prophylaxis.** Every patient in this trial went without rectal NSAIDs. Whether ice water adds anything to a patient who has received 100 mg rectal indomethacin is untested.
- **Possible loss of effect when a prophylactic pancreatic stent is placed** (RR 1.42 with EPS vs 0.35 without; interaction P = 0.075) — exploratory, small numbers (8 PEP events across 63 stented patients), and not a basis for withholding either intervention.
- **Underpowered for the outcome that matters most.** Moderate-to-severe PEP fell by roughly half in relative terms (0.7% vs 1.4%) but with 9 events in total; whether cryoprevention prevents *severe* PEP is unanswered.
- **Generalizability of the definition.** PEP incidence here was measured by a *modified* Cotton definition (enzymes within 24 h, hospitalization criterion dropped), which is not the definition used in the Western trials underlying [[asge-2023-post-ercp-pancreatitis|ASGE 2023]] — event rates are not directly comparable across those studies.
- **Single-country, elderly cohort** (median age ~74) with 84% classified high-risk; all 8 sites Japanese general hospitals.
