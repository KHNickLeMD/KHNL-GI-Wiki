## Summary

Official AGA Institute recommendations on identifying cases of [[lynch-syndrome|Lynch syndrome]] (LS) and managing colorectal cancer risk in those affected. LS — previously hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer — is the most common heritable [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]] syndrome, accounting for 2%–3% of colorectal cancers, with an estimated general-population prevalence of **1 in 440**. Lifetime cumulative incidence is up to **80% for colorectal cancer** and up to **60% for endometrial cancer**, with increased risk of stomach, small intestine, pancreas, biliary tract, ovary, urinary tract, and brain cancers. The guideline states the syndrome is often underdiagnosed.

Scope is deliberately narrow. The guideline covers case-finding (risk prediction models, tumor testing, second-stage *BRAF*/*MLH1* promoter testing, germline testing) and colorectal cancer risk management (surveillance [[colonoscopy]], aspirin chemoprevention). It **does not** address screening for noncolorectal cancers or the surgical management of colorectal cancer in LS patients — the authors state that their systematic-review-and-meta-analysis methodology did not permit that breadth, that the [[usmstf-2014-lynch-syndrome|US Multi-Society Task Force]] guideline (endorsed by the AGA) does cover those areas, and that **the two guidelines should be viewed as complementary**.

Method: GRADE, with a companion technical review (Ladabaum et al., *Gastroenterology* 2015;149:783–812) supplying original meta-analyses. Quality of evidence was graded high / moderate / low / very low (Table 1) and strength of recommendation as strong, weak/conditional, or no recommendation (Table 2). The technical review's more precise summary estimates are given as the explicit reason for discrepancies in evidence ratings versus the USMSTF guideline. Recommendations were combined into a clinical decision support tool (Figure 1), then opened to public comment, edited, and approved by the AGA Governing Board.

Most recommendations are conditional. The one statement the authors single out as possibly ripe for use as a quality-of-care process measure is **tumor testing in newly diagnosed cases of colorectal cancer to identify cases of Lynch syndrome**.

## Recommendations

**The guideline does not number its recommendations.** Seven recommendation statements appear as boxed text in the body and are tabulated in PICO form in Table 3. Each carries a strength and a quality-of-evidence rating, reproduced verbatim below.

- "In patients without a personal history of colorectal or another cancer but with a family history suggestive of Lynch syndrome, the AGA suggests that risk prediction models be offered rather than doing nothing. **Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.**"
- "In patients without a personal history of colorectal or another cancer but with a family history suggestive of Lynch syndrome, the AGA suggests that risk prediction models be offered rather than proceeding directly with germline genetic testing. **Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.**"
- "The AGA recommends testing the tumors of all patients with colorectal cancer with either immunohistochemistry (IHC) or for microsatellite instability (MSI) to identify potential cases of Lynch syndrome versus doing no testing for Lynch syndrome. **Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.**"
- "The AGA suggests that in patients with colorectal cancer with IHC absent for *MLH1*, second-stage tumor testing for a *BRAF* mutation or for hypermethylation of the *MLH1* promoter should be performed rather than proceeding directly to germline genetic testing. **Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.**"
- "The AGA recommends surveillance colonoscopy (versus doing nothing) in persons with Lynch syndrome. **Strong recommendation, moderate quality of evidence.**"
- "The AGA suggests that surveillance colonoscopy should be performed every 1 to 2 years versus less frequent intervals. **Conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence.**"
- "The AGA suggests that aspirin be offered for cancer prevention in patients with Lynch syndrome. **Conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence.**"

**Explicit non-recommendation:** "The AGA makes no recommendation regarding the use of IHC versus MSI or the use of both IHC and MSI due to low quality of evidence." IHC and MSI have comparable sensitivities and specificities; implementation varies with local expertise and availability.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Epidemiology**
- LS accounts for **2%–3%** of colorectal cancers; estimated general-population prevalence **1 in 440**.
- Lifetime cumulative incidence: colorectal cancer **up to 80%**, endometrial cancer **up to 60%**; also increased risk of stomach, small intestine, pancreas, biliary tract, ovary, urinary tract, and brain cancer.

**Case-finding without a personal history of cancer**
- If there is a **first-degree relative with a known LS mutation** → offer germline genetic testing for that specific mutation.
- If not, but **tumor tissue from an affected relative is available** → begin the screening process by testing that tumor.
- Otherwise → estimate mutation probability with **PREMM₁,₂,₆** (<http://premm.dfci.harvard.edu/>) or downloadable **MMRpro** software (<http://www4.utsouthwestern.edu/breasthealth/cagene/>). "The available evidence cannot support the preferential use of PREMM₁,₂,₆ or MMRpro over the other."
- **MMRpredict is not relevant to this population** — it predicts a mutation in a patient with *known* cancer and requires details of the cancer.
- Cost-effectiveness threshold: **>5% predicted probability** of carrying an LS mutation should prompt germline genetic testing **if universally applied to 25-year-old patients**; the threshold **could be lower in middle-aged adults** and as the cost of genetic testing decreases. Above the threshold, offer germline testing for **MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2**.
- Patients **already at high risk** (e.g., meeting the highly specific Amsterdam criteria) do **not** need a risk prediction model before proceeding to germline testing.
- Evidence for the models in this population was graded **very low**: observational, strong risk of bias, downgraded further for indirectness/poor applicability because the models were primarily tested in patients with a personal history of cancer. The AGA recommends them anyway because sensitivity/specificity are expected to be reasonably similar and because most LS kindreds likely remain undiagnosed.

**Tumor testing**
- Test **all** colorectal cancers by IHC or MSI (strong, moderate). Evidence was upgraded from observational data by the strength of association and cost data.
- **Older patients with colorectal cancer should also be tested** — traditionally excluded for lower yield, but LS can present in elderly patients and the finding may significantly affect younger family members; supported by cost-effectiveness analyses.
- Of patients with **absence of MLH1 on IHC, approximately 75% will have sporadic cancers** rather than LS. Sporadic MSI-high colorectal cancers usually show epigenetic loss of MLH1 via promoter hypermethylation; the *BRAF* V600E mutation is associated with this somatic, acquired loss.
- If **either** second-stage test (hypermethylation or *BRAF*) is positive → LS is extremely unlikely. If *MLH1* is absent on IHC **without** hypermethylation or a *BRAF* mutation → germline testing for LS is recommended.
- **Second-stage testing is not perfectly specific.** The USMSTF's strong recommendation assumed such testing was 100% specific for sporadic tumors; the technical review's meta-analysis found *BRAF* mutations and *MLH1* promoter hypermethylation **also occur in some patients with Lynch syndrome**. Second-step testing may therefore miss a small proportion of LS cases — "**no more than 10%, but likely substantially fewer than that**." Graded very low; the AGA weighed the cost and anxiety of germline testing in all MLH1-absent patients, the great majority of whom have sporadic cancer, and still conditionally favored second-stage testing.
- A system for systematic follow-up of all positive results must be in place; IHC results require appropriately trained and experienced pathologists.

**Surveillance colonoscopy**
- Technical review meta-analysis: surveillance colonoscopy in LS was associated with **decreased burden of colorectal cancer (OR 0.23; 95% CI 0.13–0.41)** and **decreased colorectal cancer mortality (OR 0.06; 95% CI 0.00–0.93)**.
- Cost-effectiveness model: frequent colonoscopic surveillance of gene carriers **increased life expectancy by 7 years**, and **costs of surveillance were less than the costs of no surveillance** for colorectal cancer.
- Start age: "Conventional practice has suggested commencing surveillance at either **20 to 25 years of age or 5 years before the youngest age of diagnosis of colorectal cancer in an affected family member, whichever occurs first.**"
- Interval: best interval **remains unknown**; **every 1 to 2 years is most prudent**. No identifiable studies directly compared surveillance intervals. Most colorectal cancers diagnosed in LS patients under surveillance are detected in 1- to 2-year intervals and are usually detected at a treatable stage.
- **Gene-specific tailoring is unsupported:** MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2 carry different long-term risks, but "no data exist to directly guide the choice of screening interval or the age of initiation of screening specific to those mutations."

**Aspirin chemoprevention**
- One high-quality RCT in adults with LS tested **aspirin 600 mg daily vs placebo for up to 4 years**: decreased incidence of colorectal cancer **beyond colonoscopy surveillance alone — incidence rate ratio 0.56 (95% CI 0.32–0.99)** — and a trend toward decreased incidence of other cancers.
- Evidence **downgraded to low for imprecision**; **no mortality data** support a benefit from long-term aspirin therapy (very low quality for that outcome).
- Harms: **1% risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and <1% risk of stroke greater than placebo — not statistically significant.**
- **Recommended dose and frequency are unknown.** The dose tested was high and uncertainties about risks versus benefits remain, so an individualized approach considering the patient's personal risk of adverse events is best. No study to date has examined the cost-effectiveness of aspirin chemoprevention in adults with LS.

**Priorities for future research (as listed)**
- Validating calibration of predictive models in patients without a personal history of cancer.
- Identifying the threshold predicted probability that should prompt germline genetic testing.
- Identifying germline genetic testing that together provides 100% sensitivity for LS.
- Updating cost-effectiveness analyses with new estimates of the accuracy of *BRAF* mutation and *MLH1* promoter hypermethylation testing, and as the cost of germline genetic testing decreases.
- Optimizing the time of onset of colonoscopy in patients with LS.
- Identifying the optimal dose and frequency of aspirin for chemoprevention (an RCT was enrolling at the time of publication).

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Second-stage *BRAF* / *MLH1* promoter testing — specificity and strength.** USMSTF 2014 makes a **strong** recommendation for second-step testing, on the assumption that a positive result is **100% specific for sporadic tumors**. This guideline's meta-analysis found *BRAF* mutations and *MLH1* hypermethylation also occur in some LS patients, so second-step testing may miss LS in **no more than 10% (likely substantially fewer)** of cases; the AGA therefore grades the same practice **conditional, very low quality**. Both guidelines still advise doing the second-stage test. **The page asserts the AGA 2015 framing** (newer publication date, same tier) and names the miss rate.
- **Aspirin — recommended or not.** This guideline **suggests aspirin be offered** for cancer prevention (conditional, low). ACG 2015 concluded the evidence "is not sufficiently robust to recommend standard use." **The page carries the AGA position as the recommendation** and retains the ACG framing alongside it, since both are 2015 and clinicians will have seen either.
- **Aspirin — which CAPP2 effect estimate.** This guideline cites an **incidence rate ratio of 0.56 (95% CI 0.32–0.99)** for colorectal cancer, an estimate whose confidence interval excludes 1. ACG 2015 cites, from the same trial, an intention-to-treat **HR 0.63 (95% CI 0.35–1.13, P = 0.12)** for time to first colorectal cancer — not significant — and HR 0.65 (95% CI 0.42–1.00, P = 0.05) for all LS cancers. USMSTF 2017 describes a >60% reduction among those taking aspirin ≥2 years. These are different analyses of one trial; the page reports them side by side rather than choosing one number.
- **Prevalence and share of colorectal cancer.** AGA 2015: **2%–3%** of colorectal cancers, prevalence **1 in 440**. USMSTF 2014: 2%–4% of colorectal cancers, ~1 in 279. ACG 2015: 1%–3%. Page states the AGA figures with the range across guidelines noted.
- **Gene-specific start age.** ACG 2015 and USMSTF 2014 suggest considering colonoscopy from **age 25–30 in MSH6/PMS2** carriers given later onset. AGA 2015 states **no data exist to directly guide interval or start age by mutation.** Both stay on the page: the gene-specific option is a "may consider," and the AGA sentence is what explains why it is not a requirement.
- **Risk prediction models before germline testing.** USMSTF 2014 gives PREMM₁,₂,₆ ≥5% as a testing threshold; AGA 2015 recommends models be used *preferentially over proceeding straight to germline testing* in people without a personal history of cancer — but adds that the >5% threshold was derived for universal application to **25-year-olds** and could be lower in middle-aged adults. The threshold is not a fixed number across ages.
- **Unresolved by this guideline:** which of IHC or MSI (or both) to use; the optimal aspirin dose, frequency, and duration; the threshold probability that should trigger germline testing; the optimal colonoscopy start age; and whether any germline testing strategy reaches 100% sensitivity.
