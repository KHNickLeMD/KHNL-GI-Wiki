## Summary

A three-author AGA commentary written to close an acknowledged education gap: a survey showed many gastroenterologists were not informed about biosimilars or their approval process. It covers what a biosimilar is, how the FDA approves one, what extrapolation and interchangeability mean, the CT-P13 switching data in IBD, and how to handle a switch in practice.

The central regulatory distinction is that **generic drugs are identical to their reference product** (usually a small molecule) whereas **biosimilars are highly similar but not identical** to a reference product that is usually a large, complex protein. Biologics are made in living systems (human, animal, or microbial), so their precise structure cannot be fully characterized with available analytic techniques. Each manufacturing step — cloning and protein expression, cell expansion, bioreactor production, recovery, purification, validation — introduces opportunity for variation in primary amino acid sequence, protein folding, protein–protein interactions, and amino-acid modification such as glycosylation, and those differences can alter immunogenicity. Because biosimilars are not identical, they cannot use the generic pathway.

Clinically, the evidence base in IBD is postmarketing and switch-focused: **no primary equivalence study of a biosimilar has been performed in an IBD population.** The taken-together data in Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) support equivalent efficacy and safety for biosimilar infliximab, with neither increased loss of response nor increased immunogenicity after transition. The commentary's sharpest clinical rule is not about efficacy but about antibodies — antidrug antibodies **cross-react** between originator infliximab and CT-P13, so a patient who has developed antibodies to one must not be switched to the other.

Practically, the authors endorse biosimilars for **new starts** of infliximab; treat transition of a patient already in remission as an **individualized, shared decision** made by the IBD provider with the patient; and single out **children and pregnant women in stable remission** for special caution, advising against nonmedical switching during the antenatal period. At the time of writing **no biologic for IBD had FDA interchangeability designation**, so pharmacy-level substitution rules remain state-by-state.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definitions and regulatory standards (FDA / PHSA wording)

| Term | Standard, as stated in the source |
|---|---|
| Generic drug | **Identical** to its reference product (often a small-molecule drug) |
| Biosimilar | **Highly similar but not identical** to its reference product (often a large, complex protein-based drug) |
| **Biosimilarity** (PHSA definition, §7002(b)(3) of the Affordable Care Act, adding §351(i)(2) of the PHSA) | "highly similar to the reference product **notwithstanding minor differences in clinically inactive components**" *and* "**no clinically meaningful differences** between the biological product and the reference product in terms of **safety, purity, and potency** of the product" |
| **Interchangeability** (BPCI additional requirements — a **higher** standard than biosimilarity) | Sponsor must demonstrate (1) the biosimilar **can produce the same clinical result as the reference product**, and (2) **no safety or efficacy differences if the interchangeable product and the originator are switched back and forth**. FDA recommends the comparator reference biologic be a **U.S.-licensed product** — a challenge, because most biologic switching studies were conducted in Europe. |
| Consequence of interchangeability | An interchangeable product **may be substituted for the reference product without the approval of the prescribing provider**. Pharmacy laws and practices **vary across states**, so even a product meeting the requirements may not be substitutable in all states. |

- Approval pathways: biologics are approved under the **Public Health Service Act (PHSA)**, amended by the **Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act of 2009 (BPCI)**, passed as part of the Affordable Care Act. Biologics require submission of clinical trials demonstrating safety and efficacy (**351(a) pathway**). Biosimilars are **not required to follow this same pathway** and use an **abbreviated pathway** — one that is nonetheless **more intensive than the process for generic drugs**.
- **FDA's recommended stepwise approach to demonstrating biosimilarity** requires demonstration that the biosimilar is similar to the originator in: **structure, function, animal toxicity, human pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD), immunogenicity, and clinical safety and efficacy.** The approach is **not intended to re-establish the safety and effectiveness** of a product, but to assess biosimilarity between proposed and reference product.
  - Sequence: structural/functional comparison → initial assessment determines what additional studies are needed → **animal studies are required** (toxicity, further support of biosimilarity) → after analytic and animal studies, **FDA review of data** to address remaining uncertainties **before human clinical trials** → finally comparative human **PK and PD (as appropriate)**, **clinical immunogenicity** studies, and **comparative clinical studies of safety and efficacy**.
- **Totality of evidence:** FDA looks at the totality of the evidence provided by a sponsor. Even with minor differences between the proposed biologic and the originator, the product **may meet FDA's criteria for biosimilarity if** there is sufficient evidence that (a) the identified differences are **not clinically meaningful** and (b) there are **no significant concerns related to the safety and purity** of the proposed agent.

### Extrapolation
- FDA **allows extrapolation** of comparative clinical data **across indications**; a sponsor fulfilling PHSA requirements may seek licensure for **more than one condition for which the reference product is approved**, including an indication **not formally studied** with the proposed biosimilar.
- **The applicant must justify extrapolation** by demonstrating that the product's **mechanism of action, PK and PD properties, immunogenicity, and expected toxicities do not differ across patient groups.**
- Worked example: Inflectra-vs-Remicade PK studies were carried out in **ankylosing spondylitis** (PLANETAS) and comparative clinical effectiveness trials in **rheumatoid arthritis** (PLANETRA); those data were **extrapolated to CD and UC**.

### Approval status at time of writing
- FDA Center for Drug Evaluation and Research had approved **7 biosimilars in the United States** overall.
- The **first biosimilar for IBD** in the U.S. was **CT-P13** (nonproprietary **infliximab-dyyb**, trade name **Inflectra**), a biosimilar of infliximab, approved **April 2016** for several indications including CD and UC.
- Of the **5 biosimilars approved for IBD, only 2 had been licensed** — reflecting the significant impediments between approval and licensing.
- **No biologic for the treatment of IBD had been designated by the FDA as interchangeable.**
- The full list of biologic therapies, related biosimilar and interchangeable products, and licensure dates is in the FDA **Purple Book**.

### Switching data in IBD
- **No primary equivalence study of a biosimilar has been performed in an IBD population.** The evidence is postmarketing experience, mostly with CT-P13, and mostly descriptive studies of patients on stable dosing transitioned from originator infliximab (Remicade) to CT-P13. Published switching studies in IBD are **from Europe and Asia**.
- Overall reading of the data: efficacy and safety of biosimilar infliximab are **equivalent** in the IBD population, with **neither an increased loss of response after transitioning nor increased immunogenicity.**

| Study | Population | Follow-up | Efficacy endpoints | Safety |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Primary endpoint: worsening disease activity during 52-week follow-up. All patients: **26.2% (IFX-R) vs 29.6% (CT-P13)**; CD: **21.2% vs 36.5%**ᵃ; UC: **9.1% vs 11.9%**ᵃ | No difference in detection of antidrug antibodies, trough drug levels, or frequency of adverse events |

- **NOR-SWITCH detail:** Norwegian-government-funded, prospective, **52-week, randomized, double-blind, noninferiority** trial enrolling patients across **all** infliximab indications who were receiving originator infliximab, randomized to continue originator or switch to CT-P13. Unique **endpoint was "disease worsening"** in any of the diseases for which originator infliximab is approved (rheumatoid arthritis, CD, UC, spondyloarthritis, psoriatic arthritis, plaque psoriasis) — **the endpoint is not validated.** No significant difference in loss of response, safety, or immunogenicity between arms.
  - Not powered for individual disease indications, but the breakdown reported: no significant difference in loss of response in CD or UC; the **CD risk difference slightly favored remaining on originator drug — risk difference −14.3%; 95% confidence interval (CI) −29.3 to 0.7**; the **UC patients were more balanced — risk difference −2.6%; 95% CI −15.2 to 10.0**.
- **SECURE trial:** infliximab levels **16 weeks after switching** from originator to biosimilar infliximab were **noninferior to baseline levels in both CD and UC**.

### Immunogenicity and cross-reactivity — the key clinical rule
- Antidrug antibodies to the biosimilar **do cross-react to the originator antibodies of infliximab**, as expected from the structure of the biosimilar antibodies.
- Demonstrated in a study of **125 IBD patients in Israel**: anti-originator infliximab (Remicade) antibodies in IBD **both recognize and functionally inhibit CT-P13** biosimilar infliximab **to a similar degree**.
- **Therefore: patients who develop antidrug antibodies to originator infliximab, or to the biosimilar, should NOT be switched to the biosimilar or the originator, respectively.** They would likely experience the same reaction, which may result in an **immediate hypersensitivity reaction such as anaphylaxis and could be life-threatening.**
- **Commercially available therapeutic drug assays can be used with the biosimilar as effectively as they are with the originator drugs.**
- The authors name recognition of antidrug-antibody cross-reactivity as a **remaining educational gap** for clinicians involved in recertification and administration of these agents.

### Considerations for use of biosimilars (near-verbatim practice guidance; unnumbered and ungraded in the original)
- **New starts:** "the available data support use of biosimilars in **new starts of infliximab**."
- **Transitioning a patient in remission:** "when given a choice, the decision to transition a patient who is in remission on an originator biologic to a biosimilar **or vice versa** should be made by the **IBD physician provider and individualized**, and **patients should be informed and involved in the decision-making process**."
- **Children and pregnant women:** "special consideration should be taken for transitioning to biosimilars in **children and pregnant women who are in stable remission**, as there is **limited pharmacokinetic, immunogenicity, efficacy, and safety data** in these vulnerable populations."
- **Antenatal period:** "because maintenance of remission during pregnancy is critical for achieving healthy outcomes for mother and fetus, **nonmedical switching should be avoided during the antenatal period until there is more safety data** on switching in this population."
- **Substitution logistics:** because none of the biosimilars has received the FDA designation of "interchangeability," they **have not demonstrated safety in multiple switches**, and therefore **still require notification determined on a state-by-state basis** from the pharmacist, infusion center, or payer.
- **Cost:** systematic reviews suggest **potentially substantial cost savings**, depending on the pricing of the biosimilar relative to the originator and on physician-prescribing practices. Whether savings to payers, infusion centers, hospitals, and health services are **passed on to patients** as reduced insurance premiums, copays, or increased access **remains to be seen** — the source gives **no dollar figures or percentage discounts**.

### Conclusions
- Biosimilars for IBD are anticipated to stay; further biosimilars to infliximab, to adalimumab, and to other monoclonal antibodies used in IBD are under development.
- **Conditional reassurance:** *provided* the regulatory pathway remains rigorous and postmarketing surveillance is performed adequately, clinicians and patients can be reassured that these agents will provide the **same well-described effectiveness for moderate-to-severe CD and UC without new safety concerns.**
- Future work: the real-world challenges of transitioning, and potential delays due to communications or supply lines.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No contradiction with existing wiki content, but a superseded fact.** [[anti-tnf-agents]] states (from [[acg-2025-crohns]]) that "**interchangeable** biosimilars can be substituted at the pharmacy level without provider intervention," which matches this source's definition. This source adds the 2018-era status that **no IBD biologic had FDA interchangeability designation** and only **2 of 5 approved IBD biosimilars had been licensed**. Those are point-in-time regulatory facts from 2018/2019 — the later ACG 2025 guidelines govern current practice, and this source's counts and Table 1 should be presented as the 2019 snapshot, not as current status.
- **Ustekinumab biosimilars** are asserted on [[il-23-and-il-12-23-inhibitors]] and [[ulcerative-colitis]] from ACG 2025. This source lists none — consistent with its publication date, not a conflict.
- **Adalimumab biosimilar switching data are absent.** Two adalimumab biosimilars appear in Table 1, but every switching study in Table 2 is infliximab CT-P13. The source's practice guidance names only "new starts of **infliximab**"; it does not state whether the switching evidence extends to adalimumab.
- **No primary equivalence trial exists in IBD.** All IBD efficacy support is extrapolated from ankylosing spondylitis and rheumatoid arthritis plus postmarketing switch cohorts, most of them uncontrolled and non-U.S.
- **NOR-SWITCH's endpoint is not validated** and the trial was not powered for CD or UC separately; the CD point estimate favored staying on originator, with a confidence interval crossing zero (−29.3 to 0.7). The source does not resolve whether this is signal or noise.
- **Multiple switching is unstudied.** The source states biosimilars "have not demonstrated safety in multiple switches"; it gives no guidance on switching between two different biosimilars of the same originator.
- **No numeric cost data.** Cost savings are described as "potentially substantial" with no figures, discount ranges, or thresholds.
- **No dosing content.** The source gives no doses, intervals, durations, or trough targets for any biologic or biosimilar.
