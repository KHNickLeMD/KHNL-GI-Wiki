- **Type:** guideline (GRADE-based clinical practice guideline)

## Summary

This ASGE Standards of Practice Committee guideline provides an evidence-based, GRADE-framework approach to the management of biliary strictures in liver transplant recipients. Bile duct strictures are a common adverse outcome after liver transplantation, associated with significant morbidity ranging from asymptomatic liver enzyme elevation to cholangitis, allograft rejection, and allograft failure. The document addresses four clinical questions developed with a multidisciplinary expert panel (transplant surgery, transplant hepatology, gastroenterology) and a patient advocate.

The four questions cover: (1) ERCP versus percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTBD) as initial therapy; (2) covered self-expandable metal stents (cSEMSs) versus multiple plastic stents (MPSs) for therapy of strictures; (3) the role of MRCP for diagnosing post-transplant biliary strictures; and (4) antibiotics versus no antibiotics during elective ERCP. The panel notably excluded patients with cholangitis from the antibiotic question (clear existing guidelines mandate antibiotics) and, for the cSEMS question, restricted analysis to anastomotic (duct-to-duct) extrahepatic strictures in patients eligible for either treatment irrespective of foregut anatomy.

Overall, in patients with post-transplant biliary strictures the ASGE suggests **ERCP as the initial intervention** and **cSEMSs as the preferred stent for extrahepatic (anastomotic) strictures**. In patients with unclear diagnoses or intermediate probability of a stricture, the ASGE suggests **MRCP as the diagnostic modality**. Antibiotics should be administered during ERCP when complete biliary drainage cannot be ensured. All four recommendations are **conditional** ("we suggest"), reflecting limited (very low to high) quality of evidence.

The guideline focuses on patients with unaltered (duct-to-duct) foregut anatomy; patients with Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy (e.g., PSC recipients) may not be candidates for conventional duodenoscope ERCP, and the role of EUS-guided biliary access and percutaneous approaches in transplant recipients remains to be defined.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Question 1 — ERCP vs PTBD as initial therapy

**Recommendation 1 (verbatim):** "In liver transplant recipients with a biliary stricture, the ASGE suggests ERCP over PTBD as initial therapy for management of strictures." — *Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.*

**Best practice advice (Table 1):**
- ERCP preferred if it is difficult for caregivers to manage percutaneous drains/catheters, risk of dislodgement of percutaneous drains/catheters, presence of multiple intrahepatic strictures in different hepatic lobes, and lack of significant biliary dilation to facilitate percutaneous drainage.
- PTBD preferred if difficult endoscopic biliary access because of various reasons including altered anatomy or increased risk of adverse events from anesthesia.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Systematic review of 4 full-text studies (all retrospective except 1 multicenter nationwide study) directly comparing ERCP and PTBD as initial therapy; 432 procedures in aggregate (275 ERCP, 157 PTBD). Patients with altered foregut anatomy (Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy) and salvage procedures were excluded.
- Allograft rejection: adjusted odds of allograft failure were **8.47 greater for PTBD** vs ERCP (95% CI 1.47–48.6; P = .017).
- PTBD associated with longer hospitalization (OR 14.4; 95% CI 3.7–25.1; P = .008), higher disposition to nursing home (OR 2.72; 95% CI 1.08–6.87; P = .03), and higher overall cost.
- One study: fewer procedures with ERCP than PTBD (2.5 ± 0.9 vs 6.1 ± 0.4; P < .01). PTBD patients had an average of 3.0 ± 0.4 additional procedures for external catheter problems (leakage 14.9%, catheter retraction 29.8%, decreased drainage 43.2%, cholangitis 6.6%, other 5.5%).
- No difference in technical success (RR 1.28; 95% CI 0.27–2.33; P = .42; I² = 53.4%), composite AEs (RR 1.12; 95% CI 0.62–2.0; P = .7; I² = 0%), adjusted inpatient mortality (OR 1.47; P = .74), nonelective 30-day readmission (OR 0.98; P = .97), or total duration of therapy (5.3 ± 0.8 months ERCP vs 6.5 ± 0.7 months PTBD; P = .31).
- Cost of hospitalization: $179,179.3 ± $123,386.6 (ERCP) vs $257,058.7 ± $201,423.3 (PTBD), P < .01.

### Question 2 — cSEMS vs MPS for extrahepatic (anastomotic) strictures

**Recommendation 2 (verbatim):** "In liver transplant recipients with biliary strictures, the ASGE suggests cSEMSs should be used instead of MPSs for initial therapy of extrahepatic biliary strictures." — *Conditional recommendation, low to moderate quality of evidence.*

**Best practice advice (Table 1):**
- Covered metal stents are most often used for extrahepatic biliary strictures, typically at the anastomosis.
- Cholangiographic findings, such as intrahepatic biliary strictures or anastomotic strictures just below the bifurcation, may preclude the use of covered metal stents.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Systematic review using existing published analyses (Visconti et al). 4 multicenter RCTs and several meta-analyses; only studies in which patients could undergo either treatment irrespective of foregut anatomy were included. 205 patients across the 4 RCTs (103 cSEMS, 102 MPS); strictures were anastomotic (duct-to-duct) and did not involve intrahepatic ducts. Follow-up ≥1 year.
- No difference in stricture resolution between cSEMS and MPS (pooled risk difference 0.01; 95% CI −0.08 to 0.10; I² = 12%).
- No difference in stricture recurrence (pooled risk difference 0.13; 95% CI −0.03 to 0.28; I² = 52%), assessed in 181 patients.
- cSEMSs associated with **fewer procedures** (mean difference −1.86; 95% CI −3.12 to −0.06; I² = 97%) and fewer days to achieve resolution (mean difference −105.07; 95% CI −202.38 to −7.76; I² = 95%).
- cSEMSs required fewer stents per patient (mean difference −10.63; 95% CI −20.82 to −0.44).
- Plastic stents were exchanged through the point of resolution, whereas metal stents were removed after a defined period of 3 to 6 months regardless of cholangiogram interpretation.
- Most common AE was stent migration, with no significant difference between SEMSs and MPSs (meta-analysis of 84 patients). Stent migration considered a procedural finding rather than an AE if the stent was recovered on schedule and the stricture resolved.
- Cost: mean treatment cost lower with cSEMS than MPS ($8288 vs $19,580; P < .01).
- Salient points: stricture location was not delineated in the 4 RCTs; cholangiographic findings such as intrahepatic strictures or anastomotic strictures just below the bifurcation may obviate the use of cSEMSs; no clear strategy for positioning across or upstream of the biliary orifice was evaluated. Indirect evidence that cSEMSs can be associated with post-ERCP pancreatitis (not reported in any RCT).

### Question 3 — MRCP as preferred diagnostic modality

**Recommendation 3 (verbatim):** "In liver transplant recipients with suspected biliary stricture, the ASGE suggests use of MRCP as a diagnostic test." — *Conditional recommendation, moderate to high quality of evidence.*

**Best practice advice (Table 1):**
- MRCP is an acceptable diagnostic test for detecting post-transplant strictures.
- Individual clinical scenarios should dictate the utility of MRCP.
- In patients with a high pretest probability for a biliary stricture or cholangitis, proceeding directly to ERCP without a diagnostic MRCP is prudent.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Systematic review of 21 studies (13 retrospective, 8 prospective); meta-analyses compared MRCP to ERCP (criterion standard).
- Sensitivity/specificity meta-analysis: 20 studies, 758 patients. Pooled **sensitivity 94.9%** (95% CI 92.4–96.6; I² = 32.8%); pooled **specificity 90.3%** (95% CI 84.7–94.0; I² = 87.8%).
- Positive predictive value: 16 studies, 584 patients; PPV 90.6% (95% CI 85.6–93.9; I² = 64.4%).
- Negative predictive value: 14 studies, 505 patients; NPV 93.7% (95% CI 86.2–97.2; I² = 81.2%).
- Accuracy: 13 studies, 508 patients; pooled accuracy 92.4% (95% CI 89.0–94.6; I² = 24.1%).
- In a population of 1000 patients with 50% pretest probability: MRCP correctly identifies 452 as no stricture and 475 as having stricture; 48 false positives, 25 false negatives.
- Overall risk of MRCP deemed low, especially vs ERCP. Limitations: incompatible metallic implants, claustrophobia.

### Question 4 — Antibiotics during elective ERCP

**Recommendation 4 (verbatim):** "In patients with post–liver transplant biliary strictures undergoing elective ERCP in whom complete biliary drainage is technically challenging to achieve (ischemic cholangiopathy, multiple strictures, failure of stenting), the ASGE suggests administration of periprocedural antibiotics over no antibiotics to reduce incidence of infectious AEs." — *Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.*

**Best practice advice (Table 1):**
- An individualized approach for administering antibiotics based on each patient's unique biliary anatomy and clinical condition is prudent.
- Patients with inadequate drainage of the biliary tree because of strictures may benefit from preprocedural antibiotics.
- Otherwise, use of antibiotics should be discussed with the patient and transplant team before ERCP.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Patients with cholangitis excluded (clear guidelines already recommend antibiotics).
- Only 2 retrospective single-center US studies met selection criteria; 361 liver transplant recipients undergoing 959 ERCP procedures. Pooled incidence of infections 1.1% (95% CI 0.6–2.0; I² = 0). Both studies found that antibiotic administration did NOT lower the risk of infections or adverse outcomes.
- Bacteremia during ERCP is typically transient and not definitely associated with AEs. Unrestricted antibiotic use may increase multidrug-resistant organisms and antibiotic resistance (especially fluoroquinolones, already a challenge in LT patients).
- Antibiotic AEs noted: QTc prolongation, torsades de pointes, fluoroquinolone tendinitis/tendon rupture (black box warning); fluoroquinolones are weak CYP3A4 inhibitors and can increase serum tacrolimus concentration.
- Patients who may benefit from periprocedural antibiotics: stenosed intra- or extrahepatic ducts, ischemic cholangiopathy, multiple intrahepatic strictures, or those in whom contrast was injected but stricture dilation or stent placement across a stricture was unsuccessful.

### What Is New
ERCP as initial therapy for biliary strictures in post–LT patients; covered metal stents as preferred implant for strictures not involving intrahepatic ducts; preprocedural MRCP to confirm and delineate stricture location in patients with uncertain diagnosis; selection of patients for periprocedural antibiotic therapy.

### Future Directions
1. Natural history and risk factors for biliary stricture formation (ischemia from vascular insult postulated for ischemic cholangiopathy and nonanastomotic strictures; other factors — medications, donor age, allograft mismatch, pretransplant MELD — remain under investigation).
2. Optimal therapeutic approach in patients with altered foregut anatomy (Roux-en-Y, as in PSC) — role of percutaneous and EUS-guided biliary access needs evaluation.
3. Role of antibiotics during ERCP — prospective trials needed to define need, class, dose, and duration.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- Largely concordant with the AASLD-AST 2025 graft-complications guideline cited on [[liver-transplantation]]. One nuance: AASLD-AST states cSEMS and MPS have equivalent resolution but cSEMS needs fewer procedures (mean difference 1.69 ERCPs), shorter treatment (40 vs 80 days), 25% lower cost; the ASGE 2023 meta-analysis numbers differ slightly (mean −1.86 procedures; cost $8288 vs $19,580) but the direction is identical.
- Evidence base is weak for Q1 (very low) and Q4 (very low); both are conditional. Q3 (MRCP) is the strongest (moderate–high).
- All cSEMS data are for anastomotic duct-to-duct strictures; cSEMS may be inappropriate for intrahepatic or near-bifurcation strictures and for non-anastomotic ischemic cholangiopathy.
