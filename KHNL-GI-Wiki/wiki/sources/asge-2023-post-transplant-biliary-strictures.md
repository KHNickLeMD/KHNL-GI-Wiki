## Summary

ASGE Standards of Practice Committee guideline on managing [[biliary-stricture|biliary strictures]] in [[liver-transplantation|liver transplant]] recipients. Bile duct strictures are a common adverse outcome of liver transplantation; the associated cholangiopathy causes outcomes ranging from asymptomatic liver-enzyme elevation to hospitalization, cholangitis, allograft rejection, and allograft failure. Diagnosis rests on a varying combination of elevated liver enzymes, conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, and biliary dilation on imaging. Therapy often requires serial interventions — stricture dilation and stent placement — chosen by location and type of stricture, type of allograft, technical expertise available, and time from transplant.

Four clinical questions were framed in GRADE format and taken to a panel including transplant surgery, transplant hepatology, and gastroenterology, plus a patient advocate: (1) [[ercp|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]] vs percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTBD) as initial therapy; (2) covered self-expandable metal stents (cSEMSs) vs multiple plastic stents (MPSs) as initial therapy; (3) [[mri-mrcp|magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)]] as the preferred diagnostic modality; (4) antibiotics vs no antibiotics during elective ERCP. Deliberation weighed certainty of evidence, benefits and harms, feasibility, patient values and preferences, resource use, cost-effectiveness, and health equity.

Net result: ERCP as the initial intervention; cSEMSs as the preferred stent for extrahepatic strictures; MRCP as the diagnostic test where the diagnosis is unclear or the probability of stricture intermediate; antibiotics during ERCP when complete biliary drainage cannot be ensured. All four are conditional recommendations, on evidence rated very low (Questions 1 and 4) to moderate-high (Question 3).

**Scope limits that shape the recommendations:**
- Question 1 excluded salvage ERCP/PTBD and patients with altered foregut anatomy (Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy).
- Question 2 included only studies where patients could undergo either treatment irrespective of foregut anatomy; the strictures analysed were anastomotic (duct-to-duct) and did not involve the intrahepatic ducts.
- Question 4 excluded patients with cholangitis — clear existing guidelines already recommend antibiotics for them.
- The guideline addresses unaltered (duct-to-duct) anatomy. Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy recipients — as in [[primary-sclerosing-cholangitis|primary sclerosing cholangitis]] — may not be candidates for ERCP with a conventional side-viewing duodenoscope; the role of percutaneous and [[eus-guided-biliary-drainage|endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided biliary access]] in transplant recipients still needs evaluation.

### Question 1 — ERCP vs PTBD as initial therapy

*Question as posed: in liver transplant recipients with a biliary stricture, should ERCP be used compared with PTBD as the initial therapy of choice?* Recommendation 1 and its best practice advice: Table 1.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Systematic review of studies comparing ERCP and PTBD, with or without cholangioplasty, as the initial intervention; 4 full-text studies met criteria. All 4 were retrospective; only 1 was a multicenter nationwide study. 432 procedures in aggregate (275 ERCP, 157 PTBD). Patients undergoing ERCP or PTBD as salvage procedures were excluded, as were patients with altered foregut anatomy (Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy).
- Outcomes of interest: technical success, readmission after the procedure, allograft failure, infection, composite adverse events.
- **Allograft failure** — single multicenter retrospective study (Nationwide Readmissions Database) assessing allograft rejection in hospitalized recipients: on multivariate analysis the adjusted odds of liver allograft failure were **8.47 greater for PTBD** than ERCP (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.47-48.6; P = .017).
- PTBD also associated with longer hospitalization (odds ratio [OR] 14.4; 95% CI 3.7-25.1; P = .008), higher disposition to a nursing home (OR 2.72; 95% CI 1.08-6.87; P = .03), and higher overall cost.
- One study: fewer procedures with ERCP than PTBD (2.5 ± .9 vs 6.1 ± .4; P < .01). PTBD patients averaged 3.0 ± .4 **additional** procedures for external catheter problems — leakage 14.9%, catheter retraction 29.8%, decreased drainage 43.2%, cholangitis 6.6%, other 5.5%.
- **No difference** in technical success (relative risk [RR] 1.28; 95% CI .27-2.33; P = .42; I² = 53.4%), composite adverse events (AEs) (RR 1.12; 95% CI .62-2.0; P = .7; I² = 0%), adjusted inpatient mortality (OR 1.47; 95% CI .15-14.6; P = .74), nonelective 30-day readmission (OR .98; 95% CI .45-2.07; P = .97), or total duration of therapy for successful treatment (5.3 ± .8 months ERCP vs 6.5 ± .7 months PTBD; P = .31).
- Cost: no study directly compared cost or cost-effectiveness of ERCP vs PTBD. One study restricted to hospitalized patients reported cost of hospitalization $179,179.3 ± $123,386.6 (inpatient ERCP) vs $257,058.7 ± $201,423.3 (PTBD), P < .01; the panel noted cost of both procedures may be comparable once extrinsic factors are accounted for.
- No studies on patient preferences. The patient advocate favored ERCP — avoids external drains and the attendant discomfort, cosmetic disfigurement, dislodgement risk, and need for specialized care.
- Panel caveats: selection bias is inherent in the retrospective studies analysed; further studies could change the strength from conditional to strong.

### Question 2 — cSEMS vs MPS for extrahepatic (anastomotic) strictures

*Question as posed: in patients with post-transplant biliary strictures, should cSEMSs be used compared with MPSs as the initial therapy of choice?* Recommendation 2 and its best practice advice: Table 1.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Systematic review restricted to cSEMS vs MPS as **first-line** therapy; only studies in which patients could undergo either treatment irrespective of foregut anatomy were included. 4 multicenter randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and several meta-analyses identified; a decision was made to use the existing published analyses from Visconti et al.
- 205 patients across the 4 RCTs — 103 cSEMS, 102 MPS. Follow-up at least 1 year in each study (Kaffes et al: *mean* follow-up at least 1 year). Although not always specifically noted, strictures were anastomotic (duct-to-duct) and did not involve the intrahepatic ducts.
- **No difference in stricture resolution** (pooled risk difference .01; 95% CI −.08 to .10; I² = 12%).
- **No difference in stricture recurrence** (pooled risk difference .13; 95% CI −.03 to .28; I² = 52%), assessed in 181 patients.
- cSEMSs: **fewer procedures** — about 2 fewer (mean difference −1.86; 95% CI −3.12 to −.06; I² = 97%) — and **fewer days to resolution** (mean difference −105.07; 95% CI −202.38 to −7.76; I² = 95%).
- 2 RCTs (112 patients) evaluated stent number: **fewer stents per patient with cSEMS** (mean difference −10.63; 95% CI −20.82 to −.44).
- **Dwell/exchange strategy differed by stent type:** plastic stents were exchanged through the point of resolution; metal stents were removed after a defined period of **3 to 6 months**, regardless of cholangiogram interpretation.
- Most common AE was stent migration, with no significant difference between self-expandable metal stents (SEMSs) and MPSs (meta-analysis of 84 patients). Migration was counted as a procedural finding rather than an AE if the stent was recovered on schedule and the stricture resolved.
- Indirect evidence links cSEMSs to post-ERCP pancreatitis; this was not reported in any of the RCTs.
- Cost: no cost-effectiveness study identified. 2 RCTs compared total cost of the treatment strategy including device expense — mean treatment cost lower with cSEMS ($8288 vs $19,580; P < .01).
- **Stent choice is multifactorial:** duration since liver transplant, stricture distance from both the bifurcation and the ampulla, length of common bile duct, stent availability, risk of pancreatitis with metal stents, and local expertise. The panel favored SEMS **assuming the available SEMS is long enough to bridge the stricture**.
- Salient panel points: stricture location was not delineated in the 4 RCTs; cholangiographic findings during the procedure — intrahepatic strictures, or anastomotic strictures just below the bifurcation — may obviate cSEMS use; no clear strategy was evaluated to guide positioning across or upstream of the biliary orifice. Data on patient values and mortality were limited; the panel believed patients would favor cSEMS for the fewer procedures and lower cost.

### Question 3 — MRCP as preferred diagnostic modality

*Question as posed: in liver transplant recipients with suspected biliary stricture, should MRCP be considered the preferred diagnostic modality?* Recommendation 3 and its best practice advice: Table 1.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Systematic review of 21 studies (13 retrospective, 8 prospective) of MRCP for predicting biliary strictures after liver transplant, with ERCP as the criterion standard.
- Sensitivity/specificity: 20 studies, 758 patients. Pooled **sensitivity 94.9%** (95% CI 92.4-96.6; I² = 32.8%); pooled **specificity 90.3%** (95% CI 84.7-94.0; I² = 87.8%).
- Positive predictive value: 16 studies, 584 patients — of those with strictures on ERCP, MRCP correctly diagnosed 90.6% (95% CI 85.6-93.9; I² = 64.4%).
- Negative predictive value: 14 studies, 505 patients — of those without strictures on ERCP, MRCP was consistent at 93.7% (95% CI 86.2-97.2; I² = 81.2%).
- Accuracy: 13 studies, 508 patients; pooled accuracy 92.4% (95% CI 89.0-94.6; I² = 24.1%).
- **Translated to 1000 patients at 50% pretest probability:** MRCP correctly identifies 452 as having no stricture and 475 as having stricture; 48 false positives, 25 false negatives.
- No study assessed the risk of MRCP, but the panel deemed overall risk low, especially compared with ERCP and its significant AE risk. No study assessed cost or cost-effectiveness in this population, and there were no patient-values data; the patient advocate expressed minimal concern about undergoing MRCP.
- Limitations: magnetic resonance imaging-incompatible metallic implants, claustrophobia.

### Question 4 — Antibiotics during elective ERCP

*Question as posed: in patients with post–liver transplant biliary strictures **without cholangitis** undergoing elective ERCP, should antibiotics be administered or not administered to reduce risk of infections?* Recommendation 4 and its best practice advice: Table 1.

**Supporting evidence:**
- Patients with cholangitis were not assessed — clear guidelines already recommend antibiotics for them.
- Only 2 retrospective single-center United States studies met selection criteria. One assessed infection risk and the impact of antibiotics over time, with liver transplant recipients undergoing ERCP captured only in a subgroup analysis. The second directly compared antibiotic administration vs nonadministration in nonhospitalized recipients, assessing "clinically significant infections" and not asymptomatic bacteremia.
- 361 liver transplant recipients undergoing 959 ERCP procedures. Pooled incidence of infections **1.1%** (95% CI .6-2.0; I² = 0). **Both studies found antibiotic administration did not lower the risk of infections or adverse outcomes.**
- Infection risk may simply be very low, making it hard to detect in small, inadequately powered studies — so the ability of antibiotics to lower it further may be limited. Bacteremia occurs often during endoscopy including ERCP, but is transient and not definitely associated with AEs.
- No study compared noninfectious AEs with vs without antibiotics; none assessed cost-effectiveness of periprocedural antibiotics.
- Harms of unrestricted use: multidrug-resistant organisms and antibiotic resistance, already a challenge in liver transplant patients, especially with fluoroquinolones. Antibiotic AEs include corrected QT interval (QTc) prolongation and torsades de pointes, which can be life-threatening; fluoroquinolones — the most common antibiotics in this setting — carry a black box warning for tendinitis and tendon rupture, and as weak CYP3A4 inhibitors can increase serum [[tacrolimus]] concentration.
- Existing recommendations for antibiotics in **all** liver transplant patients rest on conflicting data, largely extrapolated from a theoretical infection-risk increase that no study has substantiated. In the absence of high-quality data a strong recommendation could not be made.
- **Who may benefit from preprocedural antibiotics** — inadequate biliary drainage because of strictures or failed therapeutic intervention: stenosed intrahepatic or extrahepatic ducts, ischemic cholangiopathy, multiple intrahepatic strictures, or contrast injected where stricture dilation or stent placement across the stricture was unsuccessful (e.g., failure of stenting).
- Otherwise antibiotic use should be discussed among patient, endoscopist, and transplant team as shared medical decision-making. The guideline does not specify an antibiotic class, dose, or duration.

### What Is New
Existing data highlighted to support: ERCP as initial therapy for biliary strictures after liver transplant; covered metal stents as the preferred implant for strictures **not involving intrahepatic ducts**; preprocedural MRCP to confirm and delineate stricture location when the diagnosis is uncertain; selection of patients for periprocedural antibiotic therapy.

### Future Directions
1. **Natural history and risk factors** for stricture formation — ischemia from vascular insult is postulated for ischemic cholangiopathy and nonanastomotic strictures; medications, donor age, allograft mismatch, and pretransplant Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) score remain under investigation. Protective factors are unclear. Classifying biliary strictures and tailoring therapy by etiology and anatomy remain to be defined.
2. **Optimal therapeutic approach in altered foregut anatomy** (Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy) — endoscopic approaches and the role of PTBD need thorough evaluation, as does EUS-guided biliary access in transplant recipients.
3. **Role of antibiotics during ERCP** — prospective clinical trials needed to define the need, pharmacologic class, optimum dosing, and duration.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- Largely concordant with the AASLD-AST 2025 graft-complications guideline cited on [[liver-transplantation]]. One nuance: AASLD-AST states cSEMS and MPS have equivalent resolution but cSEMS needs fewer procedures (mean difference 1.69 ERCPs), shorter treatment (40 vs 80 days), 25% lower cost; the ASGE 2023 meta-analysis numbers differ slightly (mean −1.86 procedures; cost $8288 vs $19,580) but the direction is identical.
- Evidence is very low quality for Questions 1 and 4; Question 3 (MRCP) is strongest at moderate to high. No recommendation here is strong.
- All cSEMS data are for anastomotic duct-to-duct strictures. Stricture location was not delineated in the 4 RCTs, so the evidence cannot settle cSEMS use for intrahepatic or near-bifurcation strictures, or for non-anastomotic ischemic cholangiopathy.
- Optimal cSEMS positioning — across vs upstream of the biliary orifice — was not evaluated by any included study.
- Antibiotics: the two available studies show no benefit, yet the recommendation favors antibiotics in the subgroup where drainage is incomplete. The panel's stance rests on biologic rationale and panel judgment, not on a demonstrated benefit, and no agent, dose, or duration is specified.
