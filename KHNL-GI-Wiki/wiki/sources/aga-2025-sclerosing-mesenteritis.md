## Summary

Expert commentary on sclerosing mesenteritis (SM) — an uncommon idiopathic disorder of the bowel mesentery with inflammation, fat necrosis and fibrosis; the older names (mesenteric panniculitis, "misty mesentery," retractile/liposclerotic mesenteritis and others) are one continuum. Found on 0.6%–1.1% of computed tomography (CT) studies; most cases are asymptomatic and need no treatment.

Diagnosis is imaging-led: ≥3 of 5 Coulier CT criteria is suggestive, and with characteristic imaging and no alarm features a presumptive diagnosis can be made without biopsy. Biopsy is reserved for features concerning for malignancy. Follow-up: annual CT for 2 years in asymptomatic cases without malignant features. Symptomatic disease is treated with prednisone plus tamoxifen or colchicine; azathioprine and other immunosuppressants are second line; surgery only for persistent or recurrent mechanical obstruction. Figure 2 gives the evaluation and treatment algorithm.

## Key Findings / Claims (narrative)

### Epidemiology and clinical features (p.902)
- Retrospective CT studies: SM findings in **0.6%–1.1%**. Large case series: mean age **55 y**, more often male and White.
- Up to **60%** with SM on CT asymptomatic (likely underestimate).
- Symptomatic: abdominal pain most common (up to **75%**); bloating **10%–25%**; diarrhea or constipation **10%–25%**; nausea and vomiting **5%–15%**; weight loss **20%**. Palpable mass often not found.
- No higher prevalence of autoimmunity; possibly more metabolic syndrome, obesity, coronary artery disease, urolithiasis.

### Imaging — Coulier CT criteria (p.903, *Imaging*)
- (1) Hazy mesenteric fat mass displacing intestine; (2) higher attenuation in the mesenteric fat mass than adjacent mesentery, but no enhancement with intravenous contrast; (3) lymph nodes **<1 cm**; (4) hypoattenuated halo sign around central vessels; (5) thin surrounding fibrotic pseudocapsule. Examples in Figure 1.
- **≥3 features → suggestive of SM.**
- Hazy mesentery **without** nodes, halo sign or pseudocapsule → **not diagnostic**; without other symptoms, typically **no follow-up**.
- Definitive diagnosis requires biopsy, but with characteristic imaging and **no alarm features (fever, night sweats, unintentional weight loss)** malignancy risk is very low → **presumptive diagnosis without biopsy** in most cases.
- **Calcifications + retraction of surrounding bowel** (more severe disease, possible malignancy; Figure 1B, C) → typically warrants biopsy unless an IgG4 association can be established.

### Laboratory findings and IgG4 (p.903)
- Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) may be elevated, not in all cases; role in management/follow-up uncertain.
- IgG4-related SM is rare, often atypical: on CT can look malignant (indistinct margins, apparent invasion of adjacent organs, multiple enlarged nodes). Only **half** have elevated serum IgG4.
- **Surgical biopsy with IgG4 staining** may be required if IgG4 level is **normal** and imaging is **not diagnostic** for SM.

### Severe disease — features of a more severe course (p.903)
- (1) Omental, retroperitoneal, pelvic fat or colonic mesentery involvement; (2) bowel obstruction/ischemia, venous thrombosis/thromboembolism, sepsis, protein-losing enteropathy or renal failure; (3) **marked** CRP or ESR elevation; (4) coexisting autoimmune disease; (5) arterial narrowing; (6) ascites.

### Differential diagnosis (pp.903–904)
- Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (mesenteric infiltration/mass hard to separate from SM; mesenteric node enlargement ± non-mesenteric nodes or splenomegaly); peritoneal carcinomatosis (ascites, multiple enhancing nodules; desmoplastic reaction can mimic); carcinoid (often focal bowel and liver lesions; mesentery-only cases occur); mesenteric fibromatosis/desmoid (familial adenomatous polyposis; **MRI preferred**); encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis (mostly peritoneal dialysis; "cocoon"; ascites pockets 90%, peritoneal calcification 70%); retroperitoneal fibrosis (distal aorta L4–L5 to iliacs, encases ureters; back/flank pain; can coexist); pseudomyxoma peritonei; others (gastrointestinal stromal tumor, sarcoma, mesenteric edema, infections).

### Relationship to cancer (p.904)
- Current evidence does not support SM as paraneoplastic; overlap with lymphoma, gynecologic malignancy, carcinoid.
- Mesenteric mass or intra-abdominal node **>10 mm** → suspect underlying malignancy.
- Prospective study, >400 SM patients: subsequent malignancy **1%**, all B-cell lymphomas, all with a soft-tissue nodule >10 mm or abdominopelvic lymphadenopathy.
- SM reported after immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy; generally steroid-responsive.

### Management — general (p.904)
- Based on presentation; treatment generally for **symptomatic** patients (Figure 2).
- **Fever → evaluate for infection** before attributing to SM.

### Biopsy (p.904)
- Classic imaging + no cancer concern → biopsy unnecessary before treatment.
- Consider biopsy with features concerning for malignancy: (1) associated lymph nodes **>1 cm**; (2) nodes with **SUVmax ≥3.0** on ¹⁸FDG PET/CT; (3) lymphadenopathy in another intra-abdominal location; (4) spiculated, necrotic, calcified or invasive appearance, incl. **absent central halo sign**.
- Malignancy-concerning features → ¹⁸FDG-PET/CT to characterize and find accessible nodes for biopsy.
- IgG4-related SM: biopsy generally required (also to exclude malignancy).

### Follow-up imaging (p.904)
- Published advice ranges from none to annual imaging up to 5 years. Meta-analysis of 4 case-control studies: no detectable increased cancer risk; another study: small long-term increased lymphoma risk in those without initial malignant features, **80% detectable in the first year**.
- Except the most severe cases, most patients (treated or not) reach stable disease without radiologic progression after **2 years**.
- During follow-up, **significant growth of the mass or new lymphadenopathy → biopsy**.
- **"We suggest"** annual CT for **2 years** in asymptomatic cases without initial malignancy-concerning features; thereafter imaging for concerning symptoms.

### Medical therapy (pp.904–906)
- Aim = symptom relief; limited evidence treatment improves imaging or prevents complications. Asymptomatic → usually not treated.
- Only **1%–6%** of patients with SM imaging findings required treatment in several studies; may be higher in referral centers — ~**50%** treated initially in 2 large observational studies, more over time (p.905).
- No controlled trials; choice based on open-label studies/case reports. General measures: treat concomitant GI disorders (constipation, GERD, IBS); one coauthor routinely uses tricyclic antidepressants for mild pain (p.906).
- **Prednisone monotherapy** 30–40 mg/day may improve short-term symptoms; **long-term not advised** (side effects). Large retrospective series: started at **40 mg/day**, continued **3–4 months**, tapered **5 mg/week**. Benefit typically lost after stopping (p.906).
- **Tamoxifen** — most commonly used: **10 mg twice daily**, alone or with prednisone **30–40 mg daily**; with combination, prednisone usually tapered after **3 months**, tamoxifen continued for maintenance (duration undetermined). Maximal response to prednisone and tamoxifen ~**4–6 months**, **50%–60%** respond. Side effects: antiestrogenic (hot flashes, sweating, vaginal bleeding), alopecia, fatigue, thrombosis, thrombocytopenia, elevated liver enzymes. Responders with mild side effects: **10 mg daily** may maintain response. **Contraindications:** history of venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, or significant cerebrovascular, cardiovascular or peripheral vascular disease; **active tobacco use = relative contraindication** (p.906).
- **Colchicine** + prednisone: ~**50%** response (small series). **0.6 mg twice daily** with prednisone **40 mg daily**, prednisone tapered after **3 months**. GI side effects may limit; **contraindicated in renal insufficiency** (p.906).
- **Azathioprine** **2–2.5 mg/kg/day** (inflammatory bowel disease–type dosing) in normal thiopurine methyltransferase metabolizers; slow onset → coadminister prednisone early (p.906).
- **Other immunosuppressants** with limited data: rituximab, thalidomide, cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, infliximab, ustekinumab — significant side-effect potential; typically second line for **severe, refractory** disease (p.906).

### Severe disease complications (p.906)
- Bowel obstruction: nonoperative when feasible; surgical bypass if unsuccessful. Complete resection usually impossible (vascular compromise, extent).
- Chronic mesenteric ischemia from mesenteric vascular obstruction: considered for revascularization, but few are endovascular candidates (long/distal vessel involvement); surgical revascularization rarely feasible. Radiation therapy: equivocal.
- Surgery does not cure SM: in 1 study only **10%** needed no further treatment. Recurrence detected by imaging or new symptoms.
- Persistent/recurrent symptoms after surgery → **"we suggest"** medical management as for nonobstructive symptoms.

### Conclusions (pp.906–907)
- Biopsy not required if ≥3 of 5 Coulier criteria and no features of more aggressive disease or malignancy. No surgical cure. Checkpoint inhibitor–associated SM responds readily to steroids. **Most severe cases → consider referral to tertiary centers** with expertise.

### Figure 2 — evaluation and treatment algorithm (p.905)
- CT with "misty mesentery" → review CT with radiologist and history with patient → **evidence of secondary cause** (extra-mesenteric lymphadenopathy; splenomegaly; ascites or anasarca; portal hypertension; pancreatitis, appendicitis, diverticulitis, cholecystitis; extra-mesenteric mass; vasculitis; mesenteric vein thrombosis; small intestinal wall thickening; trauma/hemorrhage; heart or renal failure, cirrhosis; infection; IgG4-associated disease; immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy) → further workup or treatment guided by suspected etiology.
- No secondary cause → **meets ≥3 of 5 criteria** (figure wording: mesenteric mass encasing or displacing adjacent structures, not infiltrating; hyper-attenuating fat; soft-tissue nodules (lymph nodes); "fat halo" sign; pseudocapsule)?
  - Does not meet: asymptomatic → observe; symptomatic → consider other diagnoses.
  - Meets → assess lymph nodes: **≥10 mm** → consider PET-CT, biopsy any suspicious lesions; **<10 mm** → asymptomatic (or mild symptoms): observation, consider CT/MRI yearly ×2 years; symptomatic → treatment.
- Treatment strategies: prednisone 30–40 mg (tapered after 3 months) + tamoxifen 10 mg bid; prednisone 30–40 mg (tapered after 3 months) + colchicine 0.6 mg bid; 2nd line: other agents incl. azathioprine with prednisone 30 mg (tapered after 3 months) and/or referral to specialty center; surgery only for persistent or recurrent mechanical obstruction; CT yearly ×2 years or if new symptoms.
