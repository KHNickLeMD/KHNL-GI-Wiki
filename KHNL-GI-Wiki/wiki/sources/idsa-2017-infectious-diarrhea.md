## Summary

These IDSA guidelines address the diagnosis and management of acute and persistent infectious diarrhea in infants, children, adolescents, and adults in the United States, and replace the 2001 IDSA practice guidelines on infectious diarrhea. They are broader in scope than the contemporaneous [[acg-2016-acute-diarrhea|ACG 2016 acute diarrhea guideline]] in that they cover pediatric patients, enteric (typhoid) fever, Shiga toxin–producing organisms in detail, immunocompromised hosts, vaccines, and public-health reporting. Recommendations explicitly defer to the separate IDSA/SHEA *Clostridium difficile* guidelines for CDI management.

The Panel uses GRADE throughout, grading each statement by strength (strong/weak) and evidence quality (high/moderate/low/very low). Diarrhea is defined (per WHO) as ≥3 loose or liquid stools per 24 hours or more frequently than normal; passage of formed stools and loose "pasty" stools in breastfed infants is not diarrhea. Four temporal categories are defined: **acute** (<7 days, watery or bloody/dysenteric), **prolonged** (7–13 days), **persistent** (14–29 days), and **chronic** (≥30 days). Note this differs from the ACG 2016 cutoffs (acute <14 d, persistent 14–30 d).

Core themes: obtain a detailed clinical and exposure history; reserve stool testing for patients with fever, bloody/mucoid stools, severe disease, sepsis, immunocompromise, or epidemiologic/outbreak indications; detect and characterize STEC (Shiga toxin ± O157 culture) because antibiotics are contraindicated for STEC O157 / Shiga toxin 2 producers (HUS risk); empiric antibiotics are generally NOT recommended for either bloody or watery diarrhea in immunocompetent hosts (with defined exceptions such as suspected shigellosis, ill febrile travelers, infants <3 months, and enteric-fever sepsis); rehydration with reduced-osmolarity ORS is the foundation of supportive care; and notifiable enteric pathogens must be reported to public health.

The guideline adds management points absent from the ACG acute-diarrhea guideline: enteric (typhoid/paratyphoid) fever diagnosis and empiric therapy, blood-culture indications, HUS hematologic/renal monitoring for STEC, age-specific rules (no loperamide <18 years; ondansetron and oral zinc in children), serologic/fecal-leukocyte/lactoferrin tests should NOT be used to establish etiology, and rotavirus/typhoid/cholera vaccine guidance.

## All Recommendations (Complete Verbatim Text)

> Each recommendation is graded *(strength, quality of evidence)* as published.

### Clinical, Demographic, and Epidemiologic Features

**1.** A detailed clinical and exposure history should be obtained from people with diarrhea, under any circumstances, including when there is a history of similar illness in others *(strong, moderate)*.

**2.** People with diarrhea who attend or work in child care centers, long-term care facilities, patient care, food service, or recreational water venues (eg, pools and lakes) should follow jurisdictional recommendations for outbreak reporting and infection control *(strong, high)*.

**3.** People with fever or bloody diarrhea should be evaluated for enteropathogens for which antimicrobial agents may confer clinical benefit, including *Salmonella enterica* subspecies, *Shigella*, and *Campylobacter* *(strong, low)*.

**4.** Enteric fever should be considered when a febrile person (with or without diarrhea) has a history of travel to areas in which causative agents are endemic, has consumed foods prepared by people with recent endemic exposure, or has laboratory exposure to *Salmonella* Typhi or *Salmonella* Paratyphi *(strong, moderate)*.

**5.** People of all ages with acute diarrhea should be evaluated for dehydration, which increases the risk of life-threatening illness and death, especially among the young and older adults *(strong, high)*.

**6.** When the clinical or epidemic history suggests a possible Shiga toxin–producing organism, diagnostic approaches should be applied that detect Shiga toxin (or the genes that encode them) and distinguish *Escherichia coli* O157:H7 from other Shiga toxin–producing *E. coli* (STEC) in stool *(strong, moderate)*. If available, diagnostic approaches that can distinguish between Shiga toxin 1 and Shiga toxin 2 (typically more potent) could be used *(weak, moderate)*. *Shigella dysenteriae* type 1, and rarely other pathogens, may produce Shiga toxin and should be considered as a cause of HUS, especially with suggestive international travel or contact with a traveler *(strong, moderate)*.

**7.** Clinicians should evaluate people for postinfectious and extraintestinal manifestations associated with enteric infections *(strong, moderate)*.

### Diagnostics

**8.** Stool testing should be performed for *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Campylobacter*, *Yersinia*, *C. difficile*, and STEC in people with diarrhea accompanied by fever, bloody or mucoid stools, severe abdominal cramping or tenderness, or signs of sepsis *(strong, moderate)*. Bloody stools are not an expected manifestation of *C. difficile*. STEC O157 should be assessed by culture and non-O157 STEC detected by Shiga toxin or genomic assays *(strong, low)*. Sorbitol-MacConkey agar or an appropriate chromogenic alternative is recommended to screen for O157:H7 STEC; Shiga toxin detection is needed for other STEC serotypes *(strong, moderate)*.

**9.** Blood cultures should be obtained from infants <3 months of age, people of any age with signs of septicemia or when enteric fever is suspected, people with systemic manifestations of infection, immunocompromised people, people with high-risk conditions such as hemolytic anemia, and people who traveled to or had contact with travelers from enteric-fever-endemic areas with a febrile illness of unknown etiology *(strong, moderate)*.

**10.** Stool testing should be performed under clearly identified circumstances (Table 2) for *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Campylobacter*, *Yersinia*, *C. difficile*, and STEC in symptomatic hosts *(strong, low)*. Specifically: (a) test for *Yersinia enterocolitica* in people with persistent abdominal pain (especially school-aged children with RLQ pain mimicking appendicitis who may have mesenteric adenitis) and in people with fever at epidemiologic risk (eg, infants exposed to raw/undercooked pork); (b) test for *Vibrio* species in people with large-volume rice-water stools or exposure to salty/brackish water, raw/undercooked shellfish, or travel to cholera-endemic regions within 3 days before onset.

**11.** A broader set of bacterial, viral, and parasitic agents should be considered regardless of fever, bloody/mucoid stools, or other markers of severity in the context of a possible outbreak; agent selection should be based on host and epidemiologic risk factors and ideally coordinated with public health authorities *(strong, moderate)*.

**12.** A broad differential diagnosis is recommended in immunocompromised people with diarrhea, especially with moderate-severe primary or secondary immune deficiencies, for evaluation by culture, viral studies, and parasite examination *(strong, moderate)*. People with AIDS and persistent diarrhea should undergo additional testing for organisms including *Cryptosporidium*, *Cyclospora*, *Cystoisospora*, microsporidia, *Mycobacterium avium* complex, and cytomegalovirus *(strong, moderate)*.

**13.** Diagnostic testing is not recommended in most cases of uncomplicated traveler's diarrhea unless treatment is indicated. Travelers with diarrhea ≥14 days should be evaluated for intestinal parasites *(strong, moderate)*. Test for *C. difficile* in travelers treated with antimicrobials within the preceding 8–12 weeks. IBD and postinfectious IBS should be considered *(strong, moderate)*.

**14.** Clinical consideration should be included in interpreting multiple-pathogen nucleic acid amplification test results, because these assays detect DNA and not necessarily viable organisms *(strong, low)*.

**15.** All specimens positive for bacterial pathogens by culture-independent diagnostic testing (eg, GI panels), for which isolate submission is requested/required under public health reporting rules, should be cultured in the clinical or public health laboratory to ensure outbreaks are detected and investigated *(strong, low)*. Culture may also be required where antimicrobial susceptibility results would affect care or public health responses *(strong, low)*.

**16.** Specimens from people involved in an enteric-disease outbreak should be tested for enteric pathogens per public health department guidance *(strong, low)*.

**17.** Culture-independent (including panel-based multiplex molecular) diagnostics from stool and blood, and, when indicated, culture-dependent testing should be performed when there is clinical suspicion of enteric fever (diarrhea uncommon) or diarrhea with bacteremia *(strong, moderate)*. Cultures of bone marrow (particularly valuable if antimicrobials already given), stool, duodenal fluid, and urine may help detect enteric fever *(weak, moderate)*. Serologic tests should NOT be used to diagnose enteric fever *(strong, moderate)*.

**18.** Testing may be considered for *C. difficile* in people >2 years of age with diarrhea following antimicrobial use and in people with healthcare-associated diarrhea *(weak, high)*. Testing may be considered in people with persistent diarrhea without an etiology and without recognized risk factors *(weak, low)*. A single diarrheal stool specimen is recommended for detection of toxin or a toxigenic strain (eg, NAAT) *(strong, low)*; multiple specimens do not increase yield.

**19.** The optimal specimen for laboratory diagnosis is a diarrheal stool sample (takes the shape of the container). For bacterial detection, if a timely diarrheal stool cannot be collected, a rectal swab may be used *(weak, low)*. For viral and protozoal agents and *C. difficile* toxin, fresh stool is preferred *(weak, low)*.

**20.** Fecal leukocyte examination and stool lactoferrin detection should NOT be used to establish the cause of acute infectious diarrhea *(strong, moderate)*. There are insufficient data to make a recommendation on fecal calprotectin in acute infectious diarrhea.

**21.** Serologic tests are not recommended to establish an etiology of infectious diarrhea or enteric fever *(strong, low)*, but may be considered for people with postdiarrheal HUS in which a stool culture did not yield a Shiga toxin–producing organism *(weak, low)*.

**22.** A peripheral WBC count and differential and serologic assays should not be performed to establish an etiology of diarrhea *(strong, low)*, but may be useful clinically *(weak, low)*.

**23.** Frequent monitoring of hemoglobin and platelet counts, electrolytes, BUN, and creatinine is recommended to detect hematologic and renal abnormalities that are early manifestations of HUS and precede renal injury in people with diagnosed *E. coli* O157 or another STEC infection (especially Shiga toxin 2 producers or bloody diarrhea) *(strong, high)*. Examining a peripheral blood smear for RBC fragments is necessary when HUS is suspected *(strong, high)*.

**24.** Endoscopy or proctoscopy should be considered in people with persistent, unexplained diarrhea who have AIDS, in people with certain underlying conditions, in people with acute diarrhea with clinical colitis or proctitis, and in people with persistent diarrhea who engage in anal intercourse *(strong, low)*. Duodenal aspirate may be considered in select people for suspected *Giardia*, *Strongyloides*, *Cystoisospora*, or microsporidia infection *(weak, low)*.

**25.** Imaging (ultrasonography, CT, or MRI) may be considered to detect aortitis, mycotic aneurysms, peritonitis, intra-abdominal free air, toxic megacolon, or extravascular foci of infection in older people with invasive *Salmonella enterica* or *Yersinia* infections if there is sustained fever/bacteremia despite adequate therapy, or with underlying atherosclerosis or recent-onset chest/back/abdominal pain *(weak, low)*.

### Follow-up Evaluation

**26.** Follow-up testing is not recommended in most people following resolution of diarrhea *(strong, moderate)*. Serial stool specimens (culture-dependent) for *Salmonella* Typhi/Paratyphi, STEC, *Shigella*, nontyphoidal *Salmonella*, and other bacteria are recommended in certain situations by local health authorities to enable return to child care, employment, or group activities *(strong, moderate)*. Practitioners should collaborate with local public health authorities *(strong, high)*.

**27.** Clinical and laboratory reevaluation may be indicated in people who do not respond to an initial course of therapy, and should include consideration of noninfectious conditions including lactose intolerance *(weak, low)*.

**28.** Noninfectious conditions, including IBD and IBS, should be considered as underlying etiologies in people with symptoms lasting ≥14 days and unidentified sources *(strong, moderate)*.

**29.** Reassessment of fluid and electrolyte balance, nutritional status, and optimal dose/duration of antimicrobial therapy is recommended in people with persistent symptoms *(strong, high)*.

### Empiric Management — Bloody Diarrhea

**30.** In immunocompetent children and adults, empiric antimicrobial therapy for bloody diarrhea while awaiting results is NOT recommended *(strong, low)*, except: (a) infants <3 months with suspicion of bacterial etiology; (b) ill immunocompetent people with documented fever, abdominal pain, bloody diarrhea, and bacillary dysentery presumptively due to *Shigella*; (c) people recently traveled internationally with temperature ≥38.5°C and/or signs of sepsis *(weak, low)*.

**31.** Empiric antimicrobial therapy in adults should be either a fluoroquinolone (eg, ciprofloxacin) or azithromycin, depending on local susceptibility patterns and travel history *(strong, moderate)*. Empiric therapy for children includes a third-generation cephalosporin for infants <3 months and others with neurologic involvement, or azithromycin, depending on local susceptibility and travel history *(strong, moderate)*.

**32.** Empiric antibacterial treatment should be considered in immunocompromised people with severe illness and bloody diarrhea *(strong, low)*.

**33.** Asymptomatic contacts of people with bloody diarrhea should NOT be offered empiric treatment, but should follow appropriate infection prevention and control measures *(strong, moderate)*.

**34.** People with clinical features of sepsis suspected of enteric fever should be treated empirically with broad-spectrum antimicrobials after blood, stool, and urine culture collection *(strong, low)*; therapy should be narrowed when susceptibilities return *(strong, high)*. If no isolate is available and enteric fever is suspected, antimicrobial choice may be tailored to susceptibility patterns of the acquisition setting *(weak, low)*.

**35.** Antimicrobial therapy for infections attributed to STEC O157 and other STEC producing Shiga toxin 2 (or unknown toxin genotype) should be AVOIDED *(strong, moderate)*. Therapy for other STEC not producing Shiga toxin 2 (generally non-O157) is debatable due to insufficient evidence of benefit or potential harm *(strong, low)*.

### Empiric Management — Watery Diarrhea

**36.** In most people with acute watery diarrhea without recent international travel, empiric antimicrobial therapy is NOT recommended *(strong, low)*. An exception may be made in immunocompromised people or ill-appearing young infants. Empiric treatment should be avoided in people with persistent watery diarrhea ≥14 days *(strong, low)*.

**37.** Asymptomatic contacts of people with acute or persistent watery diarrhea should NOT be offered empiric or preventive therapy, but should follow appropriate infection prevention and control measures *(strong, moderate)*.

### Directed Management

**38.** Antimicrobial treatment should be modified or discontinued when a clinically plausible organism is identified *(strong, high)*.

### Supportive Treatment — Rehydration

**39.** Reduced-osmolarity oral rehydration solution (ORS) is recommended as first-line therapy of mild to moderate dehydration in infants, children, and adults with acute diarrhea from any cause *(strong, moderate)*, and in people with mild-moderate dehydration associated with vomiting or severe diarrhea.

**40.** Nasogastric administration of ORS may be considered in infants, children, and adults with moderate dehydration who cannot tolerate oral intake, or in children with normal mental status who are too weak or refuse to drink adequately *(weak, low)*.

**41.** Isotonic intravenous fluids (eg, lactated Ringer's, normal saline) should be administered for severe dehydration, shock, altered mental status, and failure of ORS *(strong, high)* or ileus *(strong, moderate)*. In people with ketonemia, an initial course of IV hydration may be needed to enable tolerance of ORS *(weak, low)*.

**42.** In severe dehydration, IV rehydration should continue until pulse, perfusion, and mental status normalize and the patient awakens, has no aspiration risk, and has no ileus *(strong, low)*; the remaining deficit can be replaced with ORS *(weak, low)*. People with mild-moderate dehydration should receive ORS until clinical dehydration is corrected *(strong, low)*.

**43.** Once rehydrated, maintenance fluids should be administered; replace ongoing stool losses with ORS until diarrhea and vomiting resolve *(strong, low)*.

### Feeding

**44.** Human milk feeding should be continued in infants and children throughout the diarrheal episode *(strong, low)*.

**45.** Resumption of an age-appropriate usual diet is recommended during or immediately after the rehydration process *(strong, low)*.

### Ancillary Management

**46.** Ancillary treatment with antimotility, antinausea, or antiemetic agents can be considered once the patient is adequately hydrated, but is not a substitute for fluid and electrolyte therapy *(weak, low)*.

**47.** Antimotility drugs (eg, loperamide) should NOT be given to children <18 years of age with acute diarrhea *(strong, moderate)*. Loperamide may be given to immunocompetent adults with acute watery diarrhea *(weak, moderate)*, but should be avoided at any age in suspected or proven cases where toxic megacolon may result, in inflammatory diarrhea, or diarrhea with fever *(strong, low)*.

**48.** Antinausea and antiemetic agents (eg, ondansetron) may be given to facilitate tolerance of oral rehydration in children >4 years of age and in adolescents with acute gastroenteritis associated with vomiting *(weak, moderate)*.

### Probiotics and Zinc

**49.** Probiotic preparations may be offered to reduce symptom severity and duration in immunocompetent adults and children with infectious or antimicrobial-associated diarrhea *(weak, moderate)*.

**50.** Oral zinc supplementation reduces the duration of diarrhea in children 6 months to 5 years of age who reside in countries with a high prevalence of zinc deficiency or who have signs of malnutrition *(strong, moderate)*.

### Treatment of Asymptomatic Carriers

**51.** Asymptomatic people who practice hand hygiene and live/work in low-risk settings do not need treatment, except asymptomatic people with *Salmonella* Typhi in their stool who may be treated empirically to reduce transmission *(weak, low)*. Those in high-risk settings (healthcare, child/elderly care, food service) should be treated per local public health guidance *(strong, high)*.

### Prevention

**52.** Hand hygiene should be performed after using the toilet, changing diapers, before and after preparing food, before eating, after handling garbage or soiled laundry, and after touching animals or their feces/environments, especially in public settings such as petting zoos *(strong, moderate)*.

**53.** Infection control measures including gloves and gowns, hand hygiene with soap and water, or alcohol-based sanitizers should be followed in caring for people with diarrhea *(strong, high)*. Hand hygiene product selection should be based on the known/suspected pathogen and environment *(strong, low)*.

**54.** Appropriate food safety practices are recommended to avoid cross-contamination of foods, surfaces, and utensils during shopping, preparation, and storage; ensure foods containing meats and eggs are cooked and maintained at proper temperatures *(strong, moderate)*.

**55.** Healthcare providers should direct educational efforts toward all people with diarrhea, but particularly people with primary/secondary immune deficiencies, pregnant women, parents of young children, and the elderly *(strong, low)*.

**56.** Ill people with diarrhea should avoid swimming, water-related activities, and sexual contact with others while symptomatic, with meticulous hand hygiene *(strong, low)*.

**57.** Rotavirus vaccine should be administered to all infants without a known contraindication *(strong, high)*.

**58.** Typhoid vaccination (two vaccines—oral and injectable—licensed in the US but not recommended routinely) is recommended as an adjunct to hand hygiene and avoidance of high-risk foods/beverages for travelers to moderate-to-high-risk areas, people with intimate exposure to a documented *Salmonella* Typhi chronic carrier, and microbiologists/laboratory personnel routinely exposed to *Salmonella* Typhi cultures *(strong, high)*. Booster doses are recommended for people who remain at risk *(strong, high)*.

**59.** A live attenuated cholera vaccine (single-dose oral, available in the US) is recommended for adults 18–64 years of age who travel to cholera-affected areas *(strong, high)*.

**60.** All diseases listed in the National Notifiable Diseases Surveillance System, including those that cause diarrhea, should be reported to the appropriate state, territorial, or local health department, with submission of isolates of certain pathogens (eg, *Salmonella*, STEC, *Shigella*, *Listeria*) to ensure control and prevention practices may be implemented *(strong, high)*.

## Key Findings / Claims

- **Temporal definitions (differ from ACG 2016):** acute <7 days; prolonged 7–13 days; persistent 14–29 days; chronic ≥30 days. WHO diarrhea definition = ≥3 loose/liquid stools per 24 h (or more than normal); loose "pasty" stools in breastfed infants are not diarrhea.
- **History first:** A detailed clinical and exposure history (food, water, travel, animal, sexual, antimicrobial, occupational, child/long-term-care exposures) drives the differential — Table 2 maps exposures to likely pathogens.
- **STEC / HUS is a central safety theme:** Detect Shiga toxin (and distinguish O157 by culture); AVOID antibiotics for O157 / Shiga toxin 2 producers; monitor CBC, smear for schistocytes, electrolytes, BUN/creatinine to catch early HUS.
- **Empiric antibiotics are generally not indicated** for immunocompetent adults/children with either bloody or watery diarrhea, with narrow exceptions: suspected bacillary dysentery (*Shigella*), ill febrile international travelers, infants <3 months, immunocompromised with severe bloody diarrhea, and sepsis suspicious for enteric fever. Adult empiric agent = fluoroquinolone or azithromycin per local susceptibility/travel.
- **Enteric (typhoid/paratyphoid) fever** gets a dedicated workup (blood + stool + urine ± bone marrow culture; serology NOT used) and empiric broad-spectrum therapy with later narrowing.
- **Tests to avoid for etiology:** serology, peripheral WBC count, fecal leukocytes, and stool lactoferrin should NOT be used to establish the cause of acute infectious diarrhea; calprotectin—insufficient data.
- **Molecular caveat:** multiplex NAAT/GI panels detect nucleic acid, not necessarily viable organisms; positive bacterial results requiring public-health reporting should be reflex-cultured for subtyping and susceptibility.
- **Supportive care:** reduced-osmolarity ORS is first-line for mild-moderate dehydration of any cause; isotonic IV fluids (LR, NS) for severe dehydration/shock/ileus; continue breastfeeding and resume age-appropriate diet early.
- **Age-specific ancillary care:** loperamide contraindicated <18 years (and in fever/inflammatory diarrhea/toxic-megacolon risk at any age); ondansetron may aid ORS tolerance in children >4 years; oral zinc shortens diarrhea in children 6 months–5 years in zinc-deficient/malnourished settings; probiotics may modestly reduce severity/duration.
- **Vaccines:** rotavirus vaccine for all infants; typhoid vaccine for select travelers/contacts/lab staff with boosters; oral cholera vaccine for adults 18–64 traveling to affected areas.
- **Public health:** notifiable enteric diseases (*Salmonella*, STEC, *Shigella*, *Listeria*, etc.) must be reported with isolate submission; outbreak specimens tested per public-health guidance; defer CDI management to IDSA/SHEA *C. difficile* guidelines.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Temporal cutoffs differ from ACG 2016:** IDSA defines acute as <7 days (with a separate "prolonged" 7–13 day band) and persistent as 14–29 days, whereas [[acg-2016-acute-diarrhea|ACG 2016]] uses acute <14 days and persistent 14–30 days. The wiki [[acute-diarrhea]] page uses the ACG framing; both are noted.
- This is a 2017 guideline; multiplex GI PCR panels (eg, BioFire FilmArray, 22 targets) have since become widespread and largely standard, amplifying Recommendation 14's caveat about interpreting positive nucleic-acid results (colonization vs infection, multiple detections).
- CDI recommendations defer to the IDSA/SHEA *C. difficile* guidelines, which were "in development" at publication and have since been updated; see [[acg-2021-cdiff]].
- Probiotic guidance (Rec 49, weak) predates later, more cautious analyses (eg, [[aga-2020-probiotics|AGA 2020 probiotics guidance]] generally recommends against routine probiotics for most GI indications) — note the divergence.
