## Summary

A short, entirely practical guideline on ingested foreign bodies and food bolus impaction **in adults**, organised as six nonendoscopic and seven endoscopic recommendations. Its single most useful contribution to this wiki is that it **puts a clock on "emergent"**: **preferably within 2 hours, at the latest within 6 hours**, with **urgent = within 24 hours** and **nonurgent = within 72 hours**. [[asge-2011-foreign-body-ingestion|ASGE 2011]], the other guideline on this topic in the corpus, defines "emergent" only by indication and attaches no hour value — a gap [[ingested-foreign-body]] had stated plainly on its own face.

It also replaces a vaguer prior position in two places: it makes the **push technique the suggested first move** for an esophageal food bolus rather than one of three equal options, and it puts **batteries in the stomach or small bowel in the urgent (24-hour) band** rather than ASGE's observe-to-48-hours.

The whole object × location timing matrix is printed as a single table (its Table 3), which is the form the decision actually takes at the bedside.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Recommendations — all 13, numbered by the document
*Grades are the document's own.*

#### Nonendoscopic measures
| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Diagnostic evaluation based on history and symptoms**, with a **physical examination** focused on general condition and on signs of any complication | **Strong**; low |
| 2 | **Against radiological evaluation** for **nonbony food bolus impaction without complications**. **Plain radiography** to assess presence, location, size, configuration and number **if a radiopaque object is suspected or the object type is unknown** | **Strong**; low |
| 3 | **Computed tomography (CT)** in **all** patients with suspected **perforation or other complication that may require surgery** | **Strong**; low |
| 4 | **Against barium swallow** — risk of aspiration, and it worsens endoscopic visualization | **Strong**; low |
| 5 | **Clinical observation without endoscopic removal** for **asymptomatic** patients who have ingested **blunt and small objects — except batteries and magnets**. **Outpatient management is appropriate** where feasible | **Strong**; low |
| 6 | **Body packing** (concealed drug packets, asymptomatic): **close observation**; **against endoscopic retrieval**; **surgical referral** for suspected packet rupture, failure to progress, or intestinal obstruction | **Strong**; low |

#### Endoscopic measures
| # | Recommendation | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | **Emergent therapeutic esophagogastroduodenoscopy — preferably within 2 hours, at the latest within 6 hours** — for foreign bodies causing **complete esophageal obstruction**, and for **sharp-pointed objects or batteries in the esophagus**. **Urgent (within 24 hours)** for other esophageal foreign bodies **without** complete obstruction | **Strong**; low |
| 8 | **Esophageal food bolus: gently push the bolus into the stomach**; if unsuccessful, consider retrieval (weak, low). **Medical treatment must not delay endoscopy** (strong, low) | *see text* |
| 9 | In food bolus impaction, **work up the underlying disease — including histological evaluation — in addition to the therapeutic endoscopy** | **Strong**; low |
| 10 | **Urgent (within 24 hours)** endoscopy for **gastric** foreign bodies that are **sharp-pointed, magnets, batteries, or large/long**. **Nonurgent (within 72 hours)** for **medium-sized blunt** gastric foreign bodies | **Strong**; low |
| 11 | **Use a protective device** to avoid esophagogastric/pharyngeal damage and aspiration when extracting **sharp-pointed** foreign bodies. **Consider endotracheal intubation** where aspiration risk is high | **Strong**; low |
| 12 | Choose the **extraction device according to the type and location** of the object | Weak; low |
| 13 | **Discharge** after successful, uncomplicated endoscopic removal. If the object is not or cannot be removed, take a **case-by-case approach** by size and type | Weak; low |

### Why the clock matters, and the numbers behind each band
- ⚠ **The risk of major complications — perforation with or without mediastinitis, retropharyngeal abscess, aorto-esophageal fistula — increases 14.1-fold when a foreign body is impacted in the esophagus for more than 24 hours.** Delay also reduces the likelihood of successful removal.
- **Complete esophageal obstruction is recognised clinically** — **hypersalivation and inability to swallow liquids** — and carries a high aspiration risk, hence the 2–6 hour window.
- **Sharp-pointed objects perforate in up to 35%**, which is why they are emergent in the esophagus and urgent beyond it.
- **Button or small disk batteries** damage the esophagus very quickly: the narrow lumen puts **both poles in mucosal contact**, causing electrical burns, and leaking **alkaline** contents cause necrosis, perforation or fistula. The heavy metals they contain are present in amounts unlikely to be toxic. **Cylindrical battery ingestion is rare — 0.6% of ingestions — and causes symptoms less often.**
- **Batteries in the stomach:** the risk of electrical burn is low compared with an esophageal battery, and most pass uneventfully, **but** because of liquefaction necrosis from leakage, **removal within 24 hours is considered appropriate**. Past the duodenum, **85% pass through the remaining intestine within 72 hours**.
- **Magnets** attract each other, or a single magnet attracts co-ingested metal, trapping bowel wall between them → pressure necrosis, fistula, perforation, occlusion, volvulus. ⚠ **Remove urgently even when radiographs or the history suggest only one magnet** — the undetected second object is the hazard.
- **Size and length:** objects **wider than 2–2.5 cm in diameter** will normally not pass the pylorus and should be retrieved. Objects **longer than 5–6 cm** usually lodge **at the duodenal curve** and must be removed urgently — **perforation risk 15%–35%**.
- **Spontaneous passage:** **80%–90%** of ingested foreign bodies pass without complication; once past the esophagus most objects pass in **4–6 days**. **Failure to pass beyond the stomach within 3–4 weeks → endoscopic extraction.**
- **Sites of impaction:** physiological narrowings — **upper esophageal sphincter, aortic arch, left main stem bronchus, lower esophageal sphincter, pylorus, ileocecal valve, anus** — plus one physiological **angulation, the duodenal sweep**.

### Imaging
- **Plain radiography is the initial screening method**, to minimise radiation — but ⚠ **false-negative rates are as high as 47%**.
- **No imaging at all** for an uncomplicated nonbony food bolus.
- **Radiocontrast evaluation should not delay a necessary endoscopy**, and barium is specifically rejected: aspiration risk (barium can cause pulmonary edema if aspirated) and degraded endoscopic visualization.
- **CT for any suspected perforation or complication that might need surgery.**

### Food bolus impaction
- **The push technique is primary**, with **success rates over 90%** and minimal complications. **Before pushing, try to bypass the bolus with the endoscope** to assess obstructive pathology beyond it; where that is not possible, most boluses can still be pushed safely with **air insufflation and gentle pressure**.
- ⚠ **An underlying esophageal pathology is found in more than 75% of patients presenting with food bolus impaction** — most often **(mainly peptic) stricture in more than 50%** and **eosinophilic esophagitis in about 40%**. Less often esophageal cancer or a motility disorder (achalasia, diffuse esophageal spasm, nutcracker esophagus). This is why recommendation 9 makes histology part of the same episode of care.
- **When a stricture is found:** proton pump inhibitor therapy, with **dilation performed 2–4 weeks later**.
- **Glucagon** has a limited side-effect profile but is commonly associated with **nausea and vomiting**, and ⚠ **could increase the risk of perforation and aspiration in the presence of a severe impaction**.

### Technique and devices
- **Flexible endoscopy success exceeds 95%, with complication rates of 0%–5%.**
- **Overtubes:** standard-length tubes extending past the **upper** esophageal sphincter protect the airway and ease repeated passage for multiple objects or piecemeal bolus clearance. **Longer 45–60 cm overtubes that extend past the lower esophageal sphincter are for sharp-pointed objects distal to the esophagus.**
- **No overtube available → transparent cap or latex rubber hood** to prevent mucosal injury from a sharp object. A randomized trial found **cap-assisted endoscopy safe and effective for foreign bodies in the upper esophagus, with significantly shorter operating time and a clearer visual field** than conventional esophagogastroduodenoscopy.
- **Endotracheal intubation** where the patient is uncooperative or aspiration risk is high — **full stomach, proximal esophageal location, food bolus impaction**.
- ⚠ **Standard biopsy forceps cannot be recommended** for retrieval — the jaw opening is too small, and removal is rarely successful.
- Device choice by shape: **retrieval forceps** (rat-tooth, alligator-tooth, shark-tooth); **graspers with 2–5 prongs** for soft objects but **not for harder or heavier ones** (insecure grip); **polypectomy snares** (widely available, inexpensive); **baskets** for round objects; **retrieval nets or bags** for a more secure grasp.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- ⚠ **Batteries in the stomach — the two guidelines give different windows.** [[asge-2011-foreign-body-ingestion|ASGE 2011]] places a gastric battery in the **nonurgent** band, observable **up to 48 hours** (and its two internal statements disagree about the <20 mm battery — documented on [[ingested-foreign-body]]). **ESGE 2016 places batteries in the stomach or small bowel in the urgent, within-24-hour band**, reasoning from liquefaction necrosis on leakage rather than electrical burn. **ESGE is newer within the same tier and governs the page**; the ASGE position and its internal contradiction are kept on the record beside it.
- **Food bolus technique — a preference, not a prohibition.** ASGE 2011 treats en bloc removal, piecemeal removal and the push technique as **all acceptable**; ESGE 2016 **suggests pushing first** and retrieving only if that fails (weak, low). The page states the preference with its grade; neither document forbids the alternatives.
- **Underlying pathology after food bolus impaction — the eosinophilic esophagitis figure moved.** ASGE 2011 reports eosinophilic esophagitis in "as many as 33%"; ESGE 2016 reports **>75% have some underlying pathology — stricture >50%, eosinophilic esophagitis about 40%**. Newer figure governs; both are additive rather than contradictory in direction.
- **Size thresholds are expressed as ranges, not points.** ESGE uses **>2–2.5 cm** diameter and **>5–6 cm** length where ASGE uses **>2.5 cm** and **>6 cm**. Not a conflict — ESGE is the looser statement of the same anatomy, and the page uses the ranges with both attributions.
- **European guideline.** There is **no American update on ingested foreign bodies since 2011** in the corpus, so this is used because nothing newer and US exists, not in preference to one. It covers **adults only**; pediatric content on [[ingested-foreign-body]] remains ASGE's.
