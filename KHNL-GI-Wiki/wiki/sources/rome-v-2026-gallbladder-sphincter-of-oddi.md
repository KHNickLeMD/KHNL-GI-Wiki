## Summary

The Rome V chapter covering the three biliary disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI): **dysfunctional gallbladder disorder (DGBD)**, **biliary sphincter of Oddi (SO) disorder**, and **pancreatic SO disorder**. It is a substantial rewrite rather than an incremental update, and the direction of travel is uniformly **more restrictive**: both of the tests that historically licensed intervention — gallbladder ejection fraction (GBEF) on cholescintigraphy and SO manometry — **have been removed from the diagnostic criteria**, and the Milwaukee/Rome III **type I/II/III subtyping of biliary SOD has been abandoned**.

The organizing idea is that most patients historically labelled with gallbladder or SO dysfunction have **a more classic DGBI** — visceral hypersensitivity and gut–brain dysregulation — rather than a primary sphincter or gallbladder motor disease. Because cholecystectomy and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) are both risky in this population and predictors of response remain elusive, the chapter's stated aim is to minimize harm and improve the risk-to-benefit ratio of intervention.

**Typical biliary pain** is the cardinal symptom and the single most important diagnostic criterion. Rome V deliberately renamed the construct from "criteria" to "**definition**" (pain is a symptom, not a diagnosis) and added "**typical**" to denote the pain pattern of patients who actually respond to therapy. The chapter stresses that biliary pain is commonly misunderstood: it is **not** colicky and **not** confined to the right upper quadrant — it rises quickly to maximal intensity and then stays steady, and it most commonly involves the **epigastrium**.

Three criteria sets changed in specific ways. For **DGBD**, GBEF left the supportive criteria and a new supportive criterion — **persistence of symptoms through a preoperative observation period** — was added, reflecting that DGBD is benign, is not life-threatening, and resolves spontaneously in up to 50%. For **biliary SOD**, the supportive criteria were eliminated entirely (manometry for poor sensitivity/reproducibility and failure to predict EPISOD outcomes; scintigraphy for lack of evidence; normal pancreatic enzymes because some biliary SOD patients have elevated ones). For **pancreatic SOD**, the former third criterion — **a negative endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)** — was removed, since excluding other etiologies generally requires EUS anyway.

## Key Criteria and Statements

*Rome V numbers nothing; the Box numbers below are the document's own.*

### Box 1 — Definition of Typical Biliary Pain

Pain located in the **epigastrium and/or right upper quadrant**, and **all** of the following:

1. Acute onset, lasting **at least 20 minutes** and **up to several hours**
2. **Episodic**, with varying intervals between attacks
3. **Severe enough to interrupt daily activities or lead to acute medical evaluation**
4. **Unrelated to bowel movements** and **not relieved by acid suppression or positional changes**

**Supportive characteristics** (not required):

1. Nausea and vomiting
2. Pain radiates to the back and/or right shoulder blade
3. Pain occurs postprandially
4. Pain awakens the patient from sleep

> **Changed from Rome IV:** "criteria" → "**definition**", and "**typical**" added. The pain of true gallbladder/SO dysfunction is said to mimic transient cystic-duct or bile-duct obstruction by a gallstone — **reaching maximal intensity quickly and then remaining steady**, unlike the fluctuating pain of renal colic or intestinal obstruction (Figure 1).

### Box 2 — Diagnostic Criteria for Dysfunctional Gallbladder Disorder

Must include **all** of the following:

1. **Typical biliary pain**
2. **Absence of gallstones or other structural pathology**

**Supportive criteria:**

1. **Normal liver enzymes, conjugated bilirubin, and pancreatic enzymes during the pain episode**
2. **Persistence of symptoms despite a trial of nonoperative therapy**

> **Changed from Rome IV:** the term is now **dysfunctional** gallbladder disorder (replacing functional gallbladder disorder, biliary dyskinesia, chronic acalculous GB dysfunction and chronic acalculous cholecystitis), chosen to avoid implying a demonstrable pathologic process. **GBEF was removed** from the supportive criteria. Supportive criterion 2 — persistence through a preoperative observation period — is **new**, and rests on expert opinion plus the natural history of DGBD.

### Box 3 — Diagnostic Criteria for Biliary Sphincter of Oddi Disorder

Must include **all** of the following:

1. **Typical biliary pain**
2. **Elevated liver tests and/or biliary dilation**
3. **Absence of bile duct stones or other structural abnormalities**

> **Changed from Rome IV: the supportive criteria were eliminated.** Abnormal sphincter manometry was omitted for lack of sensitivity and reproducibility; hepatobiliary scintigraphy was removed for lack of convincing evidence it predicts response to sphincterotomy; the requirement for normal amylase and lipase was dropped because some biliary SOD patients have elevated pancreatic enzymes.

### Box 4 — Diagnostic Criteria for Pancreatic Sphincter of Oddi Disorder

Must include **both** of the following:

1. **Documented recurrent episodes of acute pancreatitis**
2. **Other etiologies of pancreatitis excluded** — gallstones, alcohol, hypertriglyceridemia, autoimmune, hypercalcemia, medications, pancreatic mass, ampullary lesion, pancreas divisum, anomalous pancreaticobiliary junction, genetic, and acute exacerbation of underlying chronic pancreatitis

An episode qualifies as acute pancreatitis on **at least 2 of 3**: typical pain consistent with acute pancreatitis; **pancreatic enzyme elevation >3× the upper limit of normal**; characteristic imaging showing pancreatic inflammation.

> **Changed from Rome IV:** the former **criterion 3, a negative EUS, was removed** — excluding the other etiologies in criterion 2 generally requires EUS in any case. EUS is still the **preferred** diagnostic test for unexplained recurrent acute pancreatitis (RAP), with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) a reasonable complementary or alternative test.

### Subtyping — what Rome V discarded

- **SOD type III is abandoned.** The EPISOD trial and its long-term follow-up found no medium- or long-term benefit from sphincterotomy. These patients "likely have another DGBI and are best managed as such."
- **The type I vs type II distinction has also fallen out of favor**, because SOD subtype does not correlate strongly with outcomes.
- **"Biliary SOD" now applies to all patients** with biliary-type pain and at least transient evidence of biliary obstruction. Most have intermittent spasm; a subgroup with **static** liver-test elevation and duct dilation (no stone or mass) has mechanical obstruction or tonic contraction and is termed **SO stenosis** (or **ampullary stenosis**) — "ERCP in this context is not controversial."
- ⚠ **Do not equate SO stenosis with former type I and SO spasm with former type II.** Spasm can produce concurrent liver-test elevation *and* duct dilation (looking like type I) or just one of them (looking like type II). Many studies informing response to sphincterotomy were stratified by subtype, not by phenotype.

### Bile duct diameter — the threshold is in flux

| | Conventional upper limit | Population MRI reference (recent, needs replication) |
|---|---|---|
| **In situ gallbladder, <65 y** | 6 mm | **8 mm** |
| **In situ gallbladder, ≥65 y** | 6 mm | **11 mm** |
| **Post-cholecystectomy, <65 y** | 9 mm | **13 mm** |
| **Post-cholecystectomy, ≥65 y** | 9 mm | **14 mm** |

> The chapter notes the diagnosis and classification of SOD depend in part on duct diameter, that the upper limit of normal **is controversial and varies significantly across studies**, and that the new age-dependent reference range **requires prospective validation**. Worked example given: a 75-year-old with biliary pain, normal liver tests, prior cholecystectomy and a 11 mm duct **would not meet criteria** under the new reference standard, though they would have been called SOD II in the past.

### Evaluation and treatment — DGBD

- Algorithm (Figure 3): typical biliary pain + suspected DGBD → exclude alternative etiologies → **no potential etiology** → trial of a nonoperative approach (counseling about the lack of progression to complicated disease, lifestyle modification, pharmacologic treatment e.g. neuromodulators) → **clinical reassessment at 3–6 months** → pain unchanged or worse → consider repeat right-upper-quadrant ultrasound or EUS if not already done → **stones or sludge → cholecystectomy**; **no stones or sludge → thoughtful shared decision making** about risks and benefits of cholecystectomy. Pain improved → continue the nonoperative approach.
- **A time delay of 3–6 months before surgery** is prudent: symptoms resolve spontaneously in up to 50%, and DGBD does not predispose to serious complications, so the only reason to operate is symptom relief and quality of life.
- **Cholescintigraphy (CS) has no clear diagnostic role**, but may inform surgical decision making in two groups: (1) typical biliary pain with mild-to-moderate symptoms, where the result may shift the decision curve; (2) **atypical pain, where a normal GBEF should strongly discourage cholecystectomy**.
- Where symptoms are typical, complete pain resolution after cholecystectomy is more likely with **reduced GBEF (81% vs 70%; P = .01)**. Patients meeting DGBD criteria who underwent cholecystectomy showed **84%–93% improvement** in pain scores and quality of life.
- **Atypical pain + normal GBEF → cholecystectomy is certainly not indicated.** Atypical pain + low GBEF → cholecystectomy can be contemplated, with mandatory shared decision making.
- **Patients without upper abdominal pain as a predominant symptom are very unlikely to have a gallbladder disorder and should not be candidates for cholecystectomy regardless of GBEF.**

### Evaluation and treatment — biliary SOD

- Algorithm (Figure 5): typical biliary pain + suspected biliary SOD → exclude alternative etiologies → no potential etiology → **abnormal liver tests or dilated bile duct?** → **no** → gallbladder present? → yes → possible DGBD algorithm; no → consider another DGBI. → **yes** → grade the **level of evidence for biliary obstruction** → **high → ERCP** for biliary SOD or another unrecognized obstructive process (e.g. ampullary neoplasm); **medium to low → thoughtful shared decision making** about risks and benefits of ERCP with sphincterotomy. Factors to weigh: degree of liver-test elevation, degree of duct dilation, changes in liver tests and duct size corresponding with pain episodes, opioid use, evidence of somatization.
- **Manometry has been essentially abandoned** for this indication, on logistic and safety grounds as well as performance: sensitivity is limited by measuring briefly for intermittent spasm, repeat manometry is abnormal in **40%–60%** of persistently symptomatic patients whose initial study was normal, and manometry was neither reproducible nor predictive of outcome in EPISOD.
- **Endoscopic biliary sphincterotomy remains the primary modality** for suspected SO stenosis and spasm. Studies from the manometry era reported pain relief in **>90%** of patients with traditional type I biliary SOD regardless of documented hypertension; evidence in patients with less concrete obstruction (prior type II) **is not strong** — mostly retrospective, open-label, subjective outcomes.
- **Medical therapy directed at the sphincter or enteric nervous system does not currently have a primary role**, though nifedipine, nitrates, trimebutine and duloxetine have signals in small or uncontrolled studies; 75% on nifedipine had statistically less pain in short-term crossover studies, but slow-release nifedipine gave no benefit and more cardiovascular adverse effects.
- Candidate predictors of response reported: normal pancreatic manometry, delayed gastric emptying, daily opioid use, age <40 y (all worse outcomes); intermittent rather than continuous pain, pain accompanied by nausea and vomiting, and a pain-free interval of **≥1 year after cholecystectomy** (better response). RESPOnD found **no** association between SOD subtype and response to ERCP.
- **When a patient does not respond to biliary sphincterotomy**, the most likely explanation is that the pain was never pancreaticobiliary. Repeat ERCP after a prior biliary sphincterotomy carries a lower post-ERCP pancreatitis risk and is justified in patients with a significant prior response, though outcomes have not been rigorously studied.

### Evaluation and treatment — pancreatic SOD

- **Diagnosis is now limited to patients with unexplained RAP** — functionally synonymous with idiopathic RAP, though pancreatic SOD is only a subset of it.
- **There is no evidence that SOD causes pancreatic pain in the absence of acute or chronic pancreatitis**; dilated pancreatic ducts in "unexplained epigastric pain" are rare and more likely chronic pancreatitis or main-duct intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm.
- **EUS is the principal diagnostic modality** for unexplained RAP (yield ~30%–90%); it most often identifies occult biliary stone disease, with ampullary or pancreaticobiliary malignancy in up to **5% after 1 episode** and **12%** with RAP.
- Sphincterotomy prevents further attacks in **60%–80%** in observational studies; a small open-label randomized trial found pancreatic duct stent placement reduced RAP from **53% to 11% (P < .02)**. ⚠ **RESPOnD found the rate of recurrent pancreatitis after sphincterotomy similar to historical controls who had no intervention.**
- **SHARP trial** (international, multicenter, randomized, sham-controlled): **minor papilla sphincterotomy for RAP with pancreas divisum showed no benefit.** Pancreas divisum is not considered a sphincter disorder.
- **Many endoscopists now perform empiric biliary sphincterotomy alone at the first ERCP** and consider pancreatic endotherapy only for inadequate response, because pancreatic sphincterotomy carries higher risks of acute and long-term complications, notably **papillary restenosis**, which can be worse than the original problem.
- Randomized trials are still required to determine whether sphincterotomy is effective for pancreatic SOD and, if so, which approach (biliary, pancreatic or dual) is preferred.

## Key Findings / Claims

- **Both historical gatekeeping tests are out of the criteria**: GBEF for DGBD, and SO manometry for both biliary and pancreatic SOD.
- **SOD type III is gone**, and the type I/II split is deprecated — the phenotype that matters is **stenosis vs spasm**, not the subtype number.
- Up to **40%** of patients have persistent or recurrent pain after cholecystectomy; SOD accounts for only **2%–31%** of post-cholecystectomy pain, while **gastric pathology is the most common etiology in the first 3 years** and bile duct stones the most common after 3 years.
- Cholecystectomy for suspected DGBD rose from **43 to 89 per 1,000,000 population** in the US between 1991 and 2011 — far above international cohorts (typically <25 per 1,000,000) — against a true DGBD prevalence the Rome Global Epidemiology Study put at **<1%** overall. Biliary dyskinesia is the primary reason for cholecystectomy in up to ~**40% of adults and ~60% of children**.
- **Up to one-half of cholecystectomy candidates with low GBEF normalize their emptying on a second study.**
- Among patients with idiopathic RAP, sphincter hypertension has been observed in **25%–75%** of referral-center case series.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Directly supersedes [[asge-2015-ercp-benign-biliary]] and [[asge-2015-benign-pancreatic-disease]]** on classification and on manometry. Those documents build management on the type I/II/III scheme and reserve SO manometry for type II; Rome V abandons the subtypes and removes manometry from the criteria. Rome V is the newer consensus and governs the wiki; the ASGE sphincterotomy *indications* and grades remain useful where Rome V gives none.
- **The bile duct diameter threshold is unsettled within the document itself** — it reports conventional 6 mm / 9 mm limits and a newer age-dependent population reference (8/11 mm in situ, 13/14 mm post-cholecystectomy) and explicitly says the latter needs replication and prospective validation. The wiki carries both and flags the conflict rather than picking one.
- **Pancreatic SOD treatment evidence conflicts:** observational series report 60%–80% prevention of further attacks, while RESPOnD found post-sphincterotomy recurrence similar to untreated historical controls.
- **GBEF's status is deliberately ambiguous** — removed from the diagnostic criteria, yet retained as a possible input to the *surgical* decision in two specific groups. Not a contradiction in the document, but easy to misread as "GBEF is useless."
- The chapter lists **seven research priorities**, most centred on the absence of sham-controlled trials of sphincterotomy and of predictors of response.
