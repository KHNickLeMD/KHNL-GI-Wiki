# AASLD 2020 Practice Guidance: Vascular Liver Disorders, Portal Vein Thrombosis, and Procedural Bleeding in Liver Disease

## Summary

AASLD practice guidance covering three areas: (1) hemostasis and **procedural bleeding** in patients with [[cirrhosis]] ([[cirrhosis-hemostasis|rebalanced hemostasis]]) — bleeding-risk stratification of common procedures and whether to correct platelets, international normalized ratio (INR) or fibrinogen beforehand; (2) **[[portal-vein-thrombosis|portal vein thrombosis (PVT)]]** in patients with and without cirrhosis — standard nomenclature, thrombophilia testing, indications for anticoagulation, interventional options and choice of anticoagulant; (3) the other vascular liver disorders — hepatic vein thrombosis/**[[budd-chiari-syndrome|Budd-Chiari syndrome (BCS)]]**, **sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS)**, liver vascular malformations in **[[hereditary-hemorrhagic-telangiectasia|hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT)]]**, **idiopathic noncirrhotic portal hypertension (INCPH)** / [[porto-sinusoidal-vascular-disorder|portosinusoidal vascular disease (PSVD)]], hepatic and splenic artery aneurysms, and pediatric/congenital disorders.

Developed by expert-panel consensus with AASLD Practice Guidelines Committee oversight; the preamble states it differs from AASLD Guidelines, which use systematic review and Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation. Core positions: traditional coagulation tests do not predict procedural bleeding in cirrhosis, so prophylactic platelet, plasma or INR correction is not routinely advised; PVT treatment in cirrhosis is case-by-case, guided by occlusion degree and chronicity and by the risk that clot progression worsens [[portal-hypertension|portal hypertension]] or hinders a future [[liver-transplantation|liver transplantation (LT)]]; BCS is managed with a step-up strategy from anticoagulation to [[tips|transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS)]] to LT.

One management algorithm: **Figure 4**, recent partially or totally occlusive PVT in cirrhosis (see below). Figures are © 2020 AASLD with no Creative Commons licence, so none is embedded.

### Coagulation and Hemostasis in Cirrhosis

*Guidance Statements, "Current Understanding of Coagulation and Hemostasis in Cirrhosis," p. 369*
- Simultaneous procoagulant and anticoagulant changes in cirrhosis → complex hemostatic changes **not adequately captured** by prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) or platelet count.
- Distinct hypercoagulable and hypocoagulable features may coexist in one patient → may contribute to thrombosis or bleeding, respectively.
- Global hemostasis tests (thrombin generation, whole-blood viscoelastic tests) better capture overall hemostatic status but are **not clinically validated**.

### Procedural Bleeding in Cirrhosis

**Table 3 — bleeding-risk stratification of common procedures in cirrhosis (p. 374).** High risk = major bleeding expected in **>1.5%** of procedures, or even minor bleeding likely to cause permanent organ damage or death.

| Type | Low risk | High risk |
|---|---|---|
| Percutaneous | [[paracentesis\|Paracentesis]]; thoracentesis; drainage catheter exchange | Biliary intervention (cholecystostomy or percutaneous biliary drain); [[liver-biopsy\|liver biopsy]]; tumor ablation; nonliver intra-abdominal solid-organ biopsy; intrathoracic organ biopsy; nephrostomy tube; central nervous system procedures; intraocular procedures/injections; intra-articular injections |
| Vascular | Peripherally inserted central catheter; central venous catheter placement; central line removal; inferior vena cava (IVC) filter placement; diagnostic venography; diagnostic coronary angiography and right heart catheterization | TIPS; angiography or venography with intervention; transjugular liver biopsy; transhepatic arterial chemoembolization or radioembolization; therapeutic coronary angiography |
| Endoscopic | Diagnostic [[upper-endoscopy\|esophagogastroduodenoscopy]] and routine [[endoscopic-band-ligation\|variceal band ligation]]; enteroscopy; colonoscopy (incl. mucosal biopsy); [[ercp\|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]] without sphincterotomy; capsule endoscopy; endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) without fine-needle aspiration; transesophageal echocardiogram; diagnostic bronchoscopy without biopsy | Endoscopic polypectomy; endoscopic stricture dilation or mucosal resection; balloon-assisted enteroscopy; percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy placement; ERCP with sphincterotomy; EUS with fine-needle aspiration; cystgastrostomy; therapeutic bronchoscopy or diagnostic bronchoscopy with biopsy |
| Other | Skin biopsy; dental cleaning and nonextraction procedures | Dental extraction |

*Guidance Statements, "Examples of Common Procedure Risk Assessment in Patients With Cirrhosis," p. 375*
- Procedural bleeding risk is complex → **collaboration between specialists** to set the risk level and plan periprocedural hemostasis.
- Conflicting data → **no data-driven INR or platelet cutoff** at which procedural bleeding risk is reliably increased.
- **Identify and correct modifiable bleeding risk factors** before procedures, especially high-risk elective ones — recommended. Named factors: antithrombotic drugs, acute kidney injury (AKI), infection.
- Text: post–endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL) bleeding is delayed and not predicted by INR/platelets; advanced cirrhosis, Child-Turcotte-Pugh (CTP) class C, was the main risk factor → prophylaxis based on traditional hemostasis tests before EVL is not recommended (p. 375).

*Guidance Statements, "Platelet Interventions and Therapies Targeting Primary Hemostasis," p. 377*
- Reasonable to perform **both low- and high-risk procedures without prophylactically correcting the platelet count** (low bleeding risk of many procedures, transfusion risks, no evidence that raising platelets reduces bleeding, rescue transfusion/hemostasis available if bleeding occurs).
- **Severe thrombocytopenia before procedures → individualized approach** recommended (no definitive safety/efficacy evidence for platelet-raising interventions in cirrhosis).
- Text: thrombopoietin (TPO) receptor agonists avatrombopag and lusutrombopag (2- to 8-day oral course before the procedure) reach platelets ≥50,000/μL more often than placebo but did not reduce postprocedural bleeding → routine use to prevent procedural bleeding cannot be recommended (p. 376).

*Guidance Statements, "Coagulation Interventions and Therapies Targeting Procoagulant Factor Deficiencies," p. 378*
- **INR should not be used** to gauge procedural bleeding risk in cirrhosis in patients not taking vitamin K antagonists (VKAs).
- **Measures to lower the INR are not recommended** before procedures in cirrhosis (patients not on VKAs).
- Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) before procedures: risks, **no proven benefit**.
- Text: advises against prophylactic FFP before common procedures; prophylactic recombinant factor VIIa before invasive procedures not recommended; vitamin K has no measurable INR effect outside advanced malnutrition or chronic cholestasis (p. 377–378).

*Guidance Statements, "Fibrinogen and Therapies Targeting Excessive or Inappropriate Fibrinolysis," p. 379*
- Low fibrinogen is associated with increased bleeding risk in **critically ill** patients with cirrhosis.
- Cryoprecipitate and fibrinogen concentrate are low-volume products effective at raising fibrinogen.
- Text: correcting fibrinogen to **>100 mg/dL** (cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate) before high-risk procedures "could be considered," though data showing fewer bleeds are lacking; prophylactic epsilon-aminocaproic acid (EACA) or tranexamic acid to prevent procedural bleeding cannot be recommended; delay elective procedures during clinically apparent pathological fibrinolysis (p. 378–379).

**Table 4 — society minimum thresholds before high-risk procedures in cirrhosis (p. 377)**

*Guidance Statement, "Terminology and Classification Systems," p. 381*
- Any PVT → **standardized documentation** of initial site, extent, degree of luminal obstruction and chronicity, recommended, to allow objective serial assessment of regression or treatment response.

*Guidance Statements, "PVT Prevalence and Risk Factors," p. 384*
- **Cirrhosis:** ruling out malignant venous obstruction from [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)]] with appropriate contrast-enhanced imaging is **mandatory**.
- **Cirrhosis:** extensive thrombophilia evaluation **not necessary** unless family history or routine laboratory testing raises other concerns.
- **No cirrhosis, thrombosis of the portal venous system without a clear provoking factor:** full investigation for myeloproliferative disorders or another thrombophilic condition is warranted, **usually in consultation with a hematologist**.
- Text: combined hematologist + hepatologist evaluation is recommended for every patient without cirrhosis who develops PVT (p. 382).

### PVT — Mortality, Transplant and Goals of Therapy

*Guidance Statements, "Influence of PVT on Mortality in Patients With Cirrhosis," p. 385–386*
- Outside LT candidates: **unknown** whether PVT is a marker of progressive portal hypertension or an independent cause of mortality.
- LT recipients: PVT at transplant is associated with **increased posttransplant mortality**.
- **Insufficient data to recommend pretransplant PVT treatment** with the goal of improving posttransplant outcomes.

*Guidance Statements, "Goals of Therapy and Rationale for Treatment," p. 386–388*
- **Recent PVT + concern for intestinal ischemia (any patient):** immediate consultation with surgery, critical care, interventional radiology and hematology advised; anticoagulation **essential**; surgery if intestinal infarction.
- **No cirrhosis, recent PVT:** directed antithrombotic therapy **should be considered** — to avoid intestinal ischemia and prevent chronic PVT with portal hypertension.
- **Cirrhosis, no ischemic symptoms:** trial data weak → treat **case by case**, based on expected benefit and minimizing clot extension that could worsen portal hypertension or hinder LT.
- **Cirrhosis, recent thrombosis of small intrahepatic PV sub-branches or minimally occlusive (<50% lumen) main-PV thrombosis:** observation with **serial imaging every 3 months** without therapy is reasonable; consider treatment if the clot progresses.
- **Cirrhosis, recent occlusive or partially occlusive (>50% lumen) thrombosis of the main PV or mesenteric veins:** antithrombotic therapy **should be considered** — to avoid progression that may hinder a future LT or worsen portal hypertension.
- **Chronic complete main-PV occlusion or cavernous transformation with established collaterals:** **no established benefit** of anticoagulant or interventional therapy → treat portal hypertension complications.
- Data suggest **EVL can be done safely without stopping therapeutic anticoagulation**; start anticoagulation as soon as possible — do **not** delay until variceal eradication or adequate beta-blockade.
- Text: septic pylephlebitis (no cirrhosis): prolonged antibiotics adapted to the isolated bacteria or anaerobic digestive flora; limited retrospective data favor concurrent anticoagulation (p. 386).

|---|---|---|

### PVT — Treatment Options

*Guidance Statements, "Thrombolysis and Interventional Vascular Procedures," p. 388*
- Local or systemic **thrombolysis**: only in very selected cases of **recent PVT with intestinal ischemia persisting despite anticoagulation**.
- **Portal vein recanalization (PVR) followed by TIPS** should be considered in **LT candidates with chronic PVT** that prevents a physiological graft–recipient PV anastomosis; multidisciplinary decision with surgical and interventional radiology expertise.
- PVR followed by TIPS should be considered in **chronic PVT with recurrent bleeding and/or refractory ascites** not manageable medically or endoscopically.

*Guidance Statements, "Medical Therapies for PVT" (closing the with- and without-cirrhosis subsections), p. 391*
- Choice of agent — low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH), VKAs, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) — **individualized**; consider consultation with a hematologist and/or expert hepatologist on agent and duration.
- Therapeutic anticoagulation in cirrhosis: non-portal-hypertensive bleeding rates appear **similar to the general population**; portal hypertension–related bleeding appears **unchanged** by anticoagulants.
- DOACs: PVT safety/efficacy data limited with and without cirrhosis. **Cirrhosis with advanced portal hypertension → caution, expert consultation recommended.**
- Text: spontaneous recanalization in up to 40% of cirrhosis PVT, typically within 3 months; recanalization relates to time to start of therapy, <6 months optimal (p. 390).

### Hepatic Vein Thrombosis: Budd-Chiari Syndrome

*Guidance Statements, "Hepatic Vein Thrombosis: Budd-Chiari Syndrome," p. 394–395*
- Consider HVT/BCS in **any liver disease (recent or chronic) of unknown etiology**; suspicion higher with a recognized prothrombotic disorder.
- **Doppler ultrasound by an experienced operator = first-line imaging**; magnetic resonance (MR) or computed tomography (CT) for confirmation and interventional planning.
- At diagnosis: **full thrombophilia workup** recommended; keep looking for additional factors even after one causal factor is found; **hematology consultation** recommended.
- **HCC surveillance in chronic HVT/BCS** as in the general cirrhosis population: **ultrasound every 6 months ± alpha-fetoprotein**. HCC diagnosis should **not rely on imaging criteria alone** — requires histological confirmation (perturbed liver vascularity).
- **Progressive "step-up" strategy** from less to more invasive therapy according to clinical response; early referral to a tertiary center with expertise.
- **All HVT/BCS patients — even without a recognized prothrombotic disorder — should receive therapeutic anticoagulation.**
- **TIPS or direct intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (DIPS) with polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE)-covered stents = treatment of choice** when medical therapy or angioplasty fails or is not feasible. Surgical shunts only if TIPS/DIPS not feasible or fails.
- **LT reserved for failure** of medical and vascular interventional approaches. LT may be the **first step in [[acute-liver-failure|acute liver failure]]**; in that setting consider TIPS/DIPS while waiting (may improve liver function and potentially avoid transplant).
- Text (p. 392–394): underlying prothrombotic disorder in ~75%, >1 in ≥35%; early recognition and treatment of the underlying prothrombotic disorder is critical; immediate LMWH then VKA is the most frequent approach; long-term unfractionated heparin (UFH) should be avoided (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia); DOAC data very limited. Angioplasty ± stenting for segmental stenosis of the hepatic vein or suprahepatic IVC; stenting reserved for angioplasty failures. [[li-rads|Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS)]] criteria exclude HVT/BCS and should not be applied. After LT, keep treating the prothrombotic disorder if LT does not cure it.

### Sinusoidal Obstruction Syndrome

*Guidance Statements, "Sinusoidal Obstruction Syndrome," p. 396*
- Consider SOS in the right setting — typically **1–3 weeks after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)** — with new right upper quadrant pain, weight gain from edema, hepatomegaly and ascites.
- **[[ursodeoxycholic-acid|Ursodeoxycholic acid]] prophylaxis recommended in all patients undergoing allogeneic HSCT**: suggested dose **12 mg/kg divided in two doses**, from the day before conditioning to **3 months after HSCT**.
- **Defibrotide** = only U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved SOS treatment; recommended for **moderate-to-severe SOS**. Benefit as prophylaxis in high-risk cases not established.
- **TIPS is unproven in SOS and cannot be recommended.**
- Text (p. 395–396): defibrotide dose **25 mg/kg/d for at least 21 days and until resolution**. Uncertain diagnosis → transjugular liver biopsy with [[hepatic-venous-pressure-gradient|hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG)]]; clinically significant portal hypertension (HVPG ≥10 mm Hg) without an alternative cause is highly specific for SOS. Myeloablative regimens strongly discouraged in cirrhosis; pre-HSCT workup should focus on excluding cirrhosis.

### Liver Vascular Malformations and Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia

*Guidance Statements, "Liver Vascular Malformations and Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia," p. 398*
- Suspect HHT liver vascular malformations (LVMs) with a **liver bruit** plus CT or MR showing **enlarged hepatic artery and heterogeneous liver enhancement**, especially with family/personal history of recurrent nosebleeds, heart failure, unspecified liver disease or brain hemorrhage.
- **Asymptomatic LVMs: no therapy and no imaging surveillance.**
- Symptomatic LVMs: standard therapy of the specific complication — heart failure, portal hypertension, biliary ischemia.
- Symptomatic patients → specialized multidisciplinary center; **bevacizumab and/or LT** warrant consideration in nonresponders to standard therapy.
- Text (p. 397): liver biopsy not recommended with LVMs (nonspecific findings, possible substantial bleeding risk); ERCP avoided unless a clear dominant stricture amenable to therapy; bevacizumab 5 mg/kg intravenously every 2 weeks for six doses, maintenance cycles for persistent/recurrent symptoms; hepatic artery embolization or surgical ligation generally not recommended.

### Idiopathic Noncirrhotic Portal Hypertension

*Guidance Statements, "Idiopathic Noncirrhotic Portal Hypertension," p. 399*
- Consider INCPH with portal hypertension but **no cirrhosis or other known cause** of noncirrhotic portal hypertension.
- **Liver biopsy required to exclude cirrhosis**; may show specific changes; reticulin stain may help; referral to specialized liver pathologists recommended.
- Routinely consider underlying venous-thrombosis risk factors, immune disorders and inherited disorders associated with INCPH (Table 10: blood diseases, prothrombotic conditions, immunological/inflammatory disorders, drugs — didanosine, azathioprine, thioguanine, oxaliplatin — and genetic conditions incl. HHT with LVMs).
- PVT may be more frequent than in cirrhosis, but **routine PVT screening cannot be recommended**. HCC is rare.
- Text (p. 399): no established independent therapy for INCPH/PSVD; applying cirrhosis or chronic-PVT recommendations is reasonable.

### Hepatic and Splenic Artery Aneurysms

*Guidance Statements, "Hepatic and Splenic Artery Aneurysms," p. 401–402 (true aneurysms, not pseudoaneurysms)*
- Detect hepatic artery aneurysms (HAAs) or splenic artery aneurysms (SAAs) with **Doppler ultrasound or CT**; **multidetector CT angiography** for characterization and treatment planning.
- If intervention needed (multidisciplinary): **endovascular repair first**, open repair if inappropriate; decide with vascular surgery and interventional radiology.
- **Recently diagnosed HAA/SAA <2 cm:** early follow-up imaging (e.g., **3 and 12 months**) to assess growth; significant growth → consider intervention with other specialists.
- **Symptomatic or complicated HAA/SAA → consider urgent intervention.**
- **Pregnancy plans or LT candidates → consider elective intervention.** Others: intervention not proven superior to expectant management.

### Issues Specific to Children and Congenital Disorders

*Guidance Statements, "Issues Specific to Children and Congenital Disorders," p. 403*
- Children with congenital or acquired vascular liver disease → **early referral** to centers with pediatric liver disease, hepatobiliary surgery and LT expertise.
- Children with extrahepatic portal vein obstruction (EHPVO) → evaluation for **early intervention in the presymptomatic stage** at an expert center.
- Glutathione expression may occur in 30%–50% of infantile [[hepatic-hemangioma|hemangiomas]]; **beta-blockers such as propranolol recommended** in these patients.
- Text (p. 402): meso-Rex bypass is the primary, preferred surgical correction for EHPVO; portosystemic shunt (distal splenorenal or mesocaval) if meso-Rex anatomically not possible.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Preprocedural thresholds differ across US societies (Table 4):** AASLD advises no routine correction of platelets, INR or fibrinogen; Society of Interventional Radiology 2019, AGA 2019 and ACG 2020 set minimum platelet and fibrinogen thresholds for high-risk procedures. The document states its platelet stance deviates from other societies.
- **Fibrinogen, internal tension:** Table 4 lists "no routine preprocedure correction," while the text says correction to >100 mg/dL before high-risk procedures "could be considered" (no outcome data).
- **Newer US guidance on PVT in cirrhosis:** [[aga-2025-pvt-cirrhosis]] (AGA 2025) postdates this document; where the two differ, the AGA 2025 claim wins on topic pages.
- **Open questions named by the document:** benefit of pretransplant PVT treatment on posttransplant outcomes; DOAC safety in cirrhosis with advanced portal hypertension; formal safety of EVL without stopping anticoagulation ("more safety studies are needed"); defibrotide as SOS prophylaxis; alpha-fetoprotein >15 ng/mL as an HCC biomarker in BCS (not recommended until validated); value of PVR followed by TIPS in chronic PVT.
