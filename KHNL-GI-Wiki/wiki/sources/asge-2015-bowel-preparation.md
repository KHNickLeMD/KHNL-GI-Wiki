## Summary

ASGE Standards of Practice Committee guideline updating a prior ASCRS/ASGE/SAGES consensus document and technology status evaluation report on bowel preparation before [[colonoscopy]]. Literature search: PubMed, January 1975 – March 2014. Recommendations graded with GRADE.

Frames inadequate preparation as a driver of failed lesion detection, increased adverse-event risk, and incomplete colonoscopy — citing incomplete colonoscopy rates of 10%–20%, with inadequate prep the single most common reason (~25% of failed colonoscopies in one 8910-procedure audit). Defines the ideal preparation (empties the colon rapidly, no gross or histologic mucosal alteration, no discomfort or fluid/electrolyte shifts, safe, convenient, tolerable, inexpensive) and concedes that **no currently available preparation has all these characteristics**.

Its central positions are split dosing and 4 L PEG-ELS. Split dosing (part of the dose on the day of the procedure) yields higher-quality examinations, increases adenoma detection, and improves tolerance; the second dose should fall **3 to 8 hours** before the procedure and be complete **≥2 hours before sedation** (per ASA fasting guidance) to limit aspiration risk. The guideline names **4 L split-dose PEG-ELS the "current criterion standard"** — a position the 2025 statement reverses.

The durable value of this document is descriptive rather than prescriptive: a product-level comparison of the nine commercially available preparations (composition, volume, FDA status, 2015 pricing, dosing) and the three validated preparation-quality scales.

## Key Findings / Claims — Recommendations

All **9 recommendations, verbatim**, with GRADE quality of evidence as given. (The source renders quality as circle glyphs; mapped per its Table 1: ⊕⊕⊕⊕ High, ⊕⊕⊕◯ Moderate, ⊕⊕◯◯ Low, ⊕◯◯◯ Very low.)

1. "We recommend that bowel preparations be individualized by the prescribing provider for each patient based on efficacy, cost, safety, and tolerability considerations balanced with the patient's overall health, comorbid conditions, and preferences." (**High quality**)
2. "We recommend that verbal counseling regarding preparation administration be provided to patients along with written instructions that are simple and easy to follow and in their native language." (**Moderate quality**)
3. "We suggest intensive education and more aggressive than standard bowel preparation regimens be considered for patients with predictors for inadequate preparation." (**Low quality**)
4. "We recommend a low-residue diet be used in conjunction with FDA-approved purgatives for bowel preparation before colonoscopy." (**Moderate quality**)
5. "We recommend split-dose regimens for all patients and/or same day preparations for afternoon colonoscopies with a portion of the preparation taken within 3 to 8 hours of the procedure to enhance colonic cleansing and patient tolerance." (**Moderate quality**)
6. "We recommend that sodium phosphate and magnesium citrate preparations not be used in the elderly or patients with renal disease or taking medications that alter renal blood flow or electrolyte excretion." (**High quality**)
7. "We recommend against the use of metoclopramide as an adjunct to oral bowel preparation." (**Moderate quality**)
8. "We recommend that endoscopists document the quality of the bowel preparation at the time of colonoscopy with regard to adequacy." (**High quality**)
9. "We recommend that patients with inadequate preparation be offered a repeat colonoscopy within 1 year." (**Moderate quality**)

## Bowel Preparation Scales (Table 3)

| Scale | Scored on | Range | Anchors |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aronchick Scale** | Whole colon (segments not evaluated) | 1–5 | 1 Excellent (>95% mucosa seen); 2 Good (clear liquid over ≤25% mucosa, >90% seen); 3 Fair (semisolid stool not suctionable, >90% seen); 4 Poor (semisolid stool not suctionable, <90% seen); 5 Inadequate (repeat preparation needed) |
| **Ottawa Bowel Preparation Scale** | Each of 3 segments (right, transverse/descending, sigmoid/rectum), 0–4 each, **plus** whole-colon fluid score (0 small, 1 moderate, 2 large) | 0 (excellent) – 14 (very poor) | Per segment: 0 Excellent (mucosal detail clearly visible); 1 Good (minimal turbid fluid); 2 Fair (must suction liquid to view); 3 Poor (must wash and suction for reasonable view); 4 Inadequate (solid stool not cleared) |
| **Boston Bowel Preparation Scale (BBPS)** | Each of 3 segments (right, transverse, left), 0–3 each, **after all cleansing maneuvers** | 0 (very poor) – 9 (excellent) | 0 Unprepared segment, stool cannot be cleared; 1 Portion of mucosa seen, other areas obscured by retained material; 2 Minor residual material, mucosa generally well seen; 3 Entire mucosa well seen |

- Aronchick is a global rating best suited to **comparing different preparations** (assesses quality on initial inspection); Ottawa validated against Aronchick; BBPS found both valid and reliable.
- Prep quality should be graded **after** efforts to remove residual effluent and debris are complete.
- USMSTF definition of an adequate examination cited here: one allowing confidence that lesions other than small (≤5 mm) polyps were generally not obscured by residual colonic contents.

## Key Findings / Claims — Supporting Detail

**Agent-specific safety and performance (largely still applicable):**
- **PEG-ELS:** 5%–15% do not complete it (palatability, volume). No significant physiologic change in weight, vital signs, electrolytes, chemistries, or CBC. **Does not alter colonic mucosal histology** — usable in suspected [[inflammatory-bowel-disease]] without obscuring diagnosis or tissue analysis. Generally safe in pre-existing electrolyte imbalance and where sodium load is poorly tolerated (renal failure, congestive heart failure, advanced liver disease with [[ascites]]). Works best ingested quickly (240 mL every 10 min); chilling improves palatability. Reported adverse events: nausea ± vomiting, abdominal pain, rare pulmonary aspiration, Mallory-Weiss tear, pancreatitis, colitis, lavage-induced pill malabsorption, cardiac arrhythmia, SIADH exacerbation.
- **Adding prokinetics or bisacodyl to 4 L PEG-ELS does not improve tolerance or cleansing**; adding enemas does not improve efficacy and increases discomfort.
- **PEG-SD (PEG-3350 + sports drink):** hyposmotic, **not FDA approved** for colonoscopy prep, **not equivalent** to FDA-approved 2 L isosmotic PEG-ELS — despite wide use. Versus FDA-approved 2 L PEG-ELS, changes from baseline in serum Na, K, Cl were significantly greater with PEG-SD; hyponatremia 3.9% vs 2.2% (OR 1.82, 95% CI 0.45–8.62; not statistically significant). One study suggested a lower adenoma detection rate with 2 L PEG-SD/bisacodyl vs 4 L PEG-ELS.
- **Ascorbic-acid PEG:** use cautiously in G6PD deficiency (ascorbic acid may provoke hemolysis).
- **OSS:** noninferior to 2 L PEG-ELS with ascorbic acid; vs 4 L SF-PEG-ELS, less bloating and more frequent excellent preparation (71.4% vs 34.3%, P = .01). Vs split-dose sodium picosulfate/magnesium citrate (n = 338): higher successful prep (94.7% vs 85.7%, P = .006) and more excellent preps (54% vs 26%, P < .001), with no difference in treatment-emergent adverse events. Limited safety data.
- **Sodium picosulfate/magnesium citrate:** stimulant (picosulfate) + osmotic (magnesium citrate) mechanism. Phase 3 trials achieved adequate preparation in only **83%–84.2%**. Rare hyponatremia and clinically significant electrolyte disturbance.
- **Magnesium citrate:** not FDA approved as a colonoscopy prep; limited stand-alone efficacy data; magnesium toxicity (bradycardia, hypotension, nausea, drowsiness) with deaths reported — **not recommended for routine preparation**.
- **Sodium phosphate:** brand-name aqueous form voluntarily withdrawn; **FDA box warning on the prescription tablet form**. Acute phosphate nephropathy (age- and dose-related, reported even without underlying disease); hyperphosphatemia in up to 40% of healthy patients completing prep; also elevated BUN, increased plasma osmolality, hypocalcemia, hyponatremia, seizures.
- **Bisacodyl:** can cause cramping and has been associated with [[colon-ischemia|ischemic colitis]] — 5 and 10 mg doses recommended when used adjunctively. The only FDA-approved 2 L PEG-ELS + bisacodyl regimen was discontinued by the manufacturer in 2013.
- **Metoclopramide:** reduced nausea/bloating in one study but did not improve cleansing; a second showed no advantage for either tolerance or cleansing → recommended against (Rec 7).
- **Simethicone:** does **not** significantly change preparation quality, but reduces adherent bubbles, which may enhance visualization.
- **Senna:** no difference vs bisacodyl as a PEG-ELS adjunct; improves prep quality and reduces PEG-ELS volume required.

**Administration and logistics:**
- Clear liquids may be taken up to **2 hours** before the procedure; preparation must be complete **≥2 hours before sedation** (ASA guidance) to avoid aspiration — institutional policies may vary. See [[endoscopy-sedation]].
- Red liquids should be avoided (mistaken for blood / obscure mucosal detail).
- Unclear whether a clear liquid diet the day before offers any advantage over a low-fiber diet for prep quality; low-residue diets are at least as effective as clear liquids and improve satisfaction. In hospitalized patients, clear liquid diet before the purgative was the **only dietary modification that improved prep quality**.
- Split-dosing is recommended for **both morning and afternoon** procedures; for afternoon procedures the prep may be given entirely on the morning of the examination (superior quality/tolerability, less impact on activities of daily living, better sleep). Hospitalized patients prefer split-dosing though prep quality did not differ vs morning-only.
- When educated on the benefit, the vast majority of patients are willing to wake at 2–3 AM to complete a split-dose regimen.
- **NG tube administration:** PEG-ELS gut lavage via NG tube is the most effective cleansing method in infants and children, and is an option when patients cannot drink or have a significant swallowing disorder. **High-dose (6–8 L) PEG-ELS via NG tube is effective as rapid preparation in [[acute-lower-gi-bleeding]].** Risk of severe, life-threatening adverse events including aspiration; prokinetics/antiemetics and avoiding over-rapid instillation improve tolerability.
- **Pediatrics:** no national standards; topic deferred to a prior ASGE guideline.
- Patient education significantly improves prep quality — written instructions in the patient's native language, visual aids, educational booklets, and smartphone applications all described.

**Inadequate preparation:**
- **The most important predictor of inadequate preparation is a previous inadequate preparation.** Other predictors: later colonoscopy start time, failure to follow instructions, hospitalized status, procedural indication of [[chronic-idiopathic-constipation|constipation]], tricyclic antidepressant use, male sex, history of cirrhosis, stroke, or dementia; [[obesity]] may also predict poor prep. Additional factors: non-English speaking, Medicaid insurance, single and/or inpatient status, polypharmacy (especially constipating medications such as opiates), advanced age, diabetes mellitus, Parkinson's disease, poor adherence, erroneous purgative timing, longer appointment wait times.
- Fewer than 20% of patients with inadequate preparation reported failing to follow instructions.
- Consequences: missed lesions, cancelled procedures, increased procedural time and cost, potential increase in adverse events. With **fair** preps, 28%–42% had adenomas found when repeated within 3 years, including up to 27% with advanced adenomas. Adenoma and advanced-adenoma **miss rates were 35% and 36%** for colonoscopies repeated in <1 year. Intraprocedural cleansing accounts for an estimated **17% of total colonoscopy procedural time**.
- Patients with persistent brown effluent before the procedure should be considered for large-volume enemas or additional oral preparation before proceeding.
- No standardized approach to an inadequately prepped colon found at intubation. Anecdotal approaches: instilling an enema through the colonoscope then reattempting after evacuation, or additional oral preparation then reattempting — both require recovery from sedation and resedation. Irrigation devices permit more aggressive water instillation than standard pumps or syringe flushing.
- Immediate repeat after additional/more aggressive preparation is preferred in most patients; otherwise repeat **within 1 year**, with a **shorter interval if advanced neoplasia is discovered** in an inadequately prepped colon. When instructed to repeat the following day, 47% complied — significantly higher than for later intervals.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

**Superseded by [[usmstf-2025-bowel-prep]] (2025 wins per source priority — Guidelines tier, newer publication date):**

| Topic | ASGE 2015 | USMSTF/ACG 2025 | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Preparation volume** | 4 L split-dose PEG-ELS is the **"current criterion standard"**; highest-quality preps achieved with 4 L | **No single purgative is superior** (Strong/High); **2 L regimens preferred over 4 L** (Weak/Moderate) | 2025 wins — 4 L is no longer the reference standard; it is retained only within the high-risk protocol |
| **Second-dose timing** | Portion taken **within 3 to 8 hours** of the procedure (Rec 5) | Second portion **begins 4–6 h before**, completed **≥2 h before** procedure start (Strong/Moderate) | 2025 wins — narrower window; the 2015 3 h and 8 h extremes are out |
| **Diet** | **Low-residue diet** with FDA-approved purgatives (Rec 4); text describes low-residue diets "often recommended for **several days** before the procedure" | **Limit dietary modification to the day before** for average-risk ambulatory patients (Strong/High); low-residue or full liquids for early/midday meals | 2025 wins — multi-day restriction now reserved for **high-risk** patients only (2025 Rec 23: restrict vegetables/legumes 2–3 days prior) |
| **Simethicone** | "Simethicone does **not** significantly change the quality of the bowel preparation" — no recommendation issued | **Suggest adjunctive oral simethicone ≥320 mg** (Weak/Moderate) | 2025 wins — direct reversal on a positive recommendation |
| **Non-simethicone adjuncts** | Bisacodyl / magnesium citrate / senna presented favorably as volume-reducing adjuncts to PEG-ELS | **Against routine use of non-simethicone adjuncts** (Weak/Low) | 2025 wins — bisacodyl retained only in the specific high-risk 4 L regimen (2025 Rec 25) |
| **Quality target** | Document prep quality "with regard to adequacy" (Rec 8) — **no numeric target** | Track adequate-prep rate at **endoscopist AND unit** level; **target >90%** (Strong/Moderate) | 2025 wins and is additive (note [[acg-2014-bowel-cleansing]] set ≥85% per-physician; 2025 raised it to >90% and added the unit level) |

**Concordant / additive (2015 still corroborates):**
- Split-dose for all patients; same-day acceptable for afternoon colonoscopy (2015 Rec 5 ≈ 2025 Recs 9–10). 2025 adds that same-day is **inferior** for morning colonoscopy (Weak/Low) — 2015 is silent, not conflicting.
- Verbal + written instructions in the patient's native language (2015 Rec 2 ≈ 2025 Rec 1).
- Individualize prep by efficacy/cost/safety/tolerability and comorbidity (2015 Rec 1 ≈ 2025 Rec 7).
- Intensified education and more aggressive regimens for predictors of inadequate prep (2015 Rec 3 ≈ 2025 Recs 23–24).
- Avoid NaP and magnesium citrate in the elderly/renal disease (2015 Rec 6) ≈ 2025's generalized "against hyperosmotic regimens in patients at risk for volume overload or electrolyte disturbances" (Strong/High).
- Against metoclopramide (2015 Rec 7) — subsumed by 2025's against-routine-non-simethicone-adjuncts.
- Grade prep quality only after residual debris is cleared (2015 text ≈ 2025 Rec 16).
- Repeat within 1 year after inadequate prep (2015 Rec 9 ≈ 2025 Rec 22's 12 months). 2025 additionally requires **≤3 months** when the colonoscopy was prompted by an abnormal noncolonoscopic CRC test — 2015 has no counterpart.

**Open questions carried by this source:**
- Whether a clear liquid diet the day before confers any advantage over a low-fiber diet — unresolved in 2015; 2025 permits either.
- Safety of PEG-SD combined with bisacodyl "has not been well reported"; whether bisacodyl adds benefit or only side effects was explicitly unclear in 2015 — 2025 resolves against routine use.
- Limited safety data for OSS (no serious adverse effects reported at the time).
- No standardized approach to the inadequately prepped colon found at intubation; 2025 addresses this with same-day salvage maneuvers (Weak/Moderate).
- Widely used PEG-SD (Miralax/Gatorade) remains **not FDA approved** for colonoscopy preparation and is not equivalent to isosmotic 2 L PEG-ELS — a caution not carried forward explicitly in the 2025 statement and still worth surfacing.
