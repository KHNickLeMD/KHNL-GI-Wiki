# Tokyo Guidelines 2018: Diagnostic Criteria and Severity Grading of Acute Cholecystitis

## Summary

This is the TG18 article that holds the **diagnostic criteria (Table 1) and the severity grading (Table 7) for acute cholecystitis** — the tables the other Tokyo Guidelines 2018 papers refer to but do not print. Its headline conclusion is a non-change: after searching the literature published since the Tokyo Guidelines 2013 (TG13) and identifying 216 relevant articles including 19 randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the Revision Committee found no evidence strong enough to alter either instrument, and **adopted the TG13 diagnostic criteria and the TG13 severity grading as the TG18 criteria and grading without any modification.** The decision was ratified by public vote (89.3% yes, 10.7% no) at a public hearing in Yokohama on 9 June 2017.

The **diagnostic criteria** are a three-part structure: **A** local signs of inflammation, **B** systemic signs of inflammation, **C** imaging findings characteristic of acute cholecystitis. One item from A plus one item from B = **suspected** diagnosis; adding C makes it **definite**. This design — introduced in TG13 — exists specifically to fix the TG07 problem that two categories alone were used for a definitive diagnosis and no suspected-diagnosis category existed, which gave TG07 a sensitivity of 84.9% but a specificity of only 50.0%. Requiring imaging confirmation raised performance to 91.2% sensitivity and 96.9% specificity in the 451-patient multicenter validation. The criteria carry **no numeric cut-points** for fever, C-reactive protein (CRP) or white blood cell count (WBC) — they say "fever", "elevated CRP", "elevated WBC count" and nothing more. Acute hepatitis, other acute abdominal diseases and chronic cholecystitis must be excluded.

The **severity grading** is a 3-tier scheme read from the top down. **Grade III (severe)** = acute cholecystitis with dysfunction of any one of six organ systems, each with an explicit numeric definition. **Grade II (moderate)** = any one of four conditions, including a WBC >18,000/mm³, a palpable tender right upper quadrant mass, symptoms lasting >72 h, and "marked local inflammation" — which is where gangrenous cholecystitis, emphysematous cholecystitis, pericholecystic abscess, hepatic abscess and biliary peritonitis sit. **Grade I (mild)** is the residual category. Thirty-day mortality tracks the grade (1.1% / 0.8% / 5.4%), as do length of stay, conversion to open surgery and complication rates across six validation cohorts.

The remainder of the article is **diagnostic imaging**. Ultrasound is the recommended first-choice modality on grounds of invasiveness, availability, ease and cost — explicitly *not* on diagnostic yield, which is lower than cholescintigraphy's. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) / magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the recommended second step when ultrasound is non-diagnostic; **contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) or contrast-enhanced MRI** is recommended for gangrenous cholecystitis, and **plain CT** for emphysematous cholecystitis. Procalcitonin and color/power Doppler sonography were both assessed and both designated future research questions rather than recommendations.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Table 1 — TG18/TG13 diagnostic criteria for acute cholecystitis

| | Criterion | Items |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Local signs of inflammation etc. | (1) Murphy's sign · (2) right upper abdominal quadrant (RUQ) mass / pain / tenderness |
| **B** | Systemic signs of inflammation etc. | (1) Fever · (2) elevated CRP · (3) elevated WBC count |
| **C** | Imaging findings | Imaging findings characteristic of acute cholecystitis |

- **Suspected diagnosis:** one item in A **+** one item in B.
- **Definite diagnosis:** one item in A **+** one item in B **+** C.
- **Exclusions required:** acute hepatitis, other acute abdominal diseases, and chronic cholecystitis.
- **No numeric thresholds are attached** to fever, elevated CRP or elevated WBC in these criteria.
- Table footnote: the TG13 diagnostic criteria were judged useful indicators by numerous validation studies and adopted as the TG18 diagnostic criteria **without any modification**.

### Table 7 — TG18/TG13 severity grading for acute cholecystitis

**Grade III (severe)** — acute cholecystitis associated with dysfunction of **any one** of the following organs/systems:

1. **Cardiovascular dysfunction:** hypotension requiring treatment with dopamine ≥5 μg/kg per min, or any dose of norepinephrine
2. **Neurological dysfunction:** decreased level of consciousness
3. **Respiratory dysfunction:** PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio <300
4. **Renal dysfunction:** oliguria, creatinine >2.0 mg/dL
5. **Hepatic dysfunction:** prothrombin time–international normalized ratio (PT-INR) >1.5
6. **Hematological dysfunction:** platelet count <100,000/mm³

**Grade II (moderate)** — acute cholecystitis associated with **any one** of the following conditions:

1. **Elevated WBC count (>18,000/mm³)**
2. **Palpable tender mass** in the right upper abdominal quadrant
3. **Duration of complaints >72 h** — table footnote: *laparoscopic surgery should be performed within 96 h of the onset of acute cholecystitis*
4. **Marked local inflammation:** gangrenous cholecystitis, pericholecystic abscess, hepatic abscess, biliary peritonitis, emphysematous cholecystitis

**Grade I (mild)** — does not meet the criteria of Grade III or Grade II. Also definable as acute cholecystitis in a healthy patient with no organ dysfunction and mild inflammatory changes in the gallbladder, making cholecystectomy a safe and low-risk operative procedure.

- Table footnote: TG13 severity assessment criteria adopted as TG18 severity assessment criteria **without any modification**. **To judge predictive factors of acute cholecystitis on the flowchart in Grade III, serum total bilirubin must be measured.**

### Diagnostic performance of the criteria

| Instrument / study | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|
| TG07 criteria (original validation) | 84.9% | 50.0% |
| Murphy's sign alone (same study) | 20.5% | 87.5% |
| TG13 criteria — 451-patient multicenter validation | 91.2% | 96.9% |
| TG13 criteria — second study, pathology as gold standard | 83.1% | 37.5% |

- Diagnostic accuracy of the TG07 criteria was significantly greater than that of Murphy's sign alone (*P* = 1.31 × 10⁻¹⁰).
- Overall **diagnostic accuracy of the TG13 criteria ranges from 94.0% to 60.4%** across studies when pathological samples are the gold standard.
- In the lower-performing study, **neutrophil count was the only independent predictor** of acute cholecystitis on multivariate analysis — but using neutrophil count alone for a definitive diagnosis is unrealistic.
- The World Society of Emergency Surgery guidelines for acute calculous cholecystitis recommend combined use of clinical, laboratory and imaging findings without designating new criteria — which TG18 notes is exactly what the TG13 criteria are.
- A Japanese study found statistically significant differences in length of hospitalization and medical costs between **definite** and **suspected** diagnoses, supporting the two-level design.

### Imaging — findings and modality performance

**Generally accepted imaging findings of acute cholecystitis** (with the cut-points the paper prints):

- Thickening of the gallbladder wall **(≥4 mm)**
- Enlargement of the gallbladder **(long axis ≥8 cm, short axis ≥4 cm)**
- Gallstones or retained debris
- Fluid accumulation around the gallbladder
- Linear shadows in the fatty tissue around the gallbladder

**Figure 1 — pooled summary estimates from a 2012 meta-analysis of imaging in acute cholecystitis:**

| Modality | Sensitivity (95% CI) | Specificity (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|
| Cholescintigraphy (hepatobiliary scintigraphy / HIDA) | 0.94 (0.92–0.96) | 0.90 (0.85–0.93) |
| Ultrasonography | 0.82 (0.75–0.87) | 0.81 (0.73–0.87) |
| MRI | 0.86 (0.66–0.95) | 0.82 (0.69–0.90) |

- All studies comparing hepatobiliary scintigraphy with ultrasound found **diagnostic yield higher for HIDA scanning** — yet ultrasound is still recommended first in three newly proposed guidelines, and by TG18, despite its limited diagnostic yield. Reported rate of ultrasound use in clinical practice: **61.3%**.
- Ultrasound limitations: a causative stone in the gallbladder or bile duct may not be clearly identifiable, and **gangrenous cholecystitis may be difficult to diagnose** — hence the recommendation to add contrast-enhanced CT or MRI when required.
- MRI/MRCP detail: non-contrast MRI/MRCP already visualises wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid and gallbladder enlargement, and one study found it **not inferior to contrast-enhanced MRI**. Thickening of the gallbladder wall plus dense staining of the gallbladder bed in the early phase of contrast-enhanced MRI has **92% specificity** for acute cholecystitis versus chronic cholecystitis. Abnormal signal in the fatty tissue around the gallbladder on T2-weighted MRI had higher specificity than CT findings. MRCP shows the biliary anatomy without contrast, useful preoperatively. MRI is **expensive** relative to ultrasound and image quality degrades in a patient who cannot hold still or breath-hold.
- The 2012 MRI meta-analysis was based on three cohort studies and a cross-sectional study from around the turn of the millennium, before contrast-enhanced MRI and MRCP were in general use.

**Gangrenous cholecystitis — specific findings on dynamic CT:** irregular thickening of the gallbladder wall; poor contrast enhancement of the wall (**interrupted rim sign**); increased density of fatty tissue around the gallbladder; gas in the gallbladder lumen or wall; membranous structures within the lumen (intraluminal flap / intraluminal membrane); peri-gallbladder abscess. Irregularity or rupture of the wall is **often underestimated on abdominal ultrasound**.

| Finding | Performance |
|---|---|
| Interrupted rim sign on contrast-enhanced CT | 73% sensitivity, 95% negative predictive value |
| Intraluminal membranous structures on contrast-enhanced MRI | 80% diagnostic accuracy |
| Perfusion defect of gallbladder wall **+** no identifiable calculi | 92% diagnostic accuracy, 88.2% sensitivity, 100% specificity |

- **Gangrenous cholecystitis is classed as moderate (Grade II)** acute cholecystitis under TG13/TG18 — a serious condition that may cause organ damage if diagnosis is delayed.

**Emphysematous cholecystitis:** inflammation caused by aerogenic bacteria, with a **high perforation rate**; potentially fatal complications include intra-abdominal abscess, generalized peritonitis, gas gangrene of the abdominal wall and sepsis; the clinical course is often extremely rapid. **Also classed as moderate (Grade II)** acute cholecystitis ("marked local inflammation").

- On ultrasound it is very often difficult to distinguish intramural gas from intraluminal gas and from porcelain gallbladder, all of which appear hyperechoic — and gas may be present in the lumen after biliary surgery or sphincterotomy.
- On CT gas is clearly hypodense (usually near **−1,000 HU**), making detection extremely easy; **plain CT is the most useful method.** Contrast-enhanced CT should be considered for evaluating complications (intraperitoneal abscess, peritonitis).
- Gas appears as a signal void on MRI, but MRI is **inferior to CT** in spatial resolution for detecting minute amounts of gas.
- Intramural gas is often present in gangrenous cholecystitis as well.

### Outcomes by severity grade (the validation evidence behind Q6)

| Outcome | Grade I | Grade II | Grade III | *P* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **30-day overall mortality** (n = 1,339 / 1,702 / 680) | 15 (1.1%) | 13 (0.8%) | 37 (5.4%) | <0.001 |

- Overall mortality for acute cholecystitis is only **around 1%**; in a case series of over 5,000 patients the prognosis for Grade III was significantly worse than Grades I and II.
- **Length of hospital stay** rises with grade across six cohorts (e.g. 7.3 ± 3.5 / 9.2 ± 3.9 / 15.2 ± 8.5 mean days; 4.3 ± 2.5 / 11.0 ± 11.6 / 20.8 ± 13.5).
- **Conversion from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy** rises with grade across five cohorts (e.g. 7.0% / 25.6% / 34.6%; 6.3% / 15.2% / 34.6%).
- **Complications (morbidity)** rise with grade (e.g. 9.7% / 28.0% / 45.0%; 2.9% / 3.1% / 11.1%; 8.9% / 15.2% / 46.2%).
- In a US study, multivariate analysis showed TG13 severity grade was an **independent predictor of both length of stay and conversion to open surgery**. Intraoperative bile duct injury also occurred significantly more often in higher-grade cases, and postoperative pathological findings of gangrenous/emphysematous cholecystitis were more severe in higher grades. Medical costs are significantly higher in higher-grade cases.
- Some studies, including case series, **failed to find any association between severity grade and prognosis**; nevertheless logistic regression analysis showed TG13 severity grading predicts mortality on admission.

### Within Grade III — predictors of death (Table 6)

Multivariate analysis of 30-day mortality in Grade III patients (survivors n = 591, non-survivors n = 20):

| Factor | Multivariate *P* | Odds ratio (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|
| **Jaundice** | <0.01 | 6.470 (2.446–17.110) |
| **Respiratory dysfunction** | <0.01 | 5.843 (2.052–16.635) |
| **Neurological dysfunction** | <0.01 | 4.346 (1.640–11.515) |
| Charlson comorbidity index ≥6 | 0.380 (not significant) | — |

- These three factors were used to propose a **new treatment strategy for Grade III** — rather than change the Grade III assessment criteria, it may be possible to **subdivide Grade III** to enable safe surgery and select the appropriate treatment strategy.

### What TG18 declined to adopt, and what the grading cannot do

- **Two newly proposed severity scoring systems were reviewed and neither was adopted**, because *neither indicates criteria for severity grading*:
  - A German preoperative clinical score — 8 independent risk factors by multivariate analysis (sex, age, body mass index, American Society of Anesthesiologists [ASA] score, recurrent colic, gallbladder wall thickness, WBC, CRP), maximum 9 points, **≥7 points designated severe (Grade III)**; correlated with operating time, intensive care unit admission and length of stay, **but not with complications or conversion rate**.
  - An Italian scheme designating gangrenous and phlegmonous cholecystitis as severe, from 4 factors (fever >38 °C, distention of gallbladder, wall edema, preoperative colic): **≥2 factors positive → 54.9% sensitivity (44.1–65.2), 81.2% specificity (75.4–85.9); ≥3 factors → 15.9% sensitivity (9.5–25.3), 98.6% specificity (95.9–99.5)**.
- **"The TG13 severity grading cannot be used to assess surgical difficulty."** Producing a severity-criteria set that includes surgical difficulty would require a large-scale validation study.
- Surgery for patients classed as Grade III **is feasible even where percutaneous cholecystostomy is not always performed**, with conversion or subtotal cholecystectomy as possible procedures.
- **Procalcitonin:** systematic reviews of its value in sepsis diagnosis/grading are inconsistent in study design; only a single study is limited to acute cholecystitis (it correlated with TG13 severity grade). Evidence insufficient — designated a question for future research.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Internal inconsistency in the imaging numbers.** The running text quotes the same meta-analysis as giving ultrasound **81% sensitivity (95% CI 0.75–0.87) and 83% specificity (95% CI 0.74–0.89)** and MRI **85% sensitivity (0.66–0.95) and 81% specificity (0.69–0.90)**, while the paper's own Figure 1 forest plot prints ultrasound **0.82 / 0.81 (0.73–0.87)** and MRI **0.86 / 0.82**. The forest-plot values are used on the wiki page; the discrepancy is small and does not change any decision.
- **No numeric cut-points in the diagnostic criteria.** "Fever", "elevated CRP" and "elevated WBC count" are undefined in Table 1 — a clinician applying the criteria supplies the local reference ranges. TG18 does not acknowledge this as a limitation.
- **Specificity of the criteria is unstable.** 96.9% in the multicenter validation versus 37.5% in the study using pathology as gold standard, with overall accuracy ranging 94.0% to 60.4%. TG18 nonetheless concludes there are "no major problems".
- **Ultrasound is recommended first despite being the least accurate of the three modalities studied** — cholescintigraphy outperforms it on both sensitivity and specificity in every comparative study. The recommendation rests explicitly on invasiveness, availability, ease and cost, not yield.
- **TG18 does not supply a numeric "high surgical risk" score.** It states plainly that the severity grading *cannot* be used to assess surgical difficulty, and rejected both proposed preoperative scores. The gap left open by [[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage]] (high surgical risk defined by judgment, not criteria) is therefore **not closed** by this source.
- **Grade III is not automatically inoperable.** TG18 states surgery for Grade III is feasible even without percutaneous cholecystostomy — a different emphasis from the drainage-first framing of the endoscopic-drainage guidelines on [[acute-cholecystitis]], though neither contradicts the other outright since those address patients already deemed non-operative.
- **Severity grade and prognosis are not universally linked.** Some case series found no association; overall acute cholecystitis mortality is only ~1%, so the grading's prognostic value is driven almost entirely by the Grade III stratum.
- **10.7% of the public hearing voted against** adopting TG13 criteria and grading unchanged; the article does not report the objections.
- **Two designated future research questions:** procalcitonin (level C) and color/power Doppler sonography (level D). Neither is a recommendation for or against use.
- **Grade II "duration of complaints >72 h" vs the 96 h surgical window** are two different clocks in the same table (symptom duration grading the severity; onset-to-surgery limiting the laparoscopic approach). The paper prints both without reconciling them.
