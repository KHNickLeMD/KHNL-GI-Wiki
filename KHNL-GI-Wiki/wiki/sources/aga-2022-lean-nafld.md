## Summary

This AGA Clinical Practice Update addresses the subset of patients with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) who do **not** have an overweight or obese body habitus. NAFLD affects >25% of the US and global populations, and up to 1 in 4 of those affected have nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH); an estimated **7%–20% of individuals with NAFLD are lean**. The update's central move is to name lean NAFLD as a diagnosis with an explicit, race-adjusted BMI definition, and then to argue against treating it as a benign phenotype: lean patients with NAFLD have been shown in longitudinal cohorts to have *higher* liver-related mortality and a higher risk of progressing to severe liver disease than overweight/obese patients with NAFLD, despite less severe histology at baseline.

Because lean NAFLD is a diagnosis of exclusion in a body habitus that does not itself explain steatosis, the update devotes most of its diagnostic content to **ruling out alternative and secondary causes of fatty liver** — covert alcohol use, hepatitis C (especially genotype 3), celiac disease, hypothyroidism, drug-induced steatosis, lipodystrophy, lysosomal acid lipase deficiency, familial hypobetalipoproteinemia, and a long tail of genetic, endocrine, nutritional, and toxin exposures (Table 1), worked through in a stepwise, clinically-guided fashion (Table 2). Routine genotyping for NAFLD risk variants (*PNPLA3*, *TM6SF2*, *MBOAT7*, *GCKR*, *HSD17B13*) is explicitly **not** advised in this population.

For risk stratification, the update endorses noninvasive tests (NITs) over routine biopsy and proposes a sequential algorithm (Figure 1) anchored on FIB-4, with a second NIT (ELF or VCTE) for the indeterminate band. Liver biopsy is reserved for uncertainty about the contributing cause of liver injury or the stage of fibrosis. Treatment is lifestyle-first at a **lower weight-loss target (3%–5%)** than that used in overweight/obese NAFLD, with vitamin E and pioglitazone available only for biopsy-confirmed NASH under stated restrictions, and GLP-1 agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors judged premature for lean NASH specifically.

## Best Practice Advice Statements (all 15, verbatim; ungraded, numbered 1–15 by the source)

**Best Practice Advice 1:** Lean NAFLD should be diagnosed in individuals with NAFLD and a body mass index (BMI) <25 kg/m² (non-Asian race) or a BMI <23 kg/m² (Asian race).

**Best Practice Advice 2:** Lean individuals with NAFLD should be evaluated routinely for comorbid conditions, such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and hypertension.

**Best Practice Advice 3:** Lean individuals with NAFLD should be risk stratified for hepatic fibrosis to identify those with advanced fibrosis or cirrhosis.

**Best Practice Advice 4:** Lean individuals in the general population should not undergo routine screening for NAFLD; however, screening should be considered for individuals older than 40 years with type 2 diabetes mellitus.

**Best Practice Advice 5:** NAFLD should be considered in lean individuals with metabolic diseases (such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and hypertension), elevated liver biochemical tests, or incidentally noted hepatic steatosis.

**Best Practice Advice 6:** Clinicians should query patients routinely regarding alcohol consumption patterns in all patients with lean NAFLD.

**Best Practice Advice 7:** In patients with lean NAFLD, other causes of liver disease should be ruled out, including other causes of fatty liver, such as HIV, lipodystrophy, lysosomal acid lipase deficiency, familial hypobetalipoproteinemia, and medication-induced hepatic steatosis (methotrexate, amiodarone, tamoxifen, and steroids).

**Best Practice Advice 8:** Current evidence is inadequate to support routine testing for genetic variants in patients with lean NAFLD.

**Best Practice Advice 9:** Liver biopsy, as the reference standard, should be considered if there is uncertainty regarding contributing causes of liver injury and/or the stage of liver fibrosis.

**Best Practice Advice 10:** Serum indices (NAFLD fibrosis score and Fibrosis-4 score [FIB-4]) and imaging techniques (transient elastography [TE] and magnetic resonance elastography [MRE]) may be used as alternatives to liver biopsy for fibrosis staging and patient follow-up. These tests can be performed at the time of diagnosis and repeated at intervals of 6 months to 2 years, depending on fibrosis stage and the patient's response to intervention.

**Best Practice Advice 11:** If noninvasive tests (eg, FIB-4 and NAFLD fibrosis score) are indeterminate, a second noninvasive test (eg, TE or MRE) should be performed to confirm the stage and prognosis of NAFLD.

**Best Practice Advice 12:** In lean patients with NAFLD, lifestyle intervention, including exercise, diet modification, and avoidance of fructose- and sugar-sweetened drinks, to target a modest weight loss of 3%–5% is suggested.

**Best Practice Advice 13:** Administration of vitamin E may be considered in lean persons with biopsy-confirmed nonalcoholic steatohepatitis but without type 2 diabetes mellitus or cirrhosis. Oral pioglitazone 30 mg daily may be considered in lean persons with biopsy-confirmed nonalcoholic steatohepatitis without cirrhosis.

**Best Practice Advice 14:** The therapeutic role of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors in the management of lean NAFLD is not fully defined and requires further investigation.

**Best Practice Advice 15:** Hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance with abdominal ultrasound with or without serum α-fetoprotein twice per year is suggested in patients with lean NAFLD and clinical markers compatible with liver cirrhosis.

The only differences are expansions of abbreviations (e.g. "type 2 diabetes mellitus" in the abstract vs "T2DM" in the body).

## Key Findings / Claims

### Definition and epidemiology
- **Lean NAFLD = NAFLD + BMI <25 kg/m² (non-Asian race) or <23 kg/m² (Asian race)** (BPA 1).
- Reference BMI bands used to derive this: CDC and WHO define normal-range BMI as **18.5–24.9 kg/m²**; **25–29.9 kg/m²** = overweight; **30–34.9 kg/m²** = grade 1 obesity. WHO recommends a lower cutoff for those of Asian ancestry — **BMI 23–27.5 kg/m² for overweight and BMI >27.5 kg/m² for obesity** — recognizing metabolic risk at a lower BMI.
- The update prefers the term **lean NAFLD** (normal-range BMI with race-based cutoffs) over *nonobese NAFLD*, though the terms are often used interchangeably; it focuses on lean patients as defined by normal BMI.
- **7%–20% of individuals with NAFLD have lean body habitus.** Global NAFLD/NASH Registry: ~**6.8%** of patients with confirmed NASH are lean (older, more often Asian, fewer metabolic syndrome components, but a similar risk for advanced fibrosis). NHANES III: **10.8%** of lean individuals had evidence of NAFLD (older, more frequently men).
- If 10%–20% of individuals with NAFLD are lean, this is **8–10 million US adults**.

### Natural history — lean is not benign
- Lean patients with NAFLD have a **lower** prevalence of advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis cross-sectionally than overweight/obese patients with NAFLD.
- But in a small longitudinal study of biopsy-confirmed NAFLD followed a median **8.4 years**, lean participants had a **higher risk of liver-related death** than overweight/obese participants (lean had higher baseline advanced fibrosis in that study).
- In >**1300** subjects with biopsy-confirmed NAFLD from Europe and Australia, lean NAFLD had **less severe histologic disease**; despite this, over ~**7.5 years** of follow-up **nearly 5%** of those with lean NAFLD reported liver-related events, even though the vast majority maintained a lean BMI.
- Population-based Swedish registry with nearly **20 years** of follow-up: lean NAFLD had **lower stages of fibrosis at baseline but a higher risk of developing severe liver disease** than nonlean NAFLD.
- Conclusion: lean NAFLD is at risk for progressive liver disease independent of weight gain and **should not be classified as a benign phenotype**.

### Cardiometabolic risk (BPA 2)
- Lean NAFLD shows altered bile salt metabolism, with pathologic changes in cholesterol metabolism and liver fat.
- Genetic differences reported, including the ***TM6SF2* rs58542926 (T) allele**, which protects against diet-associated obesity relative to nonlean NAFLD.
- Observational data are mixed: some studies show lean NAFLD has a *lower* proportion of cardiometabolic risk factors (hypertension, T2DM, metabolic syndrome) and less atherosclerotic disease than nonlean NAFLD; others show a **similar or higher prevalence of multiple cardiometabolic risk factors and risk scores, and more cardiovascular events than overweight and obese persons with NAFLD**.
- Advice: evaluate and treat modifiable cardiovascular risk factors — **diabetes, dyslipidemia, and hypertension**.

### Screening (BPA 4, 5)
- **Do not screen the lean general population** — NAFLD prevalence among lean individuals is relatively low.
- **Do screen (and then risk-stratify for advanced fibrosis) lean persons >40 years with T2DM.**
- Society positions cited: EASL/EASD/EASO recommend screening all patients with obesity or metabolic syndrome; ADA recommends evaluation for NASH and hepatic fibrosis in persons with T2DM and elevated liver biochemical tests or fatty liver on ultrasound; **AASLD does not endorse NAFLD screening** and cites limited cost-effectiveness in T2DM.
- Consider NAFLD across the BMI range in anyone with multiple cardiometabolic risk factors, elevated liver biochemical tests, or incidentally noted hepatic steatosis — **the initial diagnostic approach is the same for lean and nonlean patients**. Elevated liver biochemical tests get the standard evaluation, including for drug-induced liver injury and chronic liver diseases.

### Alcohol (BPA 6)
- The conventional threshold separating alcohol-related liver disease from NAFLD is intake of **more than 14 drinks per week for women or 21 drinks per week for men**.
- **Alcohol below this threshold still contributes to liver fat.** Higher average weekly alcohol use below NAFLD-consistent thresholds was associated with a higher prevalence of CT-defined NAFLD; the **number of drinking days per week, maximum drinks in 24 hours, and binge drinking behavior** were each associated with increased odds of NAFLD.
- Underreported alcohol use likely contributes to misdiagnosis of NAFLD in lean individuals. Sensitive alcohol biomarkers to exclude alcohol overuse:
  - **Urine ethyl glucuronide** — detection window **3–5 days**
  - **Blood phosphatidylethanol** — detection window **1–2 weeks**

### Secondary causes of fatty liver (BPA 7) — Table 1
*Full table recreated on [[lean-nafld]].* Categories: specific liver conditions (chronic hepatitis C, especially genotype 3; Wilson's disease; A1 antitrypsin; liver diseases of pregnancy; drug-induced liver injury), endocrine disorders, other genetic disorders, environmental toxins, and nutritional effects. Causes of lean NAFLD itself include diet (high-fructose, high-fat), changes in fat distribution (visceral obesity), and changes in body composition (lipodystrophy in HIV and non-HIV persons).

### Genetic testing (BPA 8)
- In up to **75%** of cases, variable hepatic fat accumulation has been related to inherited factors.
- Most common variants related to NAFLD and its severity: ***PNPLA3* I148M, *TM6SF2* E167K, *MBOAT7*, *GCKR*, and *HSD17B13***. *PNPLA3* and *TM6SF2* associate with severity of steatosis, steatohepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and risk of HCC and mortality; *HSD17B13* is linked to robust protection against liver inflammation, cirrhosis, HCC, and mortality.
- Polygenic risk scores are early-stage, **not advised for clinical use**; given the paucity of data on genetic variants in lean NAFLD, routine genotyping is **not advised**.

### Liver biopsy (BPA 9)
- Reference standard for identifying NASH and staging fibrosis, but invasive, with sampling error and intra-/interobserver variability.
- Standard practice: a tissue sample of **≥2 cm in length** is preferred to allow an accurate reading.
- **Brunt criteria and Kleiner score** are used to assess NASH categorically for disease activity and fibrosis.
- Consider biopsy in lean patients when other causes cannot be excluded through routine testing, and to help consider rarer causes of lean NAFLD.

### Noninvasive tests and the staging algorithm (BPA 10, 11) — Figure 1
- NIT categories: clinical/laboratory risk scores (FIB-4, NAFLD fibrosis score [NFS]); imaging measures of liver stiffness (TE most frequently used; 2-dimensional shear wave elastography and MRE also used); blood-based fibrosis biomarkers (Enhanced Liver Fibrosis [ELF] test).
- In a multicenter study of **709** participants with NAFLD (**11%** lean), AUROCs for identifying advanced fibrosis were generally **higher for most NITs among lean compared with obese participants**.
- **FIB-4 and NFS performed similarly in lean and obese patients** — important because BMI is a component of the NFS calculation. NITs had **high negative predictive values**, demonstrating usefulness at ruling out advanced fibrosis. **Vibration-controlled TE has similar performance in lean and obese patients.**
- MRE is thought to be **less affected by BMI or body habitus**; MRE should be considered when available as a confirmatory test for fibrosis assessment.
- **ELF** = N-terminal propeptide of type III procollagen + hyaluronic acid + tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-1. Meta-analysis: **low cutoff 7.7 → sensitivity 0.93 (95% CI, 0.82–0.98) for excluding fibrosis**; **high cutoff 9.80 → specificity 0.86 (95% CI, 0.77–0.92) for diagnosing fibrosis**. ELF has **not been tested in patients with lean NAFLD**; the update concludes it may be used as a confirmatory prognostic test in lean NAFLD until further data are available.
- **Sequential testing** (2 serologic tests, or a serologic test combined with an imaging test) minimizes indeterminate cases and improves diagnostic accuracy. FIB-4 followed by TE in primary care led to a **nearly 7-fold increased diagnostic rate** for identifying advanced fibrosis; meta-analysis of >**5700** patients showed sequential FIB-4 + TE improved sensitivity and specificity to rule in or rule out advanced fibrosis. A **FIB-4 + ELF** care pathway reduced referral of patients with mild disease.
- "At-risk NASH" composite tests (NASH with F2 or greater fibrosis), none yet evaluated in lean NAFLD:
  - **FAST** (FibroScan–aspartate aminotransferase: CAP + liver stiffness on TE + AST) — **AUROC between 0.8 and 0.85**
  - **MAST** (MR imaging–aspartate aminotransferase: MRI-PDFF + MRE liver stiffness + AST) — **AUROC 0.93 derivation, 0.86 validation**
  - **NIS-4** (blood-based panel: miR-34a-5p, α-2 macroglobulin, YKL-40, glycated hemoglobin) — **AUROC 0.80 (95% CI, 0.73–0.85)** in the discovery cohort
- **Longitudinal NIT monitoring interval:** every **6 months to 1 year** in patients with **F2 or greater** fibrosis; every **1–2 years** in those with **F0 or F1**. (BPA 10 frames the same interval as "6 months to 2 years, depending on fibrosis stage and the patient's response to intervention.")

### Lifestyle (BPA 12)
- **Target a modest weight loss of 3%–5%** in lean persons with NAFLD — lower than the target for overweight/obese NAFLD.
- Large longitudinal study of >**2000** lean adults with NAFLD: weight reduction over a median follow-up of **3 years** was associated with NAFLD resolution in a **dose-dependent manner**.
- RCT from Asia, **12-month** lifestyle intervention in nonobese and obese patients: MR-spectroscopy liver fat improved more in the intervention group regardless of obesity status; **almost one-half of nonobese individuals achieved NAFLD remission with 3%–5% weight loss, the same proportion reached in obese individuals with 7%–10% weight loss.** At up to **6 years** of follow-up, nonobese patients in the lifestyle intervention group were more likely to maintain weight loss and ALT normalization.
- Physical activity — aerobic and anaerobic exercise — reduced liver fat with other metabolic benefits **independent of weight loss**.
- **Fructose:** high fructose consumption is a known risk factor for NAFLD and NASH, especially in children and adolescents. In young, nonobese individuals without metabolic risk factors, higher juice and soft-drink intake was the **single independent factor** for detection of NAFLD, contributing up to a **4-fold increased risk** of NAFLD vs those consuming fewer sugar-sweetened beverages. Limit fructose intake, particularly in younger, lean patients with NAFLD.
- Exercise, increasing physical activity, and decreasing visceral fat are beneficial. The **type of diet in lean NAFLD should be studied further**; patients may benefit from limiting fructose- and sugar-sweetened beverages.
- AASLD 2017 quoted position: "Weight loss generally reduces hepatic steatosis, achieved either by hypocaloric diet alone or in conjunction with increased physical activity."

### Pharmacotherapy (BPA 13, 14)
- Most drug development has enrolled overweight and obese NASH patients; **many trials exclude lean patients**, so evidence on effective pharmacotherapy in lean patients with NASH remains inadequate.
- **Vitamin E** — limited to patients **without T2DM**; recommended dose **800 IU daily** has shown improved histology in patients with NASH. Concerns raised about potential increase in **all-cause mortality, hemorrhagic stroke, and prostate cancer**.
- **Pioglitazone 30 mg daily** — PIVENS compared pioglitazone, vitamin E, and placebo in NASH **without T2DM**, reporting improvement in liver biochemistry, inflammation, and fibrosis in the vitamin E and pioglitazone arms; subsequent studies confirmed benefit for NASH histology in persons **with or without T2DM**. Adverse reactions: **weight gain, peripheral edema, heart failure, and fractures** — PPAR-γ agonist use requires individualized assessment.
- Both vitamin E and pioglitazone are endorsed as first-line treatment options for NASH by AASLD and EASL; use of either **should be restricted to patients with biopsy-confirmed NASH**.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors** are promising for NASH, but ongoing GLP-1 trials are mostly enriched with overweight and obese patients. Until further data are available, their use is **premature for the treatment of lean NASH**, but they may be considered in the management of comorbid metabolic conditions such as T2DM.

### HCC surveillance (BPA 15)
- Cirrhosis, whether secondary to NAFLD or other causes, is a well-established risk factor for incident HCC.
- AASLD and most guidelines recommend considering HCC screening in **all patients with cirrhosis**.
- **Abdominal ultrasound with or without serum α-fetoprotein, twice per year**, should be offered to all patients with cirrhosis including lean patients with NAFLD-related cirrhosis.
- Consider patient age, overall health status and comorbidities, functional status, and personal preferences for screening and willingness to undergo treatment should HCC be diagnosed.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **BMI cut-point and comparator vs [[aga-2021-lifestyle-modification-nafld]].** The 2021 lifestyle update's body text defines "normal-weight NAFLD" as **BMI ≤25 kg/m² (non-Asian) / ≤23 kg/m² (Asian)** and its abstract box prints **26 / 24 kg/m²**. This 2022 update defines **lean NAFLD** as **BMI <25 / <23 kg/m²**, consistently in both its abstract box and body text. Both are AGA Clinical Practice Updates (tier 1); **2022 is newer, so the strict-inequality <25 / <23 definition governs** the wiki. The practical divergence is confined to a BMI of exactly 25.0 (or 23.0).
- **Prevalence of the lean phenotype.** [[aga-2021-lifestyle-modification-nafld]] states that **up to 40%** of the NAFLD population is of normal weight (4%–10% of the general population). This 2022 update states **7%–20%** of individuals with NAFLD are lean, with registry figures of ~6.8% (biopsy-confirmed NASH) and 10.8% (NHANES III lean individuals with NAFLD). The newer figure governs; the 2021 figure is retained here for provenance only.
- **Internal variation within this update.** The introduction gives the lean proportion as **7%–20%**; the BPA 4 discussion uses "approximately **10%–20%**" when deriving the 8–10 million US adults figure. Both are printed in the document.
- **Whether to screen at all.** This update explicitly notes that **AASLD does not endorse NAFLD screening**, whereas EASL/EASD/EASO recommend screening all patients with obesity or metabolic syndrome and ADA recommends evaluation in T2DM with abnormal liver tests or fatty liver. The AGA position taken here is the narrow middle: no general-population screening in lean individuals, but screening those **>40 years with T2DM**.
- **Fibrosis thresholds differ from [[aasld-2023-nafld]].** The Figure 1 algorithm here uses **FIB-4 ≥2.67** to refer and **VCTE ≥8.6 kPa / ELF ≥9.8** as the second-test rule-in. AASLD 2023 uses **VCTE <8 to rule out, 8–12 intermediate, ≥12 to rule in**, and the same ELF ≥9.8 rule-in. The 8.6 kPa cutoff is specific to this lean-NAFLD sequential pathway. AASLD 2023 is newer and is what [[nafld-masld]] asserts generally; the lean-specific pathway is stated on [[lean-nafld]].
- **Nomenclature.** This update predates the 2023 MASLD/MASH nomenclature change ([[aasld-2023-masld-nomenclature]]) and uses NAFLD/NASH throughout. Given >99% overlap between NAFLD- and MASLD-defined populations, its advice applies to lean MASLD.
- **Untested in the lean population.** The ELF test, the at-risk-NASH composites (FAST, MAST, NIS-4), and MRE-based studies have not been specifically evaluated in lean NAFLD; the update extends them on the basis of general-NAFLD performance. Pharmacotherapy evidence in lean NASH is inadequate because trials largely excluded lean patients.
