## Summary

The British Society of Gastroenterology (BSG) update of its 2008 common bile duct stone (CBDS) guideline, covering six questions: when to investigate and treat, how to identify stones, how to improve success and reduce risk at endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), the role of surgery, what to do when stones are difficult, and management in the commonest clinical settings. It is the document that **defines "difficult stone disease"** — a definition no United States (US) guideline in this wiki supplies — and the one that prints **diagnostic performance of ultrasound and liver function tests by pre-test probability**, so the probability strata that US guidance asserts can be read off measured numbers.

Each recommendation is labelled with its provenance: `2008` (unchanged, no new evidence), `2008, amended 2016` (wording changed in a way that changes meaning), `2016` (new evidence, recommendation unchanged) and `New 2016` (new or amended on new evidence). Obsolete 2008 recommendations are omitted. Evidence is graded High / Moderate / Low / Very low and recommendations Strong / Weak, using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) principles. ⚠ The group states explicitly that **where evidence was weak but consensus was clear, it still made a strong recommendation** — so strength here does not track quality as mechanically as in some documents.

Its therapeutic centre of gravity differs from the American documents in one respect worth naming: **laparoscopic bile duct exploration (LBDE) and perioperative ERCP are presented as equally valid** (High quality, strong), with LBDE carrying a shorter hospital stay, and training of surgeons in LBDE is encouraged for its own sake.

### "Difficult stone disease" — the definition
- **Determinants of a difficult extraction:** in most situations **size, shape and number** of stones decide whether extraction is easy. **Altered anatomy from previous surgery** independently reduces the likelihood of success.
- **The operative definition:** where **standard extraction supplemented by mechanical lithotripsy, EPBD with prior sphincterotomy and cholangioscopy** — or extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy where available — **fails to remove the stones**, the patient **can be considered to have difficult stone disease**.
- ⚠ **This sits further down the ladder than the American usage.** [[asge-2015-ercp-benign-biliary|ASGE 2015]] calls a stone "difficult" when it is not removable after sphincterotomy ± balloon dilation or mechanical lithotripsy — i.e. *before* cholangioscopy. For BSG, cholangioscopic lithotripsy must also have failed. The sequel differs accordingly: for BSG, "difficult stone disease" is the trigger for **percutaneous extraction or open duct exploration**.
- **Percutaneous extraction** is usually via a transhepatic (less often transcholecystic) biliary fistula, typically with sphincter balloon dilation and antegrade push of stones into the duodenum, with lithotripsy for larger calculi. **Major complications 3.6%–6.8%** in two recent large series.

- Ultrasound "positive" was variously defined across studies as **echogenic material in the common bile duct (CBD) *or* CBD dilatation**.
- The document notes the **average adult alkaline phosphatase is 50–170 IU/L**, so the >125 IU/L cut-point sits inside the reference range — which is why its specificity is only 0.79.
- **Clinicians use LFTs and USS together, after taking pre-test probability from the history** — likely more effective than any single parameter in isolation.
- **MRCP vs EUS:** at a median pre-test probability of **0.41**, Cochrane summary **sensitivity EUS 0.95 vs MRCP 0.93**; **specificity EUS 0.97 vs MRCP 0.96**. Head-to-head studies in the same patients show **no clear superiority of either**. ⚠ These figures **do not transfer to low pre-test probability** (more false positives) or to high pre-test probability (watch for false negatives).
- **Factors favouring EUS:** intracranial metallic clips, cardiac pacemaker, mechanical heart valve, claustrophobia, morbid obesity. **Factors favouring MRCP:** wide availability, non-invasive, images the intrahepatic ducts, cost-effectiveness, altered gastric/duodenal anatomy, and all images are captured for later review.
- **Computed tomography (CT):** not routinely used to detect CBDS. Standard contrast CT on modern multislice scanners achieves **sensitivity 69%–87%, specificity 68%–96%**, falling considerably for small stones or stones of similar density to bile. **CT cholangiography** is accurate but its contrast agent **has been unavailable in the United Kingdom since 2009**. CT remains appropriate where **CBDS and malignancy coexist**.

### Natural history and the case for extraction
- CBDS present in **10%–20%** of patients with symptomatic gallstones; **<5%** where there is no clinical suspicion before surgery. **2%–4%** of people with gall bladder stones become symptomatic per year.
- **Primary** ductal stones form de novo in the ducts (most prevalent in Asian populations; cause recurrent pyogenic cholangitis). **Secondary** stones migrate from the gall bladder and account for the majority in European patients — the guideline is about secondary stones.
- **GallRiks national cohort:** 34,200 IOCs, **11.6% had ≥1 CBDS**. Of 3,828 with follow-up, 15.5% were managed conservatively. Unfavourable outcome (pancreatitis, cholangitis, biliary obstruction within 30 days of surgery, or later symptoms with proven CBDS at ERCP) occurred in **25.3% left in situ vs 12.7% scheduled for extraction** (odds ratio [OR] 0.44, 95% CI 0.35–0.55).
- ⚠ **The benefit persisted for stones <4 mm** — 8.9% with planned extraction vs 15.9% conservative (OR 0.52, 0.34–0.79). Small is not the same as harmless.
- **Counterweight the document itself prints:** some patients pass stones spontaneously before or after cholecystectomy, and trials of selective IOC report low CBDS-related complication rates in patients never cholangiogrammed. **There are no controlled studies of the natural history of incidentally found CBDS in asymptomatic patients** — the advice to extract in that setting rests on symptomatic-patient evidence plus expert opinion, and patients should be told so.

### ERCP technique and risk
- **Sedation:** **14% of ERCPs under conscious sedation are poorly tolerated** — an important cause of therapeutic failure. The national BSG audit found **33% of patients received >5.5 mg midazolam** and **~8% required a reversal agent** (flumazenil or naloxone).
- **PEP:** reported frequency ranges from <1% to >20%, with **2%–5% commonly quoted**. **ERCP for bile duct stones carries no inherent excess PEP risk** over therapeutic ERCP generally. The only certain way to avoid PEP is to avoid ERCP — hence making the diagnosis with USS, EUS or MRCP first.
- **Pancreatic stent:** avoid pancreatic duct cannulation and contrast filling wherever possible. If pancreatic duct cannulation repeatedly occurs (**e.g. more than one pancreatic wire passage**) while seeking biliary access, consider a **5F pancreatic stent** — it may both facilitate biliary access and reduce PEP. ⚠ **Failed attempts at stent placement may dramatically increase PEP risk**, so the technique requires training. Optimum duration unknown, likely hours to days; **reassess with a plain abdominal x-ray to confirm spontaneous migration**, and remove endoscopically if it has not migrated. **With universal rectal NSAIDs, the additive benefit of pancreatic stenting is uncertain.**
- **Antibiotics:** absent specific sepsis risk factors — sclerosing cholangitis, communicating pancreatic cysts, hilar strictures, liver transplantation, cholangioscopy, or a failed attempt to drain an opacified duct — **prophylactic antibiotics can safely be avoided**.
- **Coagulopathy:** the point at which clotting abnormalities become an **absolute** contraindication to sphincterotomy **cannot be asserted from the evidence**. Attempt correction (including severe thrombocytopenia) first; if not possible, **start with a lower-bleeding-risk procedure such as stenting**. For anticoagulants, the BSG/ESGE algorithms apply — stenting alone: continue warfarin, omit DOACs on the morning; elective sphincterotomy: stop oral anticoagulation **2–5 days** beforehand depending on drug and renal function, bridging only for a high-risk condition on warfarin; clopidogrel for a high-risk cardiac condition → liaise with cardiology first.
- **EPBD with prior sphincterotomy (large stones):** balloons **>10 mm** are usual; **do not dilate the sphincter beyond the diameter of the bile duct above it**, and **most practitioners advise caution beyond 18 mm**. Reduces the need for mechanical lithotripsy and may lower overall complications versus sphincterotomy alone.
- **EPBD without prior sphincterotomy:** **8 mm balloon regardless of CBD diameter** in most studies, with **longer dilation (>1 min up to 5 min) reported as the safest technique**. Similar success and overall complications to sphincterotomy for **small (<8 mm)** stones, and meta-analysis suggests **lower relative risk of cholecystitis and recurrent CBDS**. ⚠ **Accepted contraindications:** biliary stricture or malignancy, previous biliary surgery other than cholecystectomy, cholangitis, pancreatitis, prior access papillotomy, and **large CBDS (usually defined as >12 mm)**. Reported success rates in meta-analyses **included patients who crossed over to rescue sphincterotomy**.
- **Cholangioscopy-guided lithotripsy:** after failure of mechanical lithotripsy and EPBD-with-sphincterotomy, single-operator cholangioscopy with EHL or LL clears **73%–97%**. **Cholangitis in up to 9%**, which is why prophylactic antibiotics are used; other complications comparable to conventional ERCP. **In direct peroral cholangioscopy the air or carbon dioxide (CO₂) supply is switched off during cholangioscopy to reduce the risk of gas embolism.**
- **Access papillotomy** (formerly precut / needle-knife papillotomy): the 2008 restriction to experts is replaced — most clinicians performing ERCP will want to use it in selected cases, so training should make every ERCP endoscopist able to **recognise when it is indicated and perform it safely**.

### Surgery
- **Over 95% of gall bladders are removed laparoscopically**; only about **20% of bile duct explorations** are currently done laparoscopically, and the UK IOC rate is around **10%**.
- **Bile duct exploration should always use a choledochoscope** unless no alternative exists — blind instrumentation risks perforation and later stricture.
- **Transcystic vs transductal:** transcystic is more limited (small stones only, poor access to the common hepatic duct), so **most surgeons use the transductal approach**. Clearance rates approach **100%** with intraductal piezoelectric or laser lithotripsy available.
- **Primary duct closure beats planned T-tube insertion** — shorter stay, similar bile leaks and recurrent stones, shorter operative time, and return to work about **8 days** sooner. T-tube morbidity includes **10–14 days** with a tube through the abdominal wall, inadvertent early removal with bile leak and peritonitis, and leaks after planned removal.
- Complications of surgical exploration relate mainly to the **choledochotomy (bile leak)** and **T-tube** use; **pancreatitis is rare unless the papilla has been instrumented antegrade**.

### Stents, gall bladder status and specific settings
- **Stent as definitive treatment:** compares favourably with stone extraction for immediate success and complications in patients **>70 years or with debilitating disease**, but **at least a quarter develop recurrent cholangitis** during follow-up; results are better in patients without a gall bladder. In a long-term-stent series (mean follow-up 14 months): **on-demand exchange → 36% cholangitis, 8% mortality**; **elective 3-monthly exchange → 8% cholangitis, 2% mortality**.
- **Cholecystectomy after duct clearance:** Cochrane meta-analysis of 5 trials (662 participants) — **mortality 14.1% (wait and see) vs 7.9% (prophylactic cholecystectomy)**, relative risk 1.78 (95% CI 1.15–2.75), with recurrent pain, jaundice and cholangitis also more common in the wait-and-see group; benefit persisted in higher-surgical-risk patients (American Society of Anesthesiologists score 4–5). A later trial in **patients over 70 with coexisting gall bladder stones** found significantly fewer total biliary events with elective cholecystectomy.
- ⚠ **Empty gall bladder is a different question.** Large Asian series report recurrent CBDS in **15%–23.7%** with residual gall bladder stones versus **5.9%–11.3%** with an empty gall bladder in situ; smaller Asian and European studies did not confirm the difference. Several reports suggest an empty gall bladder carries lower risk of cholecystitis and later cholecystectomy — so **a wait-and-see approach may reasonably be discussed** for an empty gall bladder after duct clearance.
- **Cholangitis timing:** high-quality data on optimal ERCP timing are lacking, but early intervention is likely beneficial. In a prospective study of 199 admissions, **each day of ERCP delay increased length of stay by 1.44 days (95% CI 1.01–1.92)**, and **ERCP performed >72 hours after presentation was associated with increased vasopressor requirement**. For septic shock or deterioration despite appropriate antibiotics, decompression may be needed **within 24 hours**.
- **Gallstone pancreatitis:** Cochrane review found **no effect of early routine sphincterotomy ± stone extraction on mortality or complications regardless of severity**, but **supported** it in patients with **cholangitis or biliary obstruction**. Studies vary in what "early" means (<24 h to <72 h) and **there is no evidence supporting <24 h over <72 h** — no study was designed to answer it. Within the 72-hour group, **severe sepsis may still warrant ERCP within 24 hours**. Conversely, **jaundice without sepsis often resolves over 24–72 hours**, and early ERCP can then be avoided in favour of MRCP/EUS/IOC/LUS to exclude a retained stone.
- **Sphincterotomy is not equivalent to cholecystectomy** after gallstone pancreatitis: it significantly reduces recurrent pancreatitis but is **less effective than cholecystectomy**, particularly for preventing other biliary complications. **The greatest risk reduction comes from both.** There is **insufficient evidence to recommend routine sphincterotomy for everyone awaiting cholecystectomy** after mild gallstone pancreatitis.
- **Billroth II:** the papilla is approached from the afferent limb in an **"upside-down" (5 o'clock) orientation**, which requires altered sphincterotomy technique — sphincterotomes modified to change cutting-wire orientation, or rotatable conventional sphincterotomes. Where safe orientation cannot be achieved, **needle-knife sphincterotomy over a straight plastic stent** has **equal efficacy to EPBD**; a combination of **limited needle-knife sphincterotomy over a stent or guidewire followed by EPBD and conventional extraction** is becoming the expert-preferred method. The single randomised trial comparing scopes found **forward-viewing endoscopes more successful (87% vs 68%)** and duodenoscopes associated with an **18% visceral perforation rate** — ⚠ **not confirmed elsewhere**, where perforation rates of **2.7%–10%** are reported. A **duodenoscope-first, forward-viewing-scope-on-failure** strategy is reasonable.
- **Roux-en-Y gastric bypass:** the long afferent limb means per-oral retrograde access needs **single- or double-balloon enteroscopes or a spiral overtube**, with narrower, longer working channels limiting accessories. A series of 129 Roux-en-Y reconstructions (63 post-RYGB with intact papilla) achieved **ERCP success in 63% with a 12% complication rate**, with **no difference between enteroscopic techniques**. **Laparoscopy-assisted ERCP** through a laparoscopic gastrostomy achieved **100% therapeutic success vs 59% for long-limb enteroscopy** in a 56-patient comparison, with no difference in stay or complications — "probably the best option currently" and deliverable by most larger secondary-care units. EUS-guided gastric-remnant puncture with a lumen-apposing metal stent is described in small series only.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Gallstone pancreatitis, ERCP window.** BSG 2017 sets **within 72 hours** for biliary pancreatitis with cholangitis or persistent obstruction and says there is no evidence for <24 h over <72 h. [[asge-2019-choledocholithiasis|ASGE 2019]] recommends **against urgent (<48 h) ERCP** in gallstone pancreatitis *without* cholangitis or obstruction. **These are compatible** — different populations — and the page follows ASGE 2019 as the newer same-tier document where they overlap.
- **Cholecystectomy after mild gallstone pancreatitis.** BSG 2017 allows **within 2 weeks of presentation, preferably the same admission** (Moderate; weak). [[asge-2019-choledocholithiasis|ASGE 2019]] and [[aga-2018-initial-management-acute-pancreatitis|AGA 2018]] recommend **same-admission** cholecystectomy (the latter strong, on PONCHO). **The newer and stronger recommendation governs the wiki**; BSG's 2-week latitude is recorded here only.
- **Cholangitis drainage window.** BSG 2017 gives no general numeric window, only "early is likely beneficial" plus **within 24 hours for septic shock or antibiotic failure**. [[asge-2021-cholangitis]] sets **48 hours** generally, with <24 h only "may be considered" in fluid-refractory septic shock. **No conflict**; BSG's data are carried as support.
- **What counts as a "difficult" stone.** BSG 2017 places the label *after* cholangioscopy has failed; [[asge-2015-ercp-benign-biliary|ASGE 2015]] uses it *before* cholangioscopy. Both usages are now printed on [[cholangioscopy]] rather than one being adopted silently.
- **Billroth II first scope.** BSG 2017 suggests **duodenoscope first, forward-viewing endoscope on failure**. [[asge-2024-therapeutic-eus-biliary|ASGE 2024]] suggests **enteroscopy-assisted ERCP as the initial approach** for non–gastric-bypass altered anatomy including Billroth II. **ASGE 2024 governs [[ercp]]** as the newer same-tier document; BSG's scope-choice data and the sphincterotomy-orientation technique are kept as technique detail, which ASGE 2024 does not cover.
- **Open in the document's own words:** no randomised comparison of MRCP vs EUS exists; the natural history of untreated asymptomatic CBDS is unstudied; the harm/benefit of routine IOC in low-risk patients is unresolved; the role of pancreatic stenting in patients already receiving rectal NSAIDs is unclear; and the optimum algorithm for removing large CBDS is undefined.
- **Non-US guideline.** BSG recommendations reference UK service structures (BSG national ERCP standards framework, BSG/ESGE anticoagulation guidelines, UK unavailability of CT-cholangiography contrast since 2009). Those are recorded here and not asserted as US practice on entity pages.
