## Summary

The wiki's first tier-1 source devoted to **malignant alimentary tract obstruction (MATO)** as a subject in its own right, rather than as a complication section inside a cancer page. It covers the three anatomic sites where malignant blockage is common — **distal esophagus/gastric cardia, gastric outlet, and colon** — and issues **14 Best Practice Advice statements**.

The organizing principle is stated explicitly in the conclusion: there is *"no longer a 'one-size-fits-all' approach."* Every decision is made in a **multidisciplinary setting** (BPA 1) and turns on three inputs — **resectability**, **life expectancy / functional status**, and **local endoscopic and surgical expertise**.

The most decision-changing content is the **resectable-vs-not split in the esophagus** and the **life-expectancy threshold in gastric outlet obstruction**. In a patient still a candidate for resection or chemoradiation, a self-expanding metal stent (SEMS) should **not** be placed routinely without multidisciplinary review — the bridge-to-surgery stent carries higher morbidity and mortality and potentially lower R0 resection rates, so enteral feeding is favored instead. In gastric outlet obstruction, the SUSTENT randomized trial produces a usable cutoff: **life expectancy >2 months + good functional status + surgically fit → surgical gastrojejunostomy**; below that, an enteral stent, because the stent's earlier food intake is offset by worse long-term relief and more re-intervention.

In the colon the update is more permissive than older teaching in two specific places. SEMS is *"a reasonable choice as a bridge to surgery"* in resectable malignant colonic obstruction, and — the point most likely to be missed — SEMS is *"a reasonable option"* for **proximal / right-sided** obstruction as well, both as a bridge and palliatively, despite the long-standing concern that right-sided stenting is too technically difficult.

Two negative statements are worth carrying forward: **laser therapy and photodynamic therapy should not be used** for palliation of malignant esophageal obstruction (a Cochrane review of 53 studies / 3684 patients found laser, PDT and plastic stents all less effective with more adverse events than SEMS), and **enteral stents should not be used** when there are multiple luminal obstructions or severely impaired gastric motility (e.g. linitis plastica) — a venting gastrostomy is the alternative there.

## Key Findings / Claims

### The 14 Best Practice Advice statements (verbatim)

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 1:** For all patients with alimentary tract obstruction, the decision about specific interventions should be made in a multidisciplinary setting including oncologists, surgeons, and endoscopists and take into account the characteristics of the obstruction, patient's expectations, prognosis, expected subsequent therapies, and functional status.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 2:** For patients who present with esophageal obstruction from esophageal cancer and who are potential candidates for resection or chemoradiation, clinicians should not routinely insert a self-expanding metal stent (SEMS) without multidisciplinary review because of high rates of stent migration, higher morbidity and mortality, and potentially lower R0 (microscopically negative margins) resection rates.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 3:** For patients who present with esophageal obstruction from esophageal cancer who are potential candidates for resection and who have concerns of malnutrition, clinicians may consider the use of enteral feeding tubes (via nasogastric or percutaneous route). Clinicians should be aware of the potential risk of abdominal wall tumor seeding as well as making subsequent gastric conduit formation difficult with percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy placement.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 4:** For patients who present with esophageal obstruction from esophageal cancer who are not candidates for resection, clinicians should consider either SEMS insertion or brachytherapy as sole therapy or in combination. Clinicians should not consider the use of laser therapy or photodynamic therapy because of the lack of evidence of better outcomes and superior alternatives.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 5:** For patients with malignant esophageal obstruction who are undergoing SEMS placement, clinicians should use a fully covered or partially covered SEMS and not an uncovered SEMS, with consideration of a stent-anchoring/fixation method.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 6:** For patients with gastric outlet obstruction who have a life expectancy greater than 2 months, have good functional status, and who are surgically fit, surgical gastrojejunostomy should be considered.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 7:** For patients with gastric outlet obstruction who are undergoing surgical gastrojejunostomy, a laparoscopic approach is favored over an open approach because of lower blood loss and shorter hospital stay.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 8:** For patients with gastric outlet obstruction who are not candidates for gastrojejunostomy (surgical or endoscopic ultrasound-guided), clinicians should consider the insertion of an enteral stent.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 9:** Enteral stents should not be used in patients with multiple luminal obstructions or severely impaired gastric motility because of the limited benefit in these scenarios. Clinicians can consider placement of a venting gastrostomy in these patients.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 10:** Depending on the experience of the endoscopist, endoscopic ultrasound-guided gastrojejunostomy is an acceptable alternative to surgical gastrojejunostomy and enteral stent placement. Clinicians should be aware that there are currently no dedicated Food and Drug Administration–approved devices for endoscopic ultrasound–guided gastrojejunostomy.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 11:** For patients with malignant colonic obstruction who are candidates for resection, insertion of SEMS is a reasonable choice as a "bridge to surgery" to allow for one-stage, elective resection.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 12:** For patients with malignant colonic obstruction who are not candidates for resection, either SEMS placement or a diverting colostomy are reasonable choices depending on the patient's goals and functional status.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 13:** SEMS is a reasonable option for patients with proximal (or right-sided) malignant obstructions, both as a "bridge to surgery" and in the palliative setting.

> **BEST PRACTICE ADVICE 14:** SEMS placement is a reasonable alternative for patients with extracolonic malignancy who are not candidates for surgery, although their placement is more technically challenging, clinical success rates are more variable, and complications (including stent migration) are more frequent.

### The three decision inputs, by site

| Site | Resectable / operable | Not resectable / not operable |
|---|---|---|
| **Esophagus / gastric cardia** | **Do not** routinely stent without MDT review (BPA 2). Enteral feeding tube if malnourished (BPA 3) | SEMS **or** brachytherapy, alone or combined (BPA 4). **Not** laser, **not** PDT |
| **Gastric outlet** | Life expectancy **>2 months** + good functional status + surgically fit → **surgical GJ**, laparoscopic preferred (BPA 6, 7). EUS-GE an acceptable alternative where expertise exists (BPA 10) | Enteral stent (BPA 8) — **unless** multiple luminal obstructions or severely impaired gastric motility, where a **venting gastrostomy** is used instead (BPA 9) |
| **Colon** | SEMS as **bridge to surgery** for one-stage elective resection (BPA 11); also reasonable for **proximal/right-sided** obstruction (BPA 13) | SEMS **or** diverting colostomy, by patient goals and functional status (BPA 12). Extracolonic malignancy: SEMS reasonable but harder and less reliable (BPA 14) |

### Esophageal obstruction — the data behind BPA 2–5

- **Why not stent a resectable patient.** The seminal study (38 SEMS vs 152 propensity-matched controls) found **higher mortality and morbidity, lower R0 resection rates, shorter time to recurrence, and worse overall survival** with SEMS. A narrative review of the neoadjuvant setting (16 studies, 383 patients): **stent migration 29.9%, chest pain 15.6%, tumor ingrowth 2.2%, esophageal perforation 1.5% (5 patients)**; one study cited **30-day post-insertion mortality of 10%**. European guidelines do not recommend an esophageal stent as a bridge to surgery.
- **Enteral feeding instead (BPA 3).** Options are nasogastric/nasojejunal tubes, percutaneous gastrostomy (endoscopic, surgical or radiologic), gastrostomy with jejunal extension, or percutaneous jejunostomy. Good technical success, tolerability and weight gain; adverse events are tube blockage, dislodgement, infection. ⚠ **Enteral feeds do not improve dysphagia or oral intake.** Abdominal-wall seeding after PEG has been documented but mostly in head-and-neck cancer; PEG can also make later **gastric conduit formation** difficult — discuss the feeding route with the surgeon first.
- **Palliation (BPA 4).** Meta-analysis of 8 RCTs (732 patients): **stent + chemotherapy or radiation** gave better long-term (>5 months) dysphagia relief and **improved overall survival** vs stent alone, with **no difference in short-term (<3 months) dysphagia** and no significant difference in adverse events (though chest pain was common). **Stent alone vs brachytherapy alone:** no difference in dysphagia improvement or overall survival; stent carried **higher hemorrhage risk**, with no difference in fistula or perforation.
- **Modalities not to use.** Cochrane review, 53 studies / 3684 patients: **laser, PDT and plastic stents were all less effective than SEMS and had increased adverse events.** Cryoablation data are small and retrospective.
- **Stent selection (BPA 5).** Uncovered (UCSEMS) vs fully covered (FCSEMS): no difference in initial dysphagia improvement but **more tumor ingrowth and re-intervention with UCSEMS** — hence FCSEMS or PCSEMS. Anchoring options with demonstrated reduction in migration: **endoscopic suturing** and **over-the-scope clip**.
- **Length and diameter are left to the clinician**, guided by stricture severity and length; the general aim is **≥2 cm of margin proximally and distally**. A randomized comparison of **small (16–18 mm) vs large (20–23 mm)** diameter stents: no difference in dysphagia improvement, but **more recurrent dysphagia, tissue overgrowth and food-bolus obstruction with small diameter**, and **more hemorrhage and perforation with large diameter**. Consider larger diameters when the stent crosses the **gastroesophageal junction**, to prevent migration.
- **Reflux is near-universal after esophageal SEMS: reported in 70%–100% of cases.** Anti-reflux valve designs exist but no definitive mechanism has been established.

### Gastric outlet obstruction — the data behind BPA 6–10

- **Causes:** stricturing at the pylorus or duodenum, most often from malignancy of the **pancreas, stomach, duodenum, ampulla, or biliary tree (cholangiocarcinoma)**. Development of GOO *"generally portends an unfavorable prognosis."*
- **SUSTENT (the trial behind the 2-month rule).** Randomized GOO patients to gastrojejunostomy (open or laparoscopic) vs enteral stent. Stent allowed **earlier food intake**, but this was **offset by better long-term relief with surgical GJ**, and stent patients had **higher rates of recurrent obstruction and re-intervention**. Hence: **life expectancy >2 months and surgically fit → surgical GJ; less than that → enteral stent.**
- **Predictors of surgical-GJ clinical failure** (retrospective, 310 patients): **poor nutritional status, ascites, poor functional status.**
- **Delayed gastric emptying after GJ** occurred in **up to 50%** of patients when no intervention was made at the obstruction site; **gastric partitioning** (partial or complete) was developed to address this. Evidence is mixed — a retrospective study found partitioning as safe as conventional GJ with only a trend toward better solid-diet tolerance, while a meta-analysis showed improved overall oral nutrition and gastric emptying.
- **Enteral stent outcomes** (systematic review, 2007): **89% clinical success**; **early adverse events 7%** (migration, jaundice, bleeding); **late adverse events 18%** (mostly migration or occlusion from tumor ingrowth or food); **mean hospital stay 7 days**; **mean overall survival 105 days** after placement. Predictors of poor outcome: poor baseline functional status, peritoneal dissemination, lack of subsequent chemotherapy.
- **Contraindications to enteral stenting (BPA 9), stated explicitly:** multiple luminal obstructions (especially distal to the duodenum), very short life expectancy (**less than a few weeks**), and impaired gastric motility (e.g. **linitis plastica**).
- **Covered vs uncovered enteral SEMS is unsettled.** A 2015 pooled analysis found **higher technical and clinical success with PCSEMS** and more recurrent obstruction from ingrowth with UCSEMS, but **more migration with PCSEMS**; a separate meta-analysis found no difference in technical or clinical success, with **lower occlusion and higher migration** for PCSEMS/FCSEMS.
- ⚠ **Covering the ampulla.** Enteral stent placement raises concern about subsequent [[ercp|ERCP]]; a retrospective series still achieved **85% technical success** for biliary drainage by ERCP after enteral stent placement. Alternatives: percutaneous transhepatic cholangiographic drainage or EUS-guided biliary drainage.
- **EUS-guided gastroenterostomy (EUS-GE).** Creates a fistulous tract between stomach and jejunum. Systematic review/meta-analysis (2020, 12 studies, 285 patients): **technical success 92%, clinical success 90%, adverse events 12%**, most commonly **stent misdeployment**. Versus enteral stent: **lower re-intervention and symptom recurrence** with EUS-GE across retrospective series; versus laparoscopic GJ: similar efficacy with **significantly lower adverse events**. ⚠ Not widely available (expertise-limited), and **no dedicated FDA-approved device exists** (BPA 10).

### Malignant colonic obstruction — the data behind BPA 11–14

- **Bridge to surgery.** Systematic review (21 studies, 407 patients): **technical success 91.9%**, **71.7%** subsequently underwent one-stage surgery, **perforation 1.7% (7 patients)**, **stent migration 3.9% (16 cases)**. Two international registries (182 patients): **technical success 98%, clinical success 94%, overall adverse events 7.8%** (perforation 3%, migration 1.2%).
- **SEMS vs emergency surgery.** Meta-analysis of 7 RCTs (382 patients): SEMS group had **significantly higher rates of primary anastomosis and lower rates of permanent stoma and overall adverse events**, with **no difference in overall survival**. Two further meta-analyses (2017 RCT-only; 2019 RCT + non-RCT) reached similar conclusions.
- ⚠ **The counter-signal.** A retrospective South Korean study found that patients whose SEMS placement was **complicated by perforation** had an **odds ratio of 46** for subsequently developing metastasis from seeding — limited by being single-center, retrospective, small, and having a higher perforation rate than prior meta-analyses.
- **Palliative setting.** European guidelines (2020) recommend SEMS as the **preferred option for left-sided MCO in non-resectable patients**. SEMS vs diverting colostomy: earlier oral intake and shorter hospital stay with SEMS, but a trend favoring colostomy for overall survival and time to readmission; a 2013 meta-analysis (13 studies, 837 patients) found **lower clinical success with SEMS and no mortality difference**, while a 2015 meta-analysis found **no difference in clinical success but significantly fewer early adverse events, less stoma need, and improved overall survival with SEMS — at the cost of higher perforation risk and more late adverse events**.
- ⚠ **Performance status changes the answer.** For **ECOG 0 or 1**, palliative **surgery** had improved overall survival vs SEMS. For **ECOG 2 or 3**, there was **no difference** in overall survival.
- **Right-sided / proximal obstruction (BPA 13).** Concern has been technical difficulty, but studies of proximal colonic obstruction report high technical and clinical success with minimal or no procedure-related adverse events; one comparison in resectable right-sided cancer found SEMS associated with **lower mortality, lower morbidity, and fewer permanent stomas** than emergency surgery.
- **Extracolonic malignancy (ECM, BPA 14).** Mechanism differs — **extrinsic compression, mesenteric infiltration and dysmotility** rather than intraluminal growth. An initial study (15 patients) found **clinical success of only 20%** in ECM with more persistent obstructive symptoms and higher adverse-event rates; two later studies (60 and 44 patients) found **no difference** in technical, clinical or adverse-event rates vs colorectal-cancer obstruction. As a bridge to surgery in 9 ECM patients: **100% technical success, 77.8% went on to elective surgery, no SEMS-related adverse events.**
- **Migration and perforation, colonic.** More recent data including a systematic review/meta-analysis show **UCSEMS have fewer adverse events, longer patency and lower re-intervention rates**, with no difference in technical or clinical success — the reverse of the early studies. Perforation: **bevacizumab has not been associated with higher perforation rates** in recent large retrospective studies (with the caveat that many patients received it outside 6 weeks of stent placement); **sigmoid location and age >70** were associated with **early** perforation, while **flexure location and absence of peritoneal carcinomatosis** were associated with **delayed** perforation.

⚠ **Note the covered-vs-uncovered answer is site-specific and opposite:** in the **esophagus** use **covered** (FCSEMS/PCSEMS, BPA 5); in the **colon**, the more recent data favor **uncovered**. Do not carry one across to the other.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **Covered vs uncovered SEMS points in opposite directions in the esophagus and colon** (see the boxed note above). Both are stated in this one document; it is a site difference, not an internal contradiction, but it is the single easiest thing to get wrong.
- **SEMS vs colostomy in non-resectable colonic obstruction is genuinely unresolved in this source** — the 2013 and 2015 meta-analyses disagree on clinical success and survival, and the ECOG-stratified data flip the answer toward surgery in fit patients. BPA 12 accordingly declines to pick one and defers to patient goals and functional status. **Do not write a wiki sentence that picks a winner here.**
- **Colonic-stent perforation and tumor seeding.** The OR-46 seeding figure after perforation is from one small single-center retrospective study and the CPU explicitly lists its limitations. Report it as a signal, never as an established risk estimate.
- **Gastric partitioning at the time of GJ** — retrospective data show no significant clinical difference, a meta-analysis shows benefit. Unsettled.
- **EUS-GE is expertise- and device-limited.** No dedicated FDA-approved device as of 2021; the 90%+ success figures come from expert centers. Any wiki text must carry that qualifier.
- **Age of the source.** This is a 2021 CPU; if a newer society statement on luminal stenting or EUS-GE is ingested, re-check BPA 10 and BPA 13 against it.
