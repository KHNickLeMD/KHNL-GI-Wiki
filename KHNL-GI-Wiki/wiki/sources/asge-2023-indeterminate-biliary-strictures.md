## Summary

This American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) Standards of Practice Committee guideline provides an evidence-based, Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)-framework approach to the **endoscopic diagnosis of malignancy in [[biliary-stricture|biliary strictures]] of undetermined etiology**. It addresses four endoscopic tissue-acquisition modalities — **fluoroscopic-guided biopsy sampling, [[brush-cytology|brush cytology]], [[cholangioscopy]], and [[endoscopic-ultrasound|endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)]]** with fine-needle aspiration (FNA) or fine-needle biopsy sampling (FNB).

**Terminology (deliberate):** the committee used *undetermined* rather than the historical *indeterminate*. *Indeterminate* has historically been defined as a stricture in which a **prior [[ercp|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]] had inconclusive cytology**; *undetermined* let the evidence base include patients at their **first ERCP without a prior negative brush cytology** — the point being that tissue modalities other than brushing belong in the *initial* workup, not only after a failed one.

The clinical problem is substantial: the risk of malignancy in a biliary stricture without an obvious mass on cross-sectional imaging is approximately 55%, and benign etiologies ([[primary-sclerosing-cholangitis|primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)]], IgG4 subclass 4–related sclerosing cholangitis, fibrotic strictures, [[chronic-pancreatitis|chronic pancreatitis]]) frequently mimic malignant strictures on cross-sectional imaging, so tissue acquisition is required to distinguish them. Endoscopic sampling is preferred over percutaneous approaches (require an external drain, risk needle-track seeding) and surgical approaches. The major limitation of existing endoscopic methods is **low sensitivity** for malignancy, plus the needle-track seeding risk of EUS-guided FNA of hilar strictures. Repeat procedures drive cost, patient anxiety, diagnostic delay, and potential delay of curative treatment.

The guideline answers three clinical questions: (1) should ERCP add fluoroscopic-guided biopsy sampling to brush cytology? (2) should ERCP with cholangioscopic-guided biopsy sampling be performed versus ERCP without cholangioscopy? (3) should EUS with FNA/FNB be performed versus ERCP with any form of tissue acquisition? The panel suggests **fluoroscopic-guided biopsy sampling in addition to brush cytology** over brush cytology alone (especially for hilar strictures), **cholangioscopic and EUS-guided biopsy sampling especially for patients who undergo nondiagnostic sampling**, **cholangioscopic biopsy sampling for nondistal strictures**, and **EUS-guided biopsy sampling for distal strictures or those with suspected spread to surrounding lymph nodes and other structures**.

**Grade vocabulary as the document defines it:** the panel's own convention is that stronger recommendations are typically stated as *"we recommend…"* and weaker recommendations as *"we suggest…."* All three recommendations here are **"we suggest"** statements, each labelled exactly **"Conditional recommendation/very low quality of evidence."** The document numbers them itself (Recommendation 1–3, matching Questions 1–3) and gives **no other grade, strength, or certainty label**. Intraductal ultrasound (IDUS) and confocal laser endomicroscopy (CLE) were reviewed under "Other considerations" and received **no recommendation** of any kind.

## Recommendations (all 3, verbatim, with the document's own grades)

The document issues **exactly three**. No other statement in it is graded, numbered, or framed as a recommendation.

**Clinical question 1:** In patients with undetermined biliary strictures, should ERCP with fluoroscopic-guided biopsy sampling be performed **in addition to** brush cytology versus ERCP with brush cytology alone to diagnose malignancy?

> **Recommendation 1.** In patients with biliary strictures of undetermined etiology undergoing ERCP, the ASGE suggests the addition of fluoroscopic-guided biopsy sampling with brush cytology versus brush cytology alone to diagnose malignancy.
> *(Conditional recommendation / very low quality of evidence)*

**Clinical question 2:** In patients with undetermined biliary strictures, should ERCP with cholangioscopic-guided biopsy sampling be performed versus ERCP without cholangioscopy to diagnose malignancy?

> **Recommendation 2.** In patients with biliary strictures of undetermined etiology undergoing ERCP, the ASGE suggests the use of cholangioscopic-guided biopsy sampling in
> a. Nondistal biliary strictures where there is a high probability of adequate drainage of the critical liver segment or
> b. Previous nondiagnostic ERCP without cholangioscopy and
> c. Centers with clinical expertise and easy access to the equipment.
> Otherwise, the ASGE suggests ERCP with or without cholangioscopy in the diagnosis of malignancy.
> *(Conditional recommendation / very low quality of evidence)*

**Clinical question 3:** In patients with undetermined biliary strictures, should EUS with FNA/FNB be performed versus ERCP with any form of tissue acquisition to diagnose malignancy?

> **Recommendation 3.** In patients with biliary strictures of undetermined etiology undergoing ERCP, the ASGE suggests EUS in addition to ERCP for the diagnosis of malignancy in the presence of
> a. Prior ERCP with nondiagnostic ERCP results,
> b. Distal biliary stricture, or
> c. Presence of lymphadenopathy or metastatic disease on cross-sectional imaging.
> *(Conditional recommendation / very low quality of evidence)*

## Key Findings / Claims

**Epidemiology / rationale**
- [[cholangiocarcinoma|Cholangiocarcinoma]] incidence is approximately 8000 cases/year in the United States and increasing; overall 5-year survival rate about 10%; diagnosis at an earlier stage results in a higher likelihood of survival, so malignancy should be diagnosed as soon as possible.
- Risk of malignancy in a biliary stricture without an obvious mass on cross-sectional imaging is ~55%.
- Endoscopic sampling preferred over percutaneous (external drain, needle-track seeding risk) or surgical approaches.
- **Outcome definitions used throughout:** *technical success* = percentage of cases where the endoscopist was able to perform the desired tissue sampling; *specimen adequacy* = pathologic diagnosis with enough cellular components to make a determination of malignant or benign. Other outcomes assessed: incremental yield, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value (NPV), adverse events.

| **EUS + ERCP** combined (8 studies) | **.88** (.85-.91; I² = 53.6%) | vs ERCP alone **.61** (.57-.64; I² = 86.4%), **P < .001** | Q3 |

**Miss rates (Q1):** brush cytology alone **58%** (95% CI, 46-71; I² = 79.5%); biopsy sampling alone **41%** (95% CI, 31-52; I² = 80.3%).

**Q1 — Fluoroscopic-guided biopsy sampling + brush cytology (de novo systematic review/meta-analysis: 21 observational studies — 20 full text, 1 abstract — 2726 patients)**
- Incremental yield of adding intraductal biopsy sampling to brush cytology alone: **20% (95% CI, 9-31; I² = 54.5%)**.
- Subgroup analyses revealed **no difference** in sensitivity of brushings versus biopsy sampling for **proximal vs distal strictures** or **biliary vs pancreatic masses**.
- **No difference in technical success** (odds ratio [OR] 3.27; 95% CI, .52-20.53; I² = 65%).
- **Specimen adequacy higher for brush cytology** — but the panel attributes this to intention-to-treat analysis reflecting the technical difficulty and failures of fluoroscopic-guided biopsy rather than specimen quality itself. The panel acknowledged intraductal biopsy is technically more difficult and requires more expertise because it is typically performed **without direct endoscopic visualization**, so some studies may not have genuinely attempted it in all strictures.
- **No difference in adverse events** (OR .53; 95% CI, .14-2.05; I² = 0%). Absolute event numbers were low: **2 patients (brush cytology) and 5 patients (intraductal biopsy), out of >500 in each group**. However, **2 severe adverse events — prolonged bleeding and perforation requiring surgical choledochotomy — occurred in the fluoroscopic-guided biopsy group**.
- No significant difference in **costs or health equity** was found. A cost-utility study showed biopsy sampling was **cost-effective at a willingness-to-pay threshold of <$50,000**.
- Because biopsy sampling is more technically challenging, more time-consuming, and produced the more severe adverse events, the panel made the conditional recommendation **acknowledging that biopsy sampling should be performed either at tertiary care centers or where there is endoscopic expertise**.

**Q2 — Cholangioscopy (de novo systematic review/meta-analysis: 13 studies — 1 randomized controlled trial (RCT) + 12 observational — 1529 patients, ERCP with cholangioscopy vs ERCP with other tissue acquisition)**
- Incremental yield of ERCP with cholangioscopy over ERCP without: **27% (95% CI, 9-46; I² = 56.8%)** across 4 observational studies; **41% (95% CI, 11-72)** in the single RCT.
- One study: **higher sensitivity for distal strictures with intraductal biopsy sampling (76%) than with cholangioscopic-guided biopsy sampling (50%)**; in that study **no** sensitivity difference was found between ERCP with and without cholangioscopy for **proximal** bile duct strictures.
- **No difference** in technical success, specimen adequacy (.96; 95% CI, .23-4; I² = 0%), or adverse events (.58; 95% CI, .26-1.26; I² = 0%). Most common adverse event in both groups was **acute pancreatitis, mostly mild**.
- Cholangioscopy added **14 minutes (95% CI, 10-20)** to the ERCP (1 study).
- Higher cost (**additional ~$2637** when cholangioscopy was done during ERCP with stent placement); access limited primarily to tertiary referral centers with experienced operators. Nevertheless cost-effective — in PSC, incremental **quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) gain of .22** at an additional cost of **$8562.44**, base-case **incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) $39,277.25**, below the <$50,000 willingness-to-pay threshold. In that study cholangioscopy was **more cost-effective than brush cytology, fluoroscopic-guided biopsy sampling, and [[fish|fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)]]**.
- Cholangioscopy **may not be optimal for distal strictures** — it is often difficult and less accurate in the **very distal** portion of the bile duct because of cholangioscope instability and difficulty passing the mini-forceps.
- **Drainage before/after cholangioscopy matters:** cholangioscopy requires instillation of water or saline solution, risking introduction of infection into the proximal biliary tree if it is not adequately drained. Panel split — some experts prefer **not** to perform cholangioscopy at the initial ERCP and to wait until decompression of the proximal ducts is ensured; others would do it in the initial session as long as drainage of the proximal ducts appeared feasible.

- **Visual interpretation during cholangioscopy matters.** Malignant strictures appear **nodular, papillary, or infiltrative**; understanding the distinguishing features can assist with targeting cholangioscopic-guided biopsy sampling to potentially increase its yield. *Feature table: [[cholangioscopy]].*

**Q3 — EUS-FNA/FNB (existing meta-analysis by Chiang et al; a systematic review found no additional studies)**
- Incremental benefit of EUS **after nondiagnostic ERCP with brush cytology**: **15% (95% CI, 9-24; I² = 0%)**.
- **Subgroup — where EUS wins:** higher sensitivity for **distal strictures .82 (95% CI, .76-.87) vs .62 (95% CI, .55-.69)** and for **pancreatic masses .82 (95% CI, .62-1.02) vs .46 (95% CI, .4-.51)**; **P < .0001** for both.
- **No difference** in technical success or specimen adequacy. EUS had a significantly **lower** adverse event rate (OR 8.11; 95% CI, 2.95-22.29): only **3 minor bleeding episodes** with EUS-guided FNA versus **44 adverse events with ERCP** (1 severe pancreatitis, 27 mild pancreatitis, 10 cholangitis, 6 mild bleeding).
- EUS added an average of **23 minutes (95% CI, 14-32)** to the procedure time (1 study, historical control subjects).
- **Cost:** EUS with FNA reported at **$1076.25**; cost is much lower when combined with ERCP than when performed alone, so only a minor cost increase when EUS and ERCP are done in the same session. EUS was found to be cost-effective in biliary strictures **even without a discrete mass**. EUS is not as widely available throughout the country as ERCP.
- **When to perform it:** EUS is clearly beneficial for **distal** biliary strictures, and should be performed if a pancreatic mass, lymphadenopathy, or metastatic disease is noted **within reach of the echoendoscope** on cross-sectional imaging. Panel split for proximal strictures — some would routinely combine EUS with ERCP for any biliary stricture, others were less keen because of lower diagnostic yield and added time.
- **Needle-track seeding risk** must be emphasized with EUS-guided FNA or FNB of hilar cholangiocarcinoma, which may exclude patients from undergoing [[liver-transplantation|liver transplantation]]. Therefore, if EUS is performed for **proximal or hilar** strictures, the endosonographer should **not** perform FNA or FNB **of the biliary mass itself**.

**Other considerations (reviewed; no recommendation made on either)**
- **IDUS** — malignant features: intraluminal mass with an **irregular margin**, **wall thickness >9 mm**, heterogeneous lesion with an uneven mucosal surface, **eccentric wall thickening**, destruction of the wall layers, and masses that invade the surrounding tissue. IDUS increases the sensitivity of diagnosing malignant strictures compared with ERCP alone. Prior ASGE guidelines considered IDUS promising; because it is more widely available now, it could potentially be considered to help **localize the malignant-appearing region for targeted biopsy sampling**. One study: diagnostic accuracy of **IDUS-guided transpapillary biopsy 90.8% vs transpapillary biopsy alone 76.9% (P = .028)**. Utility needs further study before a recommendation on widespread adoption can be made.
- **CLE** — thin confocal laser probes inserted through the working channel of the duodenoscope (**probe-based confocal laser endomicroscopy, pCLE**). Pooled **sensitivity 90% (95% CI, 86-94; I² = 1.6%)** and **specificity 72% (95% CI, 65-79; I² = 0%)**; one systematic review noted its best application may be a high **NPV for malignancy of 94%**. The panel noted CLE is **difficult to master and expensive**, so widespread adoption is likely limited in the near future. Two reading systems: the **Miami classification** (consensus of a group of endoscopists; names malignancy, limited by **low interobserver agreement**) and the subsequent **Paris classification** (further defines the criteria for benign inflammatory strictures). *Criteria tables: [[confocal-laser-endomicroscopy]].*

**Summary and conclusions (ungraded, the document's own closing statement):** if the endoscopic expertise is available, ERCP with fluoroscopic-guided biopsy sampling **and** brush cytology should be performed for **any location** of biliary stricture, whereas cholangioscopy and EUS should **also** be considered, particularly in **nondistal** and **distal** strictures, respectively.

**Future directions (7, as listed):**
1. RCTs addressing the 3 clinical questions.
2. Focus on patients with PSC — the diagnostic algorithm may change in this population, where FISH analysis plays a higher role.
3. Technologic developments — mini overtubes to facilitate intraductal biopsy sampling; improvements in cholangioscopy platforms and tissue sampling devices; novel imaging modalities such as CLE.
4. Adjunctive pathologic analyses — next-generation sequencing (NGS), flow cytometry, FISH analysis, digital image analysis.
5. Diagnostic yield of cholangioscopy and/or EUS in **consecutive** patients presenting with biliary strictures, rather than limited to those in whom cholangioscopy is technically successful.
6. Interval of time before the next ERCP when the first is nondiagnostic.
7. Utility of artificial intelligence (AI)–guided visual interpretation and AI-guided sampling during cholangioscopy and EUS.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- Largely consistent with [[acg-2023-biliary-strictures]]; this ASGE document is more granular on the relative sensitivities of each tissue-acquisition modality and scopes its recommendations by stricture location.
- All three recommendations rest on **very low quality of evidence** (predominantly observational); only Q2 includes a single RCT, which supports cholangioscopy.
- **Distal strictures, cholangioscopy vs intraductal biopsy:** one study found intraductal biopsy outperformed cholangioscopic-guided biopsy for distal strictures (76% vs 50%), and cholangioscopy is mechanically poor in the very distal duct — which is why Recommendation 2 is restricted to **nondistal** strictures.
- **No recommendation on IDUS or CLE** despite favorable test characteristics — adoption limited by availability, expertise, and cost.
- **Timing of cholangioscopy and extent of EUS use are explicitly unsettled within the panel** (initial vs delayed cholangioscopy relative to proximal drainage; routine vs selective EUS for proximal strictures). Neither is resolved by a recommendation.
- The interval before a repeat ERCP after a nondiagnostic one is named as a future direction — the document gives **no interval**.
