## Summary

This update is the AGA's operating manual for the moment an endoscopist makes a hole. It covers prevention, intraprocedural recognition, and closure, organ by organ — esophagus, stomach, duodenum/periampullary, colon — and is explicit that prospective outcome data on endoscopic therapy of perforations are limited; the entire evidence base is retrospective series. All 16 statements are Best Practice Advice.

The generic response is standardized across all four organs. Alert the team; switch to **carbon dioxide insufflation at low flow**; **keep the perforation clean** by suctioning and, if needed, repositioning the patient so the defect is non-dependent; minimize insufflation to avoid compartment syndrome; give **IV antibiotics covering Gram-negative and anaerobic organisms**. Then choose a closure device by **defect size**, which is the single organizing decision of the paper: through-the-scope clips (TTSC) for the smallest defects, over-the-scope clips (OTSC) in the middle, endoscopic suturing (or TTSC + endoloop) for the largest, and a fully covered SEMS only when primary closure is not achievable. Two hard stops override all of it: a hemodynamically unstable patient, or a delayed perforation with peritoneal signs/frank peritonitis, **goes to surgery with no endoscopic attempt**. And urgent surgical consultation should be highly considered in *every* perforation, even one that was technically closed.

Organ-specific detail then modifies the size thresholds. Esophagus: TTSC/OTSC for **<2 cm**, endoscopic suturing for **>2 cm**, SEMS reserved for failed primary closure. Stomach: same **<2 cm / >2 cm** split, with TTSC optimal **<1 cm**, OTSC preferable for **1–3 cm**, and endoloop/TTSC combination or suturing for **>3 cm**. Duodenum is the most dangerous — thin wall, angulated C-loop, paradoxical scope motion, and pooled pancreatic proteolytic enzymes that digest exposed muscle at a resection base and drive *delayed* perforation. The duodenal section is organized around the **Stapfer-type scheme**: type 1 (lateral wall tear from the duodenoscope, often >3 cm, usually needs surgery) and type 2 (periampullary/retroperitoneal from an overextended sphincterotomy, subtle and easily missed). Colon: sigmoid is the dominant site of scope-trauma perforation, and the **cecum/right colon is limited to TTSC** because a tortuous or unclean colon may make it impossible to reach the defect with an OTSC or suturing device.

The update also names the procedures that carry increased perforation risk (BPA 4) so the consent conversation and the team preparation happen *before* the case, and closes on documentation: accurate records, prompt disclosure to patient and family, and reporting to the institutional quality officer and risk management team.

## Key Findings / Claims

### Best Practice Advice statements (verbatim — 16 BPA)

| # | Statement |
|---|---|
| **BPA 1** | For all procedures, especially procedures carrying an increased risk for perforation, a thorough discussion between the endoscopist and the patient (preferably together with the patient's family) should include details of the procedural techniques and risks involved. |
| **BPA 2** | The area of perforation should be kept clean to prevent any spillage of gastrointestinal contents into the perforation by aspirating liquids and, if necessary, changing the patient position to bring the perforation into a non-dependent location while minimizing insufflation of carbon dioxide to avoid compartment syndrome. |
| **BPA 3** | Use of carbon dioxide for insufflation is encouraged for all endoscopic procedures, especially any endoscopic procedure with increased risk of perforation. If available, carbon dioxide should be used for all endoscopic procedures. |
| **BPA 4** | All endoscopists should be aware of the procedures that carry an increased risk for perforation such as any dilation, foreign body removal, any per oral endoscopic myotomy (Zenker's, esophageal, pyloric), stricture incision, thermal coagulation for hemostasis or tumor ablation, percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy, ampullectomy, endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR), endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), endoluminal stenting with self-expanding metal stent (SEMS), full-thickness endoscopic resection, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) in surgically altered anatomy, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided biliary and pancreatic access, EUS-guided cystogastrostomy, and endoscopic gastroenterostomy using a lumen apposing metal stent (LAMS). |
| **BPA 5** | Urgent surgical consultation should be highly considered in all cases with perforation even when endoscopic repair is technically successful. |
| **BPA 6** | For all upper gastrointestinal perforations, the patient should be considered to be admitted for observation, receive intravenous fluids, be kept nothing by mouth, receive broad-spectrum antibiotics (to cover Gram-negative and anaerobic organisms), nasogastric tube (NGT) placement (albeit some exceptions), and surgical consultation. |
| **BPA 7** | For upper gastrointestinal tract perforations, a water-soluble upper gastrointestinal series should be considered to confirm the absence of continuing leak at the perforation site before initiating a clear liquid diet. |
| **BPA 8** | Endoscopic closure of esophageal perforations should be pursued when feasible, utilizing through-the-scope clips (TTSCs) or over-the-scope clips (OTSCs) for perforations <2 cm and endoscopic suturing for perforations >2 cm, reserving esophageal stenting with SEMS for cases where primary closure is not possible. |
| **BPA 9** | Endoscopic closure of gastric perforations should be pursued when feasible, utilizing TTSCs or OTSCs for perforations <2 cm and endoscopic suturing or combination of TTSCs and endoloop for perforations >2 cm. |
| **BPA 10** | For large type 1 duodenal perforations (lateral duodenal wall tear >3 cm), being cognizant of the difficulty in closing them endoscopically, urgent surgical consultation should be made while the feasibility of endoscopic closure is assessed. |
| **BPA 11** | Because type 2 periampullary (retroperitoneal) perforations are subtle and can be easily missed, the endoscopist should carefully assess the gas pattern on fluoroscopy to avoid delays in treatment and request a computed tomography scan if there is a concern for such a perforation; identified perforations of this type at the time of ERCP may be closed with TTSCs if feasible and/or by placing a fully covered SEMS into the bile duct across the ampulla. |
| **BPA 12** | For the management of large duodenal polyps, endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) should only be performed by experienced endoscopists and endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) only by experts because both EMR and ESD in the duodenum require proficiency in resection and mucosal defect closure techniques to manage immediate and/or delayed perforations (caused by the proteolytic enzymes of the pancreas). |
| **BPA 13** | Endoscopists should be aware that colon perforations occurring during diagnostic colonoscopy are most commonly located in the sigmoid colon due to direct trauma from forceful advancement of the colonoscope. Such tears recognized at the time of colonoscopy may be closed by TTSCs or OTSCs if the bowel preparation is good and the patient is stable. |
| **BPA 14** | Although colon perforation is responsive to various endoscopic tools such as TTSC, OTSC, and endoscopic suturing, perforations in the right colon, especially in the cecum, have been relegated to using only TTSCs because of inability to reach the site of the perforation with an endoscopic suturing device or OTSC if the colon is tortuous or unclean. Recently a new suture-based device for defect closure has been introduced allowing deep submucosal and intramuscular enhanced fixation through a standard gastroscope or colonoscope. |
| **BPA 15** | Patients with perforations who are hemodynamically unstable or who have suffered a delayed perforation with peritoneal signs or frank peritonitis should be surgically managed without any attempt at endoscopic closure. |
| **BPA 16** | In any adverse event including perforation, it is paramount to ensure accurate documentation, prompt discussion with the patient and family, and swift reporting to the quality officer (or equivalent) and risk management team of the institution (in major adverse events). |

### The first-response sequence at the moment of perforation
1. **Inform the staff** — trainees, colleagues, nurses, technicians, anesthesiologists.
2. **Confirm CO₂ insufflation and adjust to low flow.**
3. **Clear and suction the field** of luminal contents that could contaminate the peritoneum.
4. **Give IV antibiotics** with Gram-negative and anaerobic coverage.
5. Determine cause and morphology → the vast majority can be addressed endoscopically **if the tools and expertise are available**; a few types are better served by surgery with no endoscopic attempt.
- Preparation matters before the case: review surgical history, cross-sectional imaging, and prior endoscopies, and rehearse the planned procedure with the team. **Know your limits** and which cases belong at a high-volume center.

### Esophageal perforation
- **Non-iatrogenic causes:** caustic ingestion, sharp penetrating foreign bodies (e.g. fishbone), Boerhaave syndrome.
- **In [[eosinophilic-esophagitis]]:** 10 perforations (2.0%) among 511 patients over 14 years; risk factors were a **history of food impaction (OR 14.9, 95% CI 1.7–129.2)** and **a focal stricture (OR 4.6, 95% CI 1.1–19.7)**. In a systematic review of 76 perforations in 70 EoE patients, most were **non-iatrogenic**; pushing impacted food into the stomach caused 5 and esophageal dilation caused 8; 25/76 required surgery, 10 got a stent, 2 got clips.
- **Perforation rates:** esophageal dilation for simple rings or peptic strictures **0.09%–2.2%**, higher for caustic or radiation-induced strictures, and **0.4%–14% for pneumatic dilation in achalasia**. EMR in Barrett's esophagus **0%–3%**.
- **POEM:** small intraprocedural mucosal-flap perforations are common and usually inconsequential — manage with TTSC. The dangerous complication is **delayed barrier failure** → leak, mediastinitis, peritonitis; rate **0.2%–1.1%**.
  - **POEM technique to prevent it:** control energy delivery from the knife tip at all times; maintain orientation in the tunnel (avoid spiraling); avoid excessive forward force tearing the mucosotomy; avoid "counter-coup" flipping of the knife during myotomy. **Prefer posterior POEM orientation where feasible** — 3 of 4 RCTs and a meta-analysis of all 4 showed significantly more mucosal injuries with anterior POEM.
  - **Stenting is discouraged after POEM** — stent erosion through the myotomy, high migration rate, inadequate seal at the patulous post-POEM EGJ.
- **Technique:** begin clip placement at the end of the defect **distal to the endoscope**, to preserve the view of the remainder.
- **OTSC performance:** effective for **1–2 cm** perforations; systematic review clinical success **85.3% (399/468)**, with failure driven by **defects >2 cm** and **closure attempted >72 hours** after perforation. Mounting an OTSC increases scope tip diameter to **16.5–21 mm** and the cap edges are not smooth — introduce gently through the hypopharynx, especially after head-and-neck surgery or cervical radiation.
- **SEMS:** the goal is primary closure; a fully covered stent diverting flow to allow healing by secondary intention is the next best option and gives a longer, more drawn-out course. Diameter should be **slightly wider than the native esophagus** — seal the defect without expanding it. Secure the proximal end by endosuturing or a stent-fixing device (e.g. OTSC Stentfix) to prevent migration; this is essential in fistula, where there is no stricture to hold the stent.
- **Esophagectomy leaks** (within days of operation): attempt primary closure with TTSC, OTSC or endosuturing, **plus** a fully covered stent.
- **Esophageal fistula:** TTSC or endosuturing alone is usually ineffective → fully covered stent with a snug fit.
- **Endoscopic vacuum therapy** — polyurethane sponge on a nasogastric tube inserted into the defect. Review of 11 studies, 210 patients: **90% healing for anastomotic leaks, 96% closure for esophageal perforations**, median follow-up 235 days, median therapy duration **17 days**.

### Gastric perforation
- **Non-iatrogenic causes:** peptic ulcer disease, caustic ingestion. **High-risk procedures:** EMR and ESD.
- **The proximal gastric wall is thinner than the antrum** — extra caution for proximal resections.
- **Gastric ESD perforation rate 3.02% (512/16,941)**, of which **98% (506/512) recovered without surgery**. Most EMR/ESD perforations are intraprocedural; **delayed perforation rate 0.4%–0.7%**.
- **ESD perforation risk factors:** lesion location (upper third, middle third, greater curvature); lesion **size >20 mm**; depth of invasion; submucosal fibrosis; endoscopist's prior workload; **procedure length >60–120 min**; octogenarian patients; piecemeal resection.
- Perform lengthy procedures under **general anesthesia**.
- **Abdominal compartment rescue:** if the gastric lumen will not insufflate and the abdomen is tense, place a **16–18 gauge angiocath percutaneously into the peritoneum** to decompress and recover luminal insufflation. Percuss the left upper and mid-to-low abdomen for a tympanitic area; for a duodenal bulb perforation the entry point is roughly **3–5 cm below the umbilicus**. Clean with alcohol and introduce the needle slowly, monitoring air flow.
- **TTSC technique:** apply **suction before placing each clip** for tighter approximation.
- **OTSC caveats:** only **one attempt** — aim precisely. The **Twin Grasper** (two independently opening jaws) grabs one edge, pulls it across, then grasps the opposing edge; pull both into the suction cap, apply suction, then fire. OTSC may inadvertently capture adjacent organs or vessels beyond the GI wall. The **remOVE** cutting device can remove a deployed OTSC, but has a **10–15 minute warm-up**, so re-deploying after a misfire costs significant time.
- Combined **OTSC + TTSC** closed post-ESD mucosal defects in **91.3% (11/12)**, facilitating early discharge.
- **>3 cm:** endoloop/TTSC — TTSCs placed at equal distance to fix a detachable snare around the defect, then tighten the snare (double-channel scope). Alternatively endoscopic suturing, which obtains **full-thickness purchases** and is arguably superior for large defects. Endosuturing succeeded in **31/31 patients** (58.1% gastric, 41.9% rectal ESD) with no delayed bleeding or perforation.

### Duodenal and periampullary perforation
- **Why the duodenum is the highest-risk site:** thin wall; anatomic challenge of the C-loop; paradoxical endoscope motion; and **luminal exposure to pancreatic proteolytic enzymes**, which pool in the duodenum and break down exposed muscle at an EMR/ESD base — the mechanism of **delayed** perforation.
- **Iatrogenic causes:** duodenal stricture dilation, EMR (including ampullectomy), ESD, EUS, ERCP.
- **ERCP perforation:** overall risk **<1%**, with **mortality 7.8%–9.9%**. Mechanisms: duodenoscope-related trauma, sphincterotomy, intraductal guidewire manipulation.
- **ERCP perforation risk factors:** older age, longer procedure duration, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction, non-dilated bile duct, performance of a sphincterotomy/precut sphincterotomy, access papillotomy, and altered anatomy (e.g. Billroth II partial gastrectomy).
- **Surgically altered anatomy tip:** start with a standard **forward-viewing upper endoscope** to identify and mark the entrance of the pancreatobiliary limb before advancing the side-viewing duodenoscope.

**Type 1 — lateral duodenal wall tear**
- Caused by excessive shearing force or angle-related trauma from the **shaft or tip of the duodenoscope**; intraperitoneal or retroperitoneal leakage. Most often during scope advancement where the shaft presses on the duodenal wall or a periampullary diverticulum, or on forceful retraction during difficult stone extraction.
- **Presentation:** sudden bleeding, lumen deflation, difficulty maintaining insufflation.
- **Usually requires surgery** unless early endoscopic closure succeeds. **>3 cm → urgent surgical consultation** while feasibility of endoscopic closure is assessed (BPA 10).
- Small perforations (**<13 mm**) close with clips in **88%–100%**; band ligation and endoloops also reported. Where endoscopic closure succeeds, **>90%** recover clinically without surgery.
- Enteral SEMS is the last endoscopic resort — **covered duodenal stents are not available in the US**; fully covered through-the-scope esophageal stents have been used with proximal suturing to prevent migration. **Uncovered SEMS should be avoided.**

**Type 2 — periampullary (retroperitoneal)**
- Caused by **overextension of a sphincterotomy beyond the intraduodenal portion of the ampulla**.
- **Prevention:** minimize the length of cutting wire in contact with the ampulla; use stepwise incisions; direct the force and direction of the cut with the **right hand on the scope shaft creating counter-clockwise torque**, rather than lifting the elevator or bowing the sphincterotome; **terminate the sphincterotomy just before the flat transverse duodenal mucosa cephalad of the papillary mound**; use newer generators with controlled, pulsed-blended current to reduce "zipper cuts."
- **Detection:** subtle — assess the fluoroscopic gas pattern **under the liver or around the right kidney**; get a CT if concerned (BPA 11).
- **Asymptomatic retroperitoneal air after ERCP may need no intervention** — 14% (7/50) of post-ERCP patients had CT retroperitoneal air with uneventful recovery. But type 2 perforations can still lead to retroperitoneal abscess, peritonitis, and death.
- **Closure:** TTSCs if feasible, and/or a **fully covered SEMS into the bile duct across the ampulla** — likely to seal the defect with high clinical success.
- **Course:** patients declare within **24–48 h** whether they need surgery (worsening abdominal pain or systemic inflammatory response). If improving, at **2–4 days** obtain a water-soluble upper GI series or CT with oral contrast to confirm no extravasation before starting clear liquids.
- **Delayed recognition >6 hours after ERCP** is associated with increased length of stay, increased mortality, and more complicated surgery.

**Duodenal resection defects**
- Closure modalities: TTSC, OTSC, endosuturing, polymer gels or sheets, TTSC + endoloop cinching, and covering the defect with **Vicryl mesh** affixed by TTSCs (Figure 3).
- If a large post-EMR duodenal defect **cannot** be closed → admit with close observation for delayed perforation.
- **Nasopancreatic tube (NPT)** placement may prevent delayed perforation by diverting pancreatic secretions away from the defect — weigh against the risks of duodenoscope passage, pancreatitis, and drain dislodgement.

### Colon perforation
- **Non-iatrogenic causes:** diverticulitis, inflammatory bowel disease, rarely colonic obstruction. **Iatrogenic:** endoscopic resection, hot forceps biopsy, stricture dilation (Crohn's, radiation), colonic stenting, and forceful scope advancement across angulations, into diverticula, or without scope straightening.
- **Rates:** stricture dilation in Crohn's disease **3%**; colonoscopy with polypectomy **0.8 per 1000**; EMR of large colon polyps **1.5% (96/6595)**.
- **Site distribution** (systematic review, 19 studies, 744 colon perforations): **sigmoid 63.8% (310)**, cecum 10% (49), transverse 9% (44), rectum 7% (34), ascending 5.6% (27), descending 4.4% (21), hepatic flexure 0.2% (1).
- **Colorectal stenting:** meta-analysis of 4086 patients — overall perforation **7.4%**; **post-stent dilation 20.4%**; bevacizumab-based therapy **12.5%**; chemotherapy without bevacizumab **7.0%**. A tertiary cancer center series of 199 SEMS placements reported **2.0%**. Age >70 years and sigmoid location were associated with early perforation after SEMS (n=474).
- **EMR technique to avoid colon perforation:**
  1. Use a submucosal injection solution **more viscous than normal saline** — succinylated gelatin, hydroxyethyl starch, or glycerol.
  2. Consider **partially reopening and closing the snare before resection** to let the muscularis propria fall back down.
  3. **Inspect the resection base** for muscularis propria injury — look for the **target sign**.
  4. Recognize and close muscularis propria injury or perforation **early**.
  5. When submucosal injection fails to lift the polyp, consider **full-thickness resection** with a full-thickness resection device.
- **Management:** intraprocedural perforation in a **stable** patient with a **clean** site and no spillage → attempt endoscopic closure. A **rectal** perforation gives the most time and space for TTSC, OTSC, or suturing. In the systematic review, endoscopic clipping was used in **114 cases (15.4%)** and conservative management in **71 (9.1%)**; the remainder were surgical.
- **Cecum / tortuous or unclean colon:** withdrawing the colonoscope to fit an OTSC may be difficult and dangerous, and suturing may not be reachable — **TTSC only** (BPA 14).
- **Large sigmoid tears from blunt scope trauma**, hemodynamic instability, or delayed perforation with peritoneal signs → **surgery** (BPA 15).

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Stapfer typing is used but never defined in full.** The update discusses **type 1** and **type 2** duodenal/periampullary perforations in detail but does not print types 3 and 4, nor does it cite Stapfer by name. The complete four-type classification is **not supported by this source** — do not add types 3–4 to the wiki from memory; the primary (Stapfer 2000, *Ann Surg*) is not in `raw/`.
- **Size thresholds are not identical between organs and are stated as inequalities without a tie-breaker.** Esophagus splits at 2 cm; stomach splits at 2 cm in BPA 9 but the body text gives TTSC <1 cm and OTSC 1–3 cm; the esophageal body text gives OTSC as effective for 1–2 cm. Reproduce the BPA thresholds as the decision rule and the body-text ranges as device performance data — they are not in conflict but they are not interchangeable either.
- **Evidence quality is low throughout.** Every statement is Best Practice Advice from expert opinion; the authors state that prospective outcome studies on endoscopic therapy of perforations are limited and that the literature is retrospective.
- **Device availability is US-specific and dated (2021):** "covered duodenal stents are not available in the United States at this time." Flag as time-limited if it reaches a page.
- **POEM orientation.** This update prefers **posterior** POEM on mucosal-injury grounds. Check against [[aga-2024-poem-advances]] and the achalasia guidelines before the wiki asserts a preferred orientation — the newer document wins under the source-priority rule.
