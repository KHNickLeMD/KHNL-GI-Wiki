## Summary

American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) Clinical Practice Update (CPU) providing 13 Best Practice Advice (BPA) statements for the noncolorectal-cancer screening, vaccination, and general health maintenance of adult patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Colorectal cancer screening is explicitly out of scope (covered separately). Because this was an expert review and not a systematic review, the statements carry no formal evidence grade or strength rating.

Key thrust: novel biologics and small molecules improve IBD outcomes but raise rates of serious/opportunistic infection and certain cancers, so gastroenterologists must share responsibility (with primary care) for cancer screening, vaccination, bone health, and mental health rather than assuming these are handled elsewhere. The update covers age-appropriate general cancer screening (breast, prostate, lung per the US Preventive Services Task Force [USPSTF]), cervical dysplasia, skin cancer (melanoma + nonmelanoma), and anal cancer, then the adult immunization schedule with IBD-specific nuances (live-vaccine avoidance on immune-modifying therapy, hepatitis B seroprotection workup, high-dose influenza, pneumococcal, respiratory syncytial virus [RSV], recombinant herpes zoster, human papillomavirus [HPV]). It closes with bone densitometry indications and annual depression/anxiety screening.

## Key Findings / Claims

**Verbatim Best Practice Advice statements (no evidence grade per source):**

1. All adult patients with IBD should receive age-appropriate cancer screening.
2. Adult women with IBD should follow age-appropriate screening for cervical dysplasia. Data are insufficient to determine whether patients receiving combined immunosuppression or thiopurines require more frequent screening. Shared decision making and individual risk stratification are encouraged.
3. All adult patients with IBD should follow skin cancer primary prevention practices by avoiding excessive exposure to the sun's ultraviolet radiation. Patients on immunomodulators, anti-tumor necrosis factor (anti-TNF) biologic agents, or small molecules should undergo a yearly total body skin exam (TBSE). Patients with any history of thiopurine use should continue with a yearly TBSE even after thiopurine cessation.
4. At every colonoscopy, a thorough perianal and anal examination should be performed. Special attention should be made to inspection of the anal canal of patients with perianal Crohn's disease, with anal stricture, with human papillomavirus (HPV), with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and who engage in anoreceptive intercourse.
5. Gastroenterology clinicians should discuss age-appropriate vaccines with adult patients who have IBD and share responsibility with primary care providers for administering these vaccines. Patients with IBD should follow the adult immunization schedule advised by the CDC [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention] for all vaccines with the exception of live vaccines; patients receiving immune-modifying agents should be counseled against receiving live vaccines; immunization history to the 2 live pediatric vaccines (varicella and MMR [measles, mumps, rubella] series) is presumptive evidence of immunity. All adults 18 to 26 years of age should receive the HPV vaccine series, and those between 27 and 45 years of age should be vaccinated if they are likely to have a new sexual partner.
6. Inactivated vaccines are safe in patients with IBD, and their administration is not associated with exacerbation of IBD activity. We suggest that patients receive vaccines at the earliest opportunity and preferably be off corticosteroids or at the lowest tolerable corticosteroid dose.
7. All adult patients with IBD should be evaluated for latent hepatitis B infection. Patients who have previously completed a full hepatitis B vaccine series but are not seroprotected (anti-HBs <10 mIU/mL) should receive a single challenge dose of hepatitis B vaccine; 4 to 8 weeks after this challenge dose, their anti-HBs levels should be measured to evaluate for an amnestic response. An amnestic response (anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL, seroprotection) suggests immunologic memory and no further doses are needed. If no amnestic response is observed, the patient should complete a second full 2- or 3-dose series of hepatitis B vaccination.
8. All adult patients with IBD should receive an annual inactivated influenza vaccine. Patients receiving anti-TNF monotherapy or who are solid organ transplant recipients can benefit from a high-dose influenza vaccine. Adults 65 years of age and older should receive a high-dose, recombinant, or adjuvanted influenza vaccine. Live attenuated intranasal vaccines should be avoided.
9. All adult patients with IBD 19 to 64 years of age should receive an initial pneumococcal vaccine, with a subsequent second pneumococcal vaccine administered at 65 years of age and older.
10. All adult patients with IBD who are 60 years of age and older should receive a respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) vaccine. There is no preference for any of the available RSV vaccines.
11. All adult patients 19 years of age and older receiving immune-modifying therapies, or with plans to initiate immune-modifying therapies, should receive a recombinant herpes zoster vaccine series, regardless of their prior varicella vaccination status.
12. Bone densitometry should be considered in patients with IBD, regardless of age, when risk factors for osteopenia and osteoporosis are present. These risk factors include low body mass index (<20 kg/m²), >3 months of cumulative corticosteroid exposure, current smoking, postmenopausal status, or hypogonadism. In the absence of other factors, bone densitometry should be considered for postmenopausal women and men 65 years or older.
13. All adult patients with IBD should be screened for depression and anxiety annually. Patients who screen positive for depression or anxiety should be referred to the appropriate specialist, be it their primary care physician or a mental health specialist.

**Supporting data:**
- General cancer screening per USPSTF: breast (average-risk women ≥40, every other year; other societies q1–2y), prostate (individualized, men 55–69), lung (high-risk adults 50–80 with ≥20 pack-years, current or quit within 15 years).
- Cervical: per USPSTF, women 21–29 cytology q3y; 30–65 cytology q3y, high-risk HPV testing q5y, or cotesting q5y. Dutch cohort — cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 2 or worse (CIN2+) risk rose per year of immunomodulator exposure (hazard ratio [HR] 1.16, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.08–1.25). Meta-analysis (Mann et al, 74,310 IBD vs >2M reference) — no significant increased cervical cancer risk (HR 1.24, 95% CI 0.94–1.63) regardless of subtype or anti-TNF/thiopurine use; slightly elevated low-grade cervical lesion risk (HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.04–1.28).
- Cervical screening not required: women <21 y; women >65 y with adequate prior screening and low risk; after hysterectomy with cervix removal and no history of high-grade lesions or cervical cancer.
- Anal cancer: mostly squamous cell. Incidence 10.2/100,000 person-years in ulcerative colitis (UC), 7.7 in Crohn's disease (CD); further increased in anal/perianal Crohn's (19.6/100,000). Risk factors: smoking, persistent HPV, HIV, men who have sex with men (MSM), women with HPV-associated genital cancers, solid organ recipients. Screening tools: digital anorectal exam, cytology, high-risk HPV testing ± genotyping; anal cytology performance data scarce in the non-HIV population, and not yet used for routine screening.

**Vaccine timing (BPA 6 commentary):**
- Vaccinate before immune-modifying therapy whenever feasible, but vaccination should not delay its initiation; preferably off corticosteroids or at the lowest dose.
- Seasonal influenza and COVID-19 boosters: offer regardless of regimen, even on high-dose corticosteroids; give irrespective of the biologic dosing schedule.
- Yellow fever vaccine: safe without immunosuppression; on immune-modifying therapy, consider modifying travel plans to avoid endemic areas. Injectable typhoid and other inactivated vaccines safe for all.

**Hepatitis B (BPA 7 commentary):** HEPLISAV-B (higher seroconversion) should be considered preferred over the aluminum 3-dose ENGERIX-B series. Not seroprotected after the challenge dose → new series, preferably HEPLISAV-B, with seroprotection checked 4 weeks after finishing it. Primary nonresponders to the 2-dose series may benefit from a third dose.

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- **Melanoma screening:** the existing [[ibd-preventive-care]] page (per American College of Gastroenterology [ACG] 2025) recommends annual full-skin melanoma screening for ALL IBD patients regardless of biologic use. This AGA CPU (BPA 3) ties yearly TBSE to patients on immunomodulators, anti-TNF, or small molecules (plus any thiopurine history even after cessation), rather than universally. Both are guideline-tier and same publication year; the page retains the broader ACG recommendation and notes the AGA framing.
- **RSV age — internal inconsistency:** BPA 10 says all adults with IBD ≥60 y; the CPU's own Table 1 and its ACIP citation say ≥75 y, or 60–74 y with risk factors for severe RSV (plus Abrysvo at 32–36 weeks of pregnancy). The document does not reconcile the two.
- **Cervical screening frequency:** AGA finds data insufficient to mandate more frequent cervical screening on immunosuppression (BPA 2), whereas the existing page describes annual screening on immune-modifying therapy (ACG). Both are shown on the page, with AGA's "insufficient data / shared decision making" qualifier alongside.
