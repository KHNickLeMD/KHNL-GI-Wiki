## Summary

This AGA Clinical Practice Update addresses **when to stop**, not when to start: how to decide whether to continue surveillance of Barrett's esophagus and screening/surveillance colonoscopy in older adults. The framing problem is that existing guidelines define high-risk populations and surveillance intervals but **rarely specify when to stop**, while the population ≥65 is projected to reach 82 million by 2050 (23% of the US population; 1 in every 5 adults).

The organizing principle is that **age alone is an insufficient marker of prognosis**. The relevant inputs are **life expectancy** (a 10-year life expectancy is necessary to derive a survival benefit from cancer screening), **comorbidity**, **frailty**, **prior screening history**, and **patient preference** — a frail 70-year-old may have a shorter life expectancy than a robust 85-year-old. The update endorses formal life-expectancy tools (the **Lee–Schonberg 4- to 14-year mortality index** on ePrognosis is named as the most widely used and well-validated) and patient decision aids.

For **Barrett's esophagus**, the dominant fact is that nondysplastic Barrett's (≈90% of the Barrett's population) carries only a **0.1%–0.3% annual** risk of progression to adenocarcinoma, while these patients largely die of something else — after successful endoscopic therapy of Barrett's-related neoplasia, other-cause mortality was **40 times** esophageal adenocarcinoma mortality. The hard stop rule is categorical: **inability to tolerate endoscopic, surgical, or oncologic intervention ends surveillance regardless of age.**

For the **colorectum**, the update quantifies both sides of the ledger: harms of screening colonoscopy (**~26 per 1000**) likely outweigh the benefit of finding CRC (**2–3 per 1000**) in patients without prior screening. In patients >75 with a history of only low-risk adenomas the 5-year CRC risk is ~**0.5%** — similar to those with no adenomas and lower than the general population — so they can be managed much as patients without polyps.

Because systematic reviews were not performed, **the Best Practice Advice statements carry no formal ratings** for quality of evidence or strength of the considerations presented.

### Grading

**No evidence grades and no strength ratings.** The document states explicitly: "Because systematic reviews were not performed, these Best Practice Advice statements do not carry formal ratings regarding the quality of evidence or strength of the presented considerations." ### Best Practice Advice statements

- **BPA 1** — Formal determination of comorbidities and use of **life expectancy tools** may help differentiate among older adults most likely to benefit vs those most likely to be harmed by surveillance. Real-world effectiveness and cost-effectiveness analysis in diverse populations would help with widespread implementation.
- **BPA 2** — The goal of endoscopic surveillance in Barrett's esophagus is to **detect dysplasia and adenocarcinoma at an early and treatable stage**. Endoscopic surveillance of Barrett's esophagus should follow **current best practices in terms of imaging, tissue acquisition, and surveillance intervals**.
- **BPA 3** — The choice to **continue** endoscopic surveillance of Barrett's esophagus should be based on **shared decision-making** that examines comorbidities, life expectancy, risks, and benefits, as well as **patient preferences**.
- **BPA 4** — Utilization of **tools for prediction of dysplasia progression** in Barrett's esophagus may help contextualize the time frame for the development of adenocarcinoma **within an individual's life expectancy**.
- **BPA 5** — **Inability to tolerate endoscopic therapy, surgery, or oncologic interventions for esophageal adenocarcinoma should lead to cessation of Barrett's esophagus surveillance regardless of age.**
- **BPA 6** — The decision to **continue colorectal cancer screening in individuals >75 years old** should be **individualized**, based on an assessment of **benefits, screening history, comorbidities, and risks**.
- **BPA 7** — The decision to **continue surveillance of colorectal polyps in individuals >75 years old** should be **individualized**, based on an assessment of **benefits, polyp history, comorbidities, and risks**.
- **BPA 8** — With increasing age, colonoscopy is associated with a **higher incidence of adverse events regardless of the indication**. Therefore, the **clinical decision-making process for colonoscopy should be clearly documented in the electronic health record for individuals >75 years**.
- **BPA 9** — Whenever possible, **published studies of the use, effectiveness, deimplementation, and costs of colonoscopy in older adults** should be used to inform practice decisions for patients and clinicians.

### Life expectancy and frailty

- **A life expectancy of 10 years is necessary to derive a survival benefit from cancer screening.** Screening is generally not recommended for patients with limited life expectancy or those unlikely to tolerate cancer treatment.
- **Age alone is an insufficient marker of prognosis in older adults.** **Frailty** — physiological decline in reserve and function — is associated with increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes/death and reduced tolerance to medical and surgical interventions. **A frail 70-year-old may have a shorter life expectancy than a robust 85-year-old.**
- The **Lee–Schonberg 4- to 14-year mortality index** is the most widely used, well-validated clinical tool estimating life expectancy using age, frailty/function, and comorbidity. Online prognostic calculators (ePrognosis) and frailty tools can generate individual risk profiles.
- Patient decision aids such as the **ePrognosis Colorectal Cancer Screening Prognostic Calculator** incorporate graphics tailored to individual health status.
- Average US life expectancy in 2023: **81.1 years for women, 75.8 years for men**.
- Most guidelines recommend routine cancer screening until age 75, individualized screening between 75 and 85 years, and discontinuation after 85.
- Older adults support **individualizing decisions using age and health status**, preferring that **life expectancy not be emphasized** in the conversation. Provider scripts can help clinicians initiate discussions (Table 1 — core communication principles: anchor in patient goals and preferences; reference age-/health-based guidelines; focus on potential harms outweighing benefits; frame stopping as appropriate care and normalize the recommendation; **avoid language focusing on old age and life expectancy**; reassure patients they can still have testing if new symptoms develop).

### Barrett's esophagus

- **Nondysplastic Barrett's accounts for ~90% of the Barrett's population** and carries **~0.1% to 0.3% annual risk** of progression to adenocarcinoma. The exceedingly low risk is the foundation for counseling patients at any age.
- Patients with Barrett's **largely die of other causes** — cardiovascular disease, pulmonary diseases, other cancers. After successful endoscopic therapy of Barrett's-related neoplasia, **other-cause mortality was 40 times higher than esophageal adenocarcinoma mortality**, and was strongly predicted by the **Charlson Comorbidity Index**.
- **The only clinical risk factors that currently inform surveillance intervals are length of the Barrett's segment and presence and grade of dysplasia.** Factors associated with progression (increasing age, male gender, smoking) and protection (proton pump inhibitors, statins) can be used for patient education but **do not impact surveillance decisions**.
- **Progression in Barrett's Esophagus (PIB) score** — validated model incorporating clinical, endoscopic, and histologic variables from the baseline endoscopy; has potential to inform patients of their estimated risk of progression, **although the 2025 AGA surveillance guideline did not endorse its use**. Machine-learning algorithms applied to the electronic health record show promise and better performance than PIB, but **require external validation**.
- **Tissue systems pathology test** (immunofluorescence staining of 9 protein-based biomarkers plus nuclear atypia with quantitative image analysis): sensitivity/specificity for progression to high-grade dysplasia or adenocarcinoma was **38% and 94%** for the high-risk classification and **55% and 82%** for the high-/intermediate-risk classifications. The 2022 AGA Clinical Practice Update suggests it may be useful for risk stratification in nondysplastic Barrett's; the **2025 AGA Clinical Practice Guideline makes no recommendation for or against it**; exact role still evolving.
- **p53** abnormalities are the most studied molecular marker to date and can support a diagnosis of dysplasia and assessment of atypia on biopsies, **but not prognostication of cancer risk in nondysplastic Barrett's**.
- **Age thresholds from other bodies:** a cost-effectiveness analysis suggested the optimal age for the last surveillance endoscopy for nondysplastic Barrett's depends on sex and comorbidities, ranging from **69 years (women with severe comorbidities) to 81 years (men with none)**. The **2025 AGA Guideline recommends initiating a conversation about discontinuing endoscopic surveillance at age 75 in healthy men**. The **European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy** makes an explicit age-based recommendation for the **last surveillance endoscopy at 75 years, with individual exceptions up to age 80**.
- **BOSS randomized controlled trial (UK):** no differences in overall survival, cancer-specific survival, time to diagnosis of esophageal adenocarcinoma, or cancer stage with **every-2-year surveillance endoscopy vs endoscopy for symptoms only**. Stated limitations: enrollment of patients without intestinal metaplasia, more frequent endoscopy than guideline recommended, endoscopic evaluation in **59% of the control group**, limited newly diagnosed Barrett's, and more frequent advanced-stage adenocarcinoma in the surveillance arm than in the literature.
- Adherence to best-practice biopsy protocols is variable, especially as segment length increases; **nonadherence is associated with decreased detection of dysplasia**, which may contribute to post-endoscopy esophageal adenocarcinoma.
- **Overuse of surveillance endoscopy for Barrett's has been documented in Medicare and other populations.**
- Although diagnostic upper endoscopy is safe overall, **cardiopulmonary adverse events are more common in patients ≥65 years** and with concomitant diseases such as coronary artery disease, hypertension, diabetes, and obesity. Higher **American Society of Anesthesiologists scores and frailty** are associated with increased risk of serious adverse events.

- Yes → Life expectancy >5 years? **No → No** (harms > benefits). Yes → **Discuss**.

### Colorectal cancer screening and polyp surveillance

- Multiple screening guideline bodies recommend **individualizing** decisions in individuals **75 to 85** years old and **stopping in those >85**. Despite this, screening colonoscopy is frequently performed in patients with a **<10-year life expectancy**.
- **Prior normal screening lowers subsequent risk.** Patients with a prior normal screening colonoscopy or stool-based test have lower CRC risk than the general population — estimated at **0.39% and 0.23% at 2 years** and **1.29% and 1.21% at 8 years**, respectively.
- The majority of patients **age 76 to 85** undergoing screening have normal or nonsignificant findings (e.g., nonadvanced adenomas); **only 0.2% to 0.5% are diagnosed with CRC**, suggesting little benefit to most patients. **Advanced adenomas are found in 6% to 8%**, and whether they will cause any harm within the patient's lifetime is questionable.
- In a cohort study, **screening endoscopy after 75 years was not associated with a reduction in CRC death** among participants with significant comorbidities (hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes), and **was associated with increased non-CRC-related mortality and all-cause mortality**.
- **Risks of CRC screening** relate to the performance of colonoscopy: **14 to 28 per 1000 in older adults**, or **1.7 times higher** than in patients <60 years. **Non-GI harms** (arrhythmia, aspiration pneumonia, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke) **exceed GI-related harms** (bleeding, perforation). Risks increase **for every 5 years >75 years of age**, in patients with more comorbidities, individuals taking antithrombotic agents, and those with shorter life expectancy.
- **Harms of screening colonoscopy (~26 per 1000) likely outweigh the benefits of finding CRC (2–3 per 1000)** — underscoring the need to carefully select patients with longer life expectancy for screening after age 75.
- Overall risks of colonoscopy among those **>65 years are almost 22-fold higher** compared with younger adults.
- **Noninvasive stool tests have no direct harms** and may be safer in older adults by risk-stratifying those who are more likely to benefit from colonoscopy. Still, any older patient considering a noninvasive strategy must be **willing and healthy enough to undergo colonoscopy if the test is positive**.
  - **FIT performs well across all ages.** The **specificity of other noninvasive tests** — multitarget stool DNA (Cologuard, Cologuard Plus), stool RNA (Colosense), and blood-based tests (Guardant Shield, Freenome cell-free DNA) — **decreases with age to as low as 80%**, resulting in **higher false positivity**. **FIT may therefore be the preferred strategy over other noninvasive modalities in older adults** in whom minimizing unnecessary colonoscopy is a priority.
- **Polyp history quantified:**
  - **5-year CRC risk in patients >75 with a history of low-risk adenomas** (1 to 2 small [<1 cm] adenomas without villous features or high-grade dysplasia) is **~0.5%** — similar to those without adenomas and **lower than the general population**. Accordingly, these patients **could be managed similarly to those without polyps**, with careful consideration before ongoing surveillance after age 75.
  - **Patients with a history of advanced adenomas are at higher lifetime risk of future neoplasia.** For patients >75 with **advanced adenomas on the most recent colonoscopy**, the estimated **5-year risk of CRC is 0.8%**.
  - Among patients with advanced adenomas at baseline followed by **low-risk adenomas at first surveillance**, the risk of high-risk adenomas at second surveillance ranged **3% to 24.3%**, compared with **4% to 12.3% if no adenomas were detected at first surveillance**.
  - High-risk adenomas are a **surrogate marker not harmful in themselves** and may not have the opportunity to undergo malignant transformation within an older adult's lifetime, given the long adenoma–carcinoma sequence.
  - Rates of incident CRC and CRC death among those with and without a history of adenomas of any severity **are far surpassed by competing mortality from non-CRC death (~22% at 5 years, ~48% at 10 years)**.
- **Stool tests (i.e., FIT) are not currently recommended for polyp surveillance in the United States**; studies are underway to determine their effectiveness in older adults with a history of adenomas.
- **Stopping surveillance may be harder in practice than stopping screening** — because of standing recall systems, primary care provider deference to prior colonoscopy recommendations, and colonoscopy reports written without knowledge of the patient's future health.
- **Open access colonoscopy is a barrier:** supplanting it with face-to-face visits would create opportunities for shared decision-making and use of decision aids before older patients commit to colonoscopy. The update suggests **transitioning away from open-access colonoscopy in adults over age 75**.
- Prioritizing clinic or telehealth visits to discuss colonoscopy, including risks and patient preferences, is important and **should be documented in the electronic health record**.
- Patient attitudes: comfort around stopping polyp surveillance is associated with **trust in the physician and knowledge around benefits/harms**; discomfort with worry about CRC and perceived effectiveness of colonoscopy. It is important to elicit and document **whether patients could or would pursue treatment (e.g., colectomy, chemotherapy) if CRC were diagnosed** — in a review of patients >75 undergoing colonoscopy, **10 of 15 patients found to have CRC did not pursue treatment**.
- Decision-making should include **communication across specialties** (e.g., via e-consult) between primary care providers and gastroenterologists.
- A randomized controlled trial of older patients in Australia found that **frailty assessments had no impact on surveillance colonoscopy decision-making**.

## Contradictions / Open Questions

- **No contradiction with the ingested CRC screening guidelines.** [[usmstf-2021-crc-screening-age]] already sets stop-at-75 / individualize-to-85 / unscreened-up-to-85; this update works inside that frame and supplies the **quantities** behind the individualized decision, which USMSTF 2021 does not print.
- **Barrett's surveillance stopping age — the bodies differ, and the update says so.** A cost-effectiveness analysis gives an optimal last-surveillance age of **69 (women, severe comorbidities) to 81 (men, none)**; the **2025 AGA guideline** starts the discontinuation conversation at **75 in healthy men**; **ESGE** sets an explicit last surveillance endoscopy at **75, exceptions to 80**; this update's own figure says **consider deprioritizing at 80 in otherwise healthy patients**. All four are printed rather than reconciled.
- **The BOSS trial found no survival benefit from Barrett's surveillance**, and the update lists five limitations rather than accepting the result. It does not withdraw the recommendation to surveil — BPA 2 keeps surveillance per current best practices.
- **Risk-prediction tools are endorsed in principle but not in particular** — BPA 4 endorses using progression-prediction tools, while the text records that the 2025 AGA guideline **did not endorse** PIB, that machine-learning models need external validation, and that the 2025 guideline makes **no recommendation for or against** the tissue systems pathology test.
- **No evidence grading** on any statement (see *Grading*), so these statements carry less weight than a GRADE-rated guideline recommendation where they meet one.
- Open questions the update names: whether to continue colonoscopy after 75 **remains unknown**; a dearth of studies evaluates **deimplementation** of colonoscopy in older adults; more data are needed on long-term CRC risk across **serial** surveillance exams; effectiveness of FIT for polyp surveillance in older adults is under study.
