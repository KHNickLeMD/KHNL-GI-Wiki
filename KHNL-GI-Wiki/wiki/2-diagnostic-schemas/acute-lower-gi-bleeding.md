## Definition / Scope

**Acute lower gastrointestinal bleeding (LGIB)** = hematochezia or bright red blood per rectum originating from a **colorectal source** (distal to the ligament of Treitz, but [[small-bowel-bleeding|small bowel bleeding]] is considered a separate entity with its own algorithm and is excluded from this schema).

### Hemodynamic Instability Criteria

Any of the following suggests hemodynamically significant LGIB requiring immediate resuscitation before endoscopy. ⚠ **These figures are conventional, not guideline-set** — [[acg-2023-lgib|American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2023]] states only that blood pressure and heart rate should be optimised before endoscopic evaluation and never puts numbers on "instability." Do not confuse them with the **Oakland score** heart-rate bands below, which are a discharge-prediction instrument.

- Tachycardia (heart rate >100 bpm)
- Hypotension (systolic blood pressure [SBP] <90 mm Hg)
- Shock index (heart rate [HR] ÷ SBP) ≥1
- Orthostatic vital sign changes
- Active large-volume hematochezia with clinical signs of volume depletion

### Distinction from UGIB

Severe hematochezia with hemodynamic instability may reflect a **brisk upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) source** in 15% of cases. Features that raise suspicion for proximal source:

- History of [[peptic-ulcer-disease|peptic ulcer disease]], [[cirrhosis|decompensated liver disease]], hematemesis
- **Blood urea nitrogen:creatinine (BUN:Cr) ratio >30** — likelihood ratio (LR) 7.5 (95% confidence interval [CI] 2.8–12.0) for UGIB. Caveat: in another accuracy study the ratio discriminated upper vs lower source poorly overall (**area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) 0.63**); a higher cutoff of **35** gave specificity 90.1% / positive predictive value (PPV) 89.1%
- **BUN alone >21 mg/dL** — specificity **93.0%** for an upper source (strongest single variable in that study)
- Melena on rectal exam (argues against colonic source)
- **Blood clots in stool argue *against* UGIB** — their **presence** carries LR **0.05** (95% CI 0.01–0.38) for an upper source
- Nasogastric (NG) aspirate is unreliable — **sensitivity only 28%** for an upper source, so a negative aspirate does not exclude UGIB; **routine NG tube placement is NOT recommended** ([[acg-2023-lgib]]). A systematic review found the NG aspirate and BUN:Cr ratio equally poor (negative LR 0.6). *(The guideline also prints "negative predictive value was <1%" for the same cohort — reproduced here as printed, but read with caution: a <1% negative predictive value would mean almost every negative aspirate had an upper source, the opposite of the sentence it sits in.)*

If [[upper-gi-bleeding|UGIB]] is clinically suspected, perform **urgent [[upper-endoscopy|esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD)]] before [[colonoscopy]]**.

## Differential Diagnosis

### Most Common

| Etiology                     | Notes                                                                                                                                       |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Diverticular hemorrhage**  | Most common cause; 26–64% of LGIB depending on colonoscopy utilization; painless, arterial, large-volume; from neck or dome of diverticulum |
| **[[colon-ischemia\|Ischemic colitis]]**         | Second most common; typically left colon (watershed areas); associated with hemodynamic compromise, vasopressor use, cardiovascular disease |
| **[[hemorrhoids\|Hemorrhoids]] (internal)**   | Common but often low-volume; not covered by this schema                                                                                     |
| **[[angioectasia\|Angioectasias]] (arteriovenous malformations, AVMs)**     | Typically older patients; often right colon; associated with anticoagulant use, chronic kidney disease, aortic stenosis ([[heydes-syndrome\|Heyde syndrome]])    |
| **Colorectal neoplasia**     | Malignant lesions found in 2.5% of LGIB colonoscopies; weight loss, anemia, change in bowel habits are alarm features                       |
| **Postpolypectomy bleeding** | Immediate or delayed (up to 2 weeks); see [[polypectomy]]                                                                               |

### Colitis Subtypes

| Etiology | Distinguishing Features |
|---|---|
| **Ischemic colitis** | Acute onset, abdominal pain + hematochezia, watershed distribution (splenic flexure, sigmoid), computed tomography (CT) showing bowel wall thickening |
| **[[inflammatory-bowel-disease\|Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]]** | [[crohns-disease]] or [[ulcerative-colitis]]; chronic course with diarrhea, tenesmus; may present acutely |
| **Infectious colitis** | Fever, diarrhea, exposure history; stool cultures, [[clostridioides-difficile\|C. diff]] testing |
| **[[radiation-proctopathy\|Radiation proctopathy]]** | History of pelvic radiation; rectal bleeding, telangiectasias on endoscopy; [[argon-plasma-coagulation\|argon plasma coagulation (APC)]] is the mainstay endoscopic therapy |
| **[[nsaid-enteropathy\|Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID)-induced colitis / ulcers]]** | NSAID use history; ulcers especially in right colon and ileocecal region |

### Less Common / Rare

- Rectal / stercoral ulcers
- Dieulafoy lesion (aberrant submucosal vessel — minute mucosal defect, large bleed)
- Colorectal varices ([[portal-hypertension|portal hypertension]]; see [[variceal-upper-gi-bleeding]] for contrast)
- [[rectal-prolapse#Solitary Rectal Ulcer Syndrome|Solitary rectal ulcer syndrome]]
- Endometriosis (cyclical rectal bleeding)
- Anastomotic ulcers (post-surgical)

**No diagnosed etiology:** Found in 23% of cases in the UK national audit (low colonoscopy use); as low as 5% in high-colonoscopy cohorts.

## Diagnostic Algorithm

### Step 1: Initial Assessment (Simultaneous with Resuscitation)

- Focused history: abdominal pain, bowel habit changes, weight loss, prior gastrointestinal (GI) surgery, prior GI bleeding (GIB), cardiovascular/oncologic/renal comorbidities
- Medications: NSAIDs, antiplatelets, [[anticoagulation-gi-bleeding|anticoagulants]]
- Physical exam: vital signs, volume status, rectal exam (blood vs. melena, anorectal lesions)
- Labs: complete blood count (CBC) (hemoglobin [Hgb], platelet count), basic metabolic panel (BMP) (BUN:Cr ratio), coagulation studies (international normalized ratio [INR], activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT]), type and screen
- Intravenous (IV) access; IV fluid resuscitation with crystalloids if hemodynamically unstable

### Step 2: Risk Stratification

**Oakland Score** (validated; recommended by ACG 2023):

| Variable | Points |
|---|---|
| Age <40 yr | 0 |
| Age 40–69 yr | 1 |
| Age >70 yr | 2 |
| Female sex | 0 |
| Male sex | 1 |
| No prior LGIB admission | 0 |
| Prior LGIB admission | 1 |
| No blood on digital rectal exam (DRE) | 0 |
| Blood on DRE | 1 |
| Heart rate <70 bpm | 0 |
| Heart rate 70–89 bpm | 1 |
| Heart rate 90–109 bpm | 2 |
| Heart rate >110 bpm | 3 |
| SBP 50–89 mm Hg | 5 |
| SBP 90–119 mm Hg | 4 |
| SBP 120–129 mm Hg | 3 |
| SBP 130–159 mm Hg | 2 |
| SBP >160 mm Hg | 0 |
| Hgb 3.6–6.9 g/dL | 22 |
| Hgb 7.0–8.9 g/dL | 17 |
| Hgb 9.0–10.9 g/dL | 13 |
| Hgb 11.0–12.9 g/dL | 8 |
| Hgb 13.0–15.9 g/dL | 4 |
| Hgb >16.0 g/dL | 0 |

*(Cut-points reproduced as printed in [[acg-2023-lgib|ACG 2023]] Table 3.)*

**Oakland Score ≤8**: 95% probability of safe discharge; sensitivity **98%** for safe discharge in U.S. external validation (**AUROC 0.87**, across 140 hospitals, and despite DRE being unavailable in that data set). Extending the cut-point to **≤10** still gives **96%** sensitivity.

**"Safe discharge" is a defined composite** — absence of *all* of: rebleeding (further transfusion requirement **or** a further fall in hematocrit of **≥20% after 24 hours of clinical stability**), red-cell transfusion, therapeutic intervention to control bleeding (endoscopic, radiologic, or surgical hemostasis), in-hospital death, and readmission with further LGIB within **28 days**.

**SHA2PE Score** (alternative) — **score ≤1 means hospital-based intervention is unlikely** ("hospital-based intervention" = red-cell transfusion, endoscopic hemostatic therapy, arterial embolization, or surgery):

| Variable | Points |
|---|---|
| Systolic pressure <100 mm Hg | 1 |
| Hemoglobin <10.5 g/dL | 2 |
| Hemoglobin 10.5–12.0 g/dL | 1 |
| Antiplatelet therapy | 1 |
| Anticoagulant therapy | 1 |
| Pulse >100 bpm | 1 |
| Emergency department (ED) bleeding | 1 |

AUROC 0.83 in derivation; an external validation of 251 patients **misclassified 14 patients as low-risk who did in fact require a hospital-based intervention**.

**Neither tool is yet proven as an ED discharge strategy** — ACG notes the absence of prospective multicenter data on using either score to manage LGIB patients as outpatients.

Scores supplement but do not replace clinical judgment. Low-risk patients (Oakland ≤8) without ongoing bleeding who have had a high-quality colonoscopy within 12 months can be considered for early discharge with outpatient follow-up.

**These two instruments predict *discharge safety*. They are not the severity-prediction rules.** The older **Strate rule**, together with the Velayos predictors and the BLEED criteria, predicts severe bleeding rather than disposition, and its seven criteria and three-stratum risk table live on [[asge-2014-lgib|ASGE 2014: Role of Endoscopy in the Patient with Lower GI Bleeding]] — the same page carries that document's endoscopic device settings by lesion type. Do not substitute one class of score for the other.

### Step 3: Resuscitation and Stabilization

- **Transfusion threshold: restrictive, Hgb 7 g/dL** in **hemodynamically stable** patients (Rec 2, conditional / low-quality)
  - **8 g/dL** in stable patients with **pre-existing cardiovascular disease (CVD)** (carried over from prior guidelines; no supporting randomized controlled trial (RCT) evidence)
  - **8 g/dL can *also* be considered in acute coronary syndrome (ACS) + GIB** — data "extremely limited" in this population
  - **Exception:** the restrictive strategy applies to the stable patient — make exceptions for **significant ongoing active bleeding** or **shock**
  - ⚠ **Contradiction with [[upper-gi-bleeding]]:** [[acg-2021-ugib]] holds that ACS "must be differentiated" from pre-existing CVD and that a threshold **higher than 8 g/dL** may be considered in UGIB + ACS. [[acg-2023-lgib]] governs this page (newer, and specific to LGIB), but the two guidelines do not agree on the ACS threshold.
- Platelet transfusion: maintain >30×10⁹/L (>50×10⁹/L if endoscopic procedure planned); do NOT transfuse empirically for patients on antiplatelets
- **Do NOT give tranexamic acid** (no benefit; increased venous thromboembolism (VTE) and seizures in large RCT)

#### Anticoagulant Management in Acute LGIB

| Anticoagulant | Action |
|---|---|
| Vitamin K antagonist (VKA) / warfarin, minor bleed (Oakland ≤8) | May continue if necessary |
| VKA / warfarin, significant bleed | Hold; consider reversal only for life-threatening bleed with INR substantially supratherapeutic |
| VKA reversal | **4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) preferred over fresh frozen plasma (FFP)** (faster INR reduction, superior hemostasis, less volume overload, lower mortality) |
| Direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC), significant bleed | Hold DOAC; most resolve with cessation alone |
| DOAC reversal (life-threatening, not responding to resuscitation) | Idarucizumab for dabigatran; andexanet alfa for apixaban/rivaroxaban (if DOAC taken within 24h) |
| [[endoscopic-hemostasis\|Endoscopic hemostasis]] safe at: | INR ≤2.5 |

#### Antiplatelet Management in Acute Setting

- Aspirin for **secondary** prevention: continue during hospitalization if possible
- Aspirin for **primary** prevention: hold and consider permanent discontinuation
- Nonaspirin antiplatelets (P2Y12 inhibitors): hold for severe hematochezia; for patients with stents <1 year, use multidisciplinary approach; resume within 5 days maximum if stent <1 year

### Step 4: Determine Initial Diagnostic Strategy

![[lgib-2023-management-algorithm-17.png|700x712]]
*Figure 1 — Suggested approach to the management of patients with severe hematochezia. IVF: intravenous fluid; PRBC: packed red blood cell; UGI: upper gastrointestinal; PEG: polyethylene glycol; CTA: CT angiography. ([[acg-2023-lgib]])*

**If hemodynamically significant ongoing hematochezia:**
→ **CTA first** (Conditional recommendation)

- No bowel prep required; rapid acquisition
- Sensitivity 90%, specificity 92%
- If positive (extravasation): proceed promptly to **transcatheter arteriography (TA) + embolization** (within 90 minutes of positive CTA)
- Alternatively, colonoscopy can follow positive CTA at experienced centers
- If negative: ~80% settle spontaneously; no role for immediate TA after negative CTA; consider conservative management or elective colonoscopy

**If hemodynamically stable or bleeding has resolved:**
→ **Colonoscopy** (elective/next available inpatient opportunity)

- CTA is low yield when bleeding has clinically subsided

**If suspected proximal source (severe hematochezia with clinical red flags):**
→ **Urgent EGD first** to exclude UGIB

**[[asge-2026-lgib|ASGE 2026]] restates the same split, and it is the newer guideline** (same tier as [[acg-2023-lgib]], newer publication year — it governs where they differ):

| Patient | Initial modality |
|---|---|
| **Stable on presentation, or stable after initial resuscitation** | **Colonoscopy** suggested |
| **Ongoing hemodynamic instability** (i.e. unstable *despite* standard resuscitation) | **Contrast CT ± angiography** suggested |

*Either modality is acceptable as the initial one; Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.*

> [!pitfall] There is deliberately no definition of "stable"
> ASGE **declined to set any criterion, validated score, transfusion requirement, or ongoing-bleeding sign** as the stable/unstable discriminator, holding that no single criterion or score replaces clinical judgment. Local availability and expertise also legitimately influence the choice ([[asge-2026-lgib]]).

- **Upper endoscopy is not optional in the unstable patient:** perform it for **all patients presenting with hematochezia and initial hemodynamic instability**, to rule out an upper source — **unless there is direct imaging evidence of LGIB** ([[asge-2026-lgib]], ungraded). Yield is **15%** within 6 hours in that population (RCT, n = 85; peptic ulcers and esophagogastric varices commonest), and **11%–22%** for upper endoscopy after a negative colonoscopy. Other features favouring an upper source: **male sex, known [[peptic-ulcer-disease|peptic ulcer disease]] or [[cirrhosis]], blood urea nitrogen–to-creatinine ratio >30**.
- **Expectant management is a legitimate branch** for a stable patient without transfusion requirement — for example, stable hematochezia in someone who had a complete colonoscopy with adequate preparation 6 months prior showing only sigmoid diverticulosis ([[asge-2026-lgib]] Figure 1).
- **Nuclear scintigraphy is now a fallback only** — CT availability and performance have reduced its role to settings where CT or endoscopy are unavailable or inconclusive ([[asge-2026-lgib]]).

### Step 5: Colonoscopy Timing and Preparation

**Timing:**

- **Nonemergent inpatient colonoscopy** is recommended for most hospitalized LGIB patients (Strong recommendation)
- Urgent colonoscopy within 24 hours does NOT improve rebleeding, mortality, diagnostic yield, or need for endoscopic intervention (based on 2 RCTs and multiple meta-analyses)
- One RCT showed urgent colonoscopy associated with **increased** rebleeding (13% vs. 3%) and readmission (11% vs. 3%)
- Perform colonoscopy at next available nonurgent opportunity to minimize length of stay
- Urgent colonoscopy may be appropriate in highly selected patients with high pretest probability of stigmata of recent hemorrhage (SRH) (e.g., postpolypectomy, postprocedure) at experienced centers

> [!pearl] Stability and prep tolerance set the timing, not the clock
> **Perform colonoscopy when the patient is stable enough for the procedure and able to tolerate a full bowel preparation — regardless of timing** ([[asge-2026-lgib]], Conditional recommendation, **moderate** quality of evidence — the only moderate-certainty statement in that document).

- **The evidence behind it:** across 4 RCTs, urgent vs nonurgent colonoscopy gave no difference in 30-day mortality (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.16–3.50) or rebleeding (OR 1.23, 0.46–3.27), and no difference in need for surgery, radiologic intervention, transfusion, or adverse events. Restricting to the 24-hour cutoff, **rebleeding was higher with urgent colonoscopy (OR 3.05, 1.23–7.56)**. Urgent colonoscopy shortened stay by about **1 day** (SMD −0.96, −1.46 to −0.47) — the one argument in its favour, and one limited in practice by after-hours staffing and endoscopy access ([[asge-2026-lgib]]).
- **If you do go within 24 hours:** give a **full bowel preparation** and do the procedure **in a controlled setting with the full range of hemostasis devices available**, so a lesion found can be definitively treated at that sitting ([[asge-2026-lgib]]).

**Exceptions — colonoscopy may be omitted if:**

- Bleeding has subsided AND
- High-quality colonoscopy within 12 months with adequate prep showing diverticulosis and no colorectal neoplasia (Conditional recommendation)

**Bowel Preparation:** *(regimens and quality scales: [[bowel-preparation]])*

- Elective inpatient colonoscopy: **split-dose PEG preparation** (adequacy 86% vs. 43% with evening-before dosing)
- Urgent colonoscopy: 4–6 L PEG over 3–4 hours until rectal effluent is clear
- Low-volume preparations (1 L same-day PEG) may be equivalent — emerging data
- **Full bowel preparation unless there is strong suspicion of either a distal bleeding source or iatrogenic bleeding with known location** — [[asge-2026-lgib]], **Strong** recommendation (very low quality of evidence), and the only strong recommendation in that document. In those two exceptions, **colonoscopy or sigmoidoscopy unprepped or with enema(s)** is appropriate. Qualifying iatrogenic/distal examples: postpolypectomy, post-EMR, post-ESD bleeding; malignancy of known location; known left-sided diverticular disease; rectal trauma or ulceration.
- ⚠ **One exception to that exception — [[radiation-proctopathy|radiation proctopathy]]**: a suspected distal source, but it is generally treated with APC, so it should **still be approached only after a full bowel preparation** because of the risk of **colonic explosion** with APC at higher intestinal gas levels ([[asge-2026-lgib]]).
- Unprepared colonoscopy and routine flexible sigmoidoscopy are otherwise NOT recommended unless anorectal source is confirmed
- NG tube for prep administration: use only when necessary
  - Older ASGE guidance called high-dose **6–8 L** PEG by NG tube an effective rapid preparation for acute LGIB ([[asge-2015-bowel-preparation]]); the 2023 ACG volume above (4–6 L over 3–4 h, NG route only when necessary) is the newer guideline and governs. Case reports describe severe, life-threatening adverse events including aspiration with NG instillation; prokinetics/antiemetics and avoiding over-rapid instillation improve tolerance ([[asge-2015-bowel-preparation]]).

**Colonoscopy Technique:**

- Use clear cap (recommended — improves detection and facilitates hemostasis)
- Water-jet scope
- Careful inspection on insertion and withdrawal; aggressive washing
- Intubate terminal ileum if no colonic source identified

### Step 6: Endoscopic Treatment

![[lgib-2023-endoscopic-treatment-algorithm-18.png|700x317]]
*Figure 2 — Preferred endoscopic treatment options during colonoscopy based on etiology. EBL: endoscopic band ligation; APC: argon plasma coagulation; OTSC: over-the-scope clip; SRH: stigmata of recent hemorrhage. ([[acg-2023-lgib]])*

**Treat all SRH regardless of etiology** (Key Concept)

**Diverticular hemorrhage:**

**Choose by which side of the colon the lesion is on — [[asge-2026-lgib]], Conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence.** Band ligation or clipping "based on the location of bleeding, clinical expertise, and device availability":

| Lesion location | Suggested modality |
|---|---|
| **Transverse colon or more proximal** | **Band ligation when feasible** — through-the-scope clipping is a viable option if band ligation is not feasible |
| **Distal to the transverse colon** | **Through-the-scope clipping** |
| Clipping chosen, any location | **Direct clipping over indirect**, whenever feasible and **particularly in the proximal colon** (separate conditional recommendation) |

- **Why the split:** banding beat clipping on recurrent bleeding (OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.23–0.47; 2329 patients) and need for radiologic intervention (OR 0.43, 0.24–0.76), with no difference in need for surgery — but in the subgroup data **the whole benefit came from right-sided lesions, with no apparent benefit on the left**. A non-significant trend toward **more adverse events with banding** (OR 2.41, 0.70–8.24); its perforations **required surgery 100% of the time**.
- **Direct vs indirect clipping:** direct clipping had lower recurrent bleeding (OR 0.58, 0.44–0.77) and less radiologic intervention (OR 0.50, 0.25–0.97), with **no difference in initial hemostasis or adverse events** — which is why ASGE is more confident here than about banding. **Indirect** = closing the whole diverticular orifice, sometimes a multi-clip "zipper"; **direct** = visualizing the vessel in the dome or along the lip and clipping it. Small-mouthed diverticula can make direct clipping impossible.

> [!pitfall] Two practical barriers to banding a right-sided diverticulum
> **Device:** most band ligators load onto a gastroscope; a **colonoscope-loadable** device is **not readily available in North America**. **Technique:** banding needs the colonoscope withdrawn entirely, loaded, and reinserted to the site — hard in an angulated or redundant colon, amid active bleeding, and in higher-BMI patients. **Mark the site with a clip and/or a tattoo** before withdrawing (the tattoo is permanent and guides later endoscopic or surgical management). Attempt it **only if experienced and appropriately equipped** ([[asge-2026-lgib]]).

- ⚠ **Generalizability caveat:** every comparative study behind both recommendations was done in **Asia**, where right-sided diverticular lesions are far more common than in Western practice.
- **Thermal modalities** are possible but risky given thin-walled diverticular anatomy — apply at the **rim, not the base**, with **lower power and shorter coagulation duration** ([[asge-2026-lgib]]).
- **Over-the-scope clipping** is not extensively studied here, but early evidence appears promising; **Doppler probes**, validated for risk stratification in upper GI bleeding, have **no data to guide their use in LGIB** ([[asge-2026-lgib]]).
- [[endoscopic-band-ligation|EBL]] vs clipping per [[acg-2023-lgib]]: lower early rebleeding (8% vs. 19%) and late rebleeding (9% vs. 29%) — the newer ASGE statement above narrows that preference to right-sided lesions (technique, the device-diameter feasibility criterion, and the perforation rate are on [[endoscopic-band-ligation]])
- Bipolar/multipolar coagulation
- Initial hemostasis rates 99–100% for all modalities
- Despite hemostasis, early rebleeding 17.4%, late rebleeding 32.0%
- ⚠ **The older [[asge-2014-lgib|ASGE 2014]] guideline made dilute [[epinephrine|epinephrine]] injection *combined with* thermal coagulation or clip placement the preferred management (Rec 9, high quality)** — 1:10,000 or 1:20,000 in 1-mL aliquots up to 2 mL. [[acg-2023-lgib|ACG 2023]] is newer and lists clips, band ligation, and bipolar coagulation without epinephrine, so the modalities above are what the page asserts; epinephrine remains an adjunct for visibility and is **never used alone** (dose detail: [[epinephrine]]).

**Colonic angioectasias — and radiation proctopathy:**

- **APC is the suggested modality for vascular lesions, [[angioectasia|angioectasias]] and [[radiation-proctopathy|radiation proctopathy]] alike** ([[asge-2026-lgib]], ungraded). First-line settings 0.8–1.0 L/min, 20–40 W ([[acg-2023-lgib]]); rebleeding after APC for actively and recently bleeding angioectasias was **7.4%** in a large series.
- **Know your electrosurgical unit:** **pulsed APC modes cauterize more superficially than continuous modes.** In the thinner-walled right colon, **submucosal injection before APC** further reduces perforation risk ([[asge-2026-lgib]]).
- Clips for refractory cases.

**Postresection (postpolypectomy, post-EMR, post-ESD) bleeding:**

- **Through-the-scope clipping or thermal coagulation as the initial modalities; hemostatic powder for adjunctive use, or for refractory bleeding despite initial endoscopic therapy** ([[asge-2026-lgib]], ungraded). Hemostatic powders 97–98% initial hemostasis ([[acg-2023-lgib]]).
- **Timing:** typically **within 14 days**, rarely up to **30 days**. Predictors of finding active bleeding at colonoscopy: **left-sided lesions, pedunculated lesions, anticoagulant use.** Repeat colonoscopy for suspected delayed postpolypectomy bleeding found and treated a lesion in **22%** of cases — **number needed to treat 4.5** ([[asge-2026-lgib]]).
- Apply thermal therapy cautiously to a resection bed, **particularly in the right colon**; [[epinephrine|epinephrine]] injection is a **temporizing** measure only.

**Malignant bleeding:**

- **Hemostatic powder or the Nd:YAG laser for temporary hemostasis** — with **palliative intent or as a bridge before definitive surgical management**; endoscopic therapy here is **temporizing only** ([[asge-2026-lgib]], ungraded). Hemostatic powder appears to be the mainstay, Nd:YAG availability waning in Western practice.

### Step 7: Recurrent Bleeding

- In-hospital rebleeding rate: 13.6% at median 3 days (UK audit)
- **Repeat colonoscopy** if known source was treated and patient is stable
- **TA + embolization** for failed or refractory endoscopic therapy; previously placed clip guides focused angiography
- **Surgery**: last resort (0.2% of UK audit patients); targeted localized resection preferred; total colectomy associated with higher cardiac/renal complications and ileus
  - **The threshold, stated** ([[asge-2026-lgib]], ungraded): operate only when bleeding is **either not amenable to endoscopic or radiologic intervention**, or when **both have failed** to control it. Emergency surgery for refractory LGIB carries **30-day mortality 12.2%** — higher with older age, functional dependence and higher American Society of Anesthesiologists score; **lower** with a laparoscopic approach and a higher hematocrit at surgery. Rare scenarios where laparotomy is the *initial* modality: **aortocolonic fistula, bleeding [[meckels-diverticulum|Meckel's diverticulum]]**.
- Provocative angiography (vasodilators, anticoagulants, thrombolytics): identifies extravasation in ~44–50% of recurrent cases — limited evidence

### Step 8: Post-Discharge Antithrombotic Management

| Medication | Recommendation After Diverticular Hemorrhage |
|---|---|
| Nonaspirin NSAIDs | **Discontinue permanently** (Strong) |
| Aspirin — primary prevention | **Suggest discontinuation** (Conditional) |
| Aspirin — secondary prevention / CVD | **Suggest continuing** (Conditional) — reduces ischemic events and mortality |
| P2Y12 inhibitors | **Multidisciplinary re-evaluation required** (Strong) — hazard ratio 1.47 for recurrent hemorrhage |
| Anticoagulants (all) | **Resume after LGIB cessation** (Strong) — reduces thromboembolism and mortality; optimal timing within 7 days |

## Key Tests

### Colonoscopy

- Diagnostic test of choice for most admitted LGIB
- Diagnostic yield 68–79% (definitive or presumptive etiology)
- SRH identified in 15–31%; active bleeding in 3.8%
- Endoscopic intervention rate 3–6% in Western cohorts
- **Timing: nonemergent (next available opportunity)** for most patients

### CT Angiography (CTA)

- Sensitivity 90%, specificity 92%
- **First-line for hemodynamically significant ongoing hematochezia**
- No bowel prep required; rapid acquisition (minutes)
- Positive study (extravasation): immediate TA ± embolization within 90 minutes
- Negative study: low yield for immediate TA; 80% settle spontaneously
- Factors increasing positivity: shock index ≥1, >3 units PRBC/day, NSAID/DOAC use, CTA within 4 hours of hematochezia

### Transcatheter Arteriography (TA) / Embolization

- Indicated for positive CTA; must be performed within 90 minutes of CTA for best yield
- Embolization: superselective with microcatheter; agents: microcoils, n-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA), ethylene-vinyl alcohol copolymer
- Technical success: 98%; 30-day rebleeding: 15.7%; mortality: 12.7%

### Nuclear Scintigraphy (99mTc-labeled RBC scan)

- Rapidly diminishing role — largely replaced by CTA
- Limitations: long study duration; imprecise localization
- Reserve for situations where CTA is unavailable or contraindicated (contrast nephropathy concern)

### Upper Endoscopy (EGD)

- Indicated when UGIB is clinically suspected (history, BUN:Cr, vital sign pattern)
- Should precede colonoscopy in these cases

## Red Flags / Alarm Features

### Immediate / Hemodynamic

*Use the same numbers as [[#Hemodynamic Instability Criteria]] — HR **>100 bpm**, SBP **<90 mm Hg**, shock index ≥1 — and read them with the caveat stated there: they are conventional, not guideline-set, and are not the Oakland score's heart-rate bands.*

- Tachycardia / hypotension / shock index ≥1 as above
- Ongoing large-volume hematochezia despite initial resuscitation
- Requirement for >3 units PRBC/day
- Hemodynamic instability not responding to IV fluids

### Clinical Predictors of High-Risk Hospital Course

- Oakland score >8 — i.e. **not** in the low-risk band, so early discharge is not supported. ⚠ Read this as the *absence* of the discharge criterion, **not** as a mandate to admit: [[acg-2023-lgib|ACG 2023]] makes no admission recommendation off the Oakland score, and neither Oakland nor the Strate score is proven as an ED disposition strategy (see [[#Step 2: Risk Stratification]])
- Age >70 years
- Male sex
- Prior LGIB admission
- Antiplatelet or anticoagulant use
- Diverticular etiology (highest rebleeding risk)
- Melena on rectal exam (increases suspicion for UGIB)
- Elevated BUN or BUN:Cr ratio

### Alarm Features Suggesting Alternative Diagnoses

- Unintentional weight loss → colorectal neoplasia
- Change in bowel habits, [[iron-deficiency-anemia|iron deficiency anemia]] → [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]] or [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|IBD]]
- Abdominal pain preceding hematochezia → ischemic colitis, IBD, infectious colitis
- Recent colonoscopy with polypectomy → postpolypectomy bleeding
- History of pelvic radiation → radiation proctopathy
- Hematemesis or coffee-ground emesis → UGIB source
- Stigmata of liver disease, history of varices → portal hypertensive source (see [[variceal-upper-gi-bleeding]])
- New-onset LGIB after starting oral anticoagulant → increased risk of underlying [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer]]

### Rebleeding Risk Indicators

- Diverticular etiology: early rebleeding up to 24% within 30 days; late recurrence 15.7% at 5 years
- Older age
- Hemodynamic instability at presentation
- Ongoing NSAID or antiplatelet use after discharge
- In-hospital rebleeding: median 3 days post-presentation; rate 13.6% overall
