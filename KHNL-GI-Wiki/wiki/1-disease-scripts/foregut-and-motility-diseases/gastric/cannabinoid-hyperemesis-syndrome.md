Chronic [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|disorder of gut–brain interaction (DGBI)]] of cyclic vomiting, nausea, and abdominal pain arising from chronic (years), heavy (daily/near-daily) cannabis use; predominantly male. Rising and underrecognized ([[aga-2024-chs]]). Included in the Rome classification of DGBI since Rome IV (2016).

**Where it sits in the classification — the two sources differ, and the difference is not trivial:**

| Source | Placement |
|---|---|
| [[aga-2024-chs]] / [[aga-2024-cvs]] (2024 Clinical Practice Updates [CPUs]) | Treated as a **subtype of [[cyclic-vomiting-syndrome\|cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS)]]** — cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS) criteria are defined by reference to CVS-like episodes |
| [[rome-v-2026-dgbi]] (2026, newer) | A **separate adult entity, B2c**, alongside B2a chronic nausea vomiting syndrome and B2b CVS under "Nausea and vomiting disorders." In the **pediatric** classification it remains nested under CVS (**G3b.i**) |

Rome V is the newer consensus, so in adults **treat CHS as its own entity (B2c)** — the framing used below. Whether CHS is a CVS subtype or a distinct entity is still debated; the older subtype view describes the practical clinical relationship, since the workup and much of the therapy are shared ([[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]]).

## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

Suspect in any patient with **chronic nausea/vomiting + cannabis use**. Diagnosis is clinical; no confirmatory test.

**Rome V criteria (B2c)** ([[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]]) — must include **all**:

> 1. **Stereotypical episodic vomiting resembling cyclic vomiting syndrome** in onset, duration and frequency
> 2. **Presentation after prolonged (1 year) and excessive (≥4 days per week or ≥15 doses per week, or both) cannabis use**
> 3. **Relief of vomiting episodes by sustained cessation of cannabis use — at least 6 months or 3 typical emetic cycles**
>
> *Supportive remark:* may be associated with pathologic bathing behavior (prolonged hot baths or showers).

**What changed** — Rome V revised the Venkatesan criteria that [[aga-2024-chs]] carries, to quantify the use profile and the abstinence duration:

| Element | AGA 2024 CPU (Venkatesan) | Rome V 2026 (operative) |
|---|---|---|
| Vomiting pattern | Stereotypical episodic vomiting resembling CVS in **onset**, frequency **≥3 times annually** | Stereotypical episodic vomiting resembling CVS in **onset, duration and frequency** (no annual episode count) |
| Cannabis exposure | Use **>1 year** before symptom onset; **>4 times per week** on average | Prolonged (**1 year**) and excessive — **≥4 days per week or ≥15 doses per week, or both** |
| Cessation response | Resolution after abstinence **≥6 months**, or **≥ the total duration of 3 typical vomiting cycles** | Relief of episodes after sustained cessation — **at least 6 months or 3 typical emetic cycles** |

> [!pitfall] Cannabis use in a cyclic vomiter is not CHS
> **Up to 80% of CVS patients use cannabis to relieve vomiting or abdominal pain during acute attacks and likely do not have CHS** ([[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]]). The use must be prolonged and heavy, and must *precede* symptom onset ([[aga-2024-cvs]]).

- **Criterion 3 is the practical roadblock, not a formality** — **only 19% of patients were able to abstain from cannabis for >4 weeks** in one analysis, so the confirmatory step often cannot be completed. Offer abortive and preventive therapy regardless of cannabis use or diagnostic certainty ([[aga-2024-cvs]]).
- **Not every case is episodic** — up to **18%** of cases in one review did not show episodic emesis, so not all CHS mimics CVS.
- **Compulsive hot-water bathing** (long hot baths/showers) relieves symptoms: reported in **71%** of CHS cases, and **92%** report it reduces vomiting and pain during attacks. Supportive but **not pathognomonic** — also reported in CVS, where it relieves ~48% of non-cannabis users.
- Factors associated with the diagnosis in one review: cannabis use >1 year · daily use · at least weekly use · symptom resolution with abstinence · compulsive bathing to relieve symptoms · **male sex** · **age <50 years**.
- **Complications:** dehydration, acute kidney injury, electrolyte/metabolic imbalance; rarely pneumothorax/pneumomediastinum; death.

### Severity Assessment

- **Neither source gives a severity score or stratum for CHS** — unlike CVS, which has American Neurogastroenterology and Motility Society (ANMS)–Cyclic Vomiting Syndrome Association (CVSA) mild vs moderate–severe criteria gating prophylaxis.
- The framework on offer is **phasic, not graded**: CHS runs through the same four CVS phases (inter-episodic → prodromal → emetic → recovery), used to time therapy — abortive treatment in the prodrome, supportive care in the emetic phase. The phases and their management live on **[[cyclic-vomiting-syndrome#Phases|cyclic vomiting syndrome]]**.
- **Attack intensity gauges the repletion need:** the acute emetic phase lasts **a few hours to 1 week**, with vomiting **>20 times daily** alongside abdominal pain, diarrhea and autonomic symptoms ([[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]]).

| Feature | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|

| Share of CVS patients with CHS — all / cannabis users | **18%** / **39%** | [[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]] |
| Male | **69%** (271-case systematic review) | [[aga-2024-chs]] |
| Age | mean **30 years**; median **28 years** at diagnosis | [[aga-2024-chs]] / [[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]] |
| Diagnostic delay >4 years | **40%** of patients | [[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]] |
| Cannabis use before symptom onset | mean **6.6 years** | [[aga-2024-chs]] |
| Use frequency | daily in **68%**; at least daily **61%**, weekly **19%** | [[aga-2024-chs]] / [[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]] |
| Screen positive for anxiety / depression | **59%** / **68%** | [[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]] |

- CHS diagnoses **doubled from 2000 to 2015**, attributed to cannabis products higher in Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) content.

### Pathophysiology

- THC acts on **CB1 receptors**, densely expressed in the **dorsal vagal complex** — the emetic neurocircuitry — and throughout the gut myenteric/submucosal neurons. Loss of endocannabinoid negative feedback on the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis increases vagal discharge ([[aga-2024-chs]]).
- Peripheral CB1 activation inhibits gastric acid secretion, gastric emptying, and distal gut motility.
- THC also binds **transient receptor potential vanilloid type 1 (TRPV1)** — the rationale for topical capsaicin and, plausibly, for hot showers.
- Mechanisms are likely multifactorial and THC contributes **both antiemetic and proemetic** actions. Postulated: down-regulation, desensitization or internalization of CB1 receptors in the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system, plus altered TRPV1 function ([[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]]).

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[nausea-and-vomiting]].*

Outpatient differential diagnosis (DDx) after excluding structural disease ([[aga-2024-chs]]):
- [[cyclic-vomiting-syndrome]]
- [[rumination-syndrome]]
- [[gastroparesis]]
- [[nausea-and-vomiting-of-pregnancy|pregnancy]]
- migraine
- functional chronic nausea and vomiting syndrome

**Emergency department (ED) priority** — exclude life-threatening mimics first: acute abdomen, bowel obstruction, [[acute-mesenteric-ischemia|mesenteric ischemia]], [[acute-pancreatitis|pancreatitis]], myocardial infarction.

## Diagnostics

- **Clinical diagnosis**; no confirmatory test. **Diagnostic testing is rarely indicated** in the absence of complications (e.g. hemorrhage) ([[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]]).
- There is **no consensus on a minimum test set** — evaluation is limited, individualized and driven by clinical suspicion, aimed only at excluding mimics ([[aga-2024-chs]]).
- Anatomic evaluation: [[upper-endoscopy|upper endoscopy]] or computed tomography (CT) of abdomen or pelvis to exclude obstruction.
- **Brain imaging** if focal neurologic symptoms and signs.
- **Urine drug testing for THC metabolites** — consider in patients who do not admit to cannabis use, but **no metabolite profile is specific for CHS**. Metabolites persist **≥1 month** and cannabis-induced brain impairment may last longer, which is why the diagnostic abstinence period is set at 6 months or 3 typical cycles.

## Therapeutics

### Abortive Therapy

| Agent | Detail | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Topical capsaicin 0.1% cream** — upper abdomen | TRPV1 agonist; shortens episodes and reduces antiemetic requirement | Several reports / case series |
| **Haloperidol** or **droperidol** (parenteral dopamine D2 antagonists) | Abort episodes | Uncontrolled series + one controlled trial |
| **Benzodiazepines** (lorazepam, alprazolam) | Relief during episodes | Small series |
| **Promethazine, olanzapine, ondansetron** | Acute and short-term antiemetic control | Case series, small trials |
| **Opioids — avoid** | Worsen nausea; high risk of addiction | — |

Neither source gives CHS-specific antiemetic doses; the dosed abortive and supportive regimens are on [[cyclic-vomiting-syndrome#Therapeutics|cyclic vomiting syndrome]].

### Preventive and Long-Term Therapy

- **Cannabis cessation is the treatment.** Counseling to achieve cessation is considered necessary for successful management; cessation leads to CHS resolution and resuming cannabis promotes symptom resurgence.
- **[[brain-gut-behavioral-therapy|Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)]]** may help reduce cannabis dependence in CHS.

> [!pitfall] Do not tell them to stop "cold turkey"
> Cannabinoid withdrawal syndrome is common after heavy, prolonged use — **pooled prevalence 47%** across 47 studies of 23,518 cannabis users, higher with daily use and with concurrent tobacco or other substance use disorder. Abrupt cessation brings significant withdrawal symptoms and a **high frequency of recidivism**, and cannabis-specific withdrawal resources are limited ([[aga-2024-chs]]).

| Preventive agent | Dose | What the sources say |
|---|---|---|
| **Tricyclic antidepressant (TCA)** — amitriptyline | Start **25 mg at bedtime**; titrate in increments **each week** to the minimal effective dose, **75–100 mg at bedtime**, monitoring efficacy and adverse effects closely | The 2024 AGA CPU (US — governs) makes amitriptyline plus cessation counseling the long-term mainstay and reports it efficacious in **~70%** with minor adverse events. ⚠ Rome V 2026 (international) states CHS **responds poorly to preventive TCA therapy** |
| **Olanzapine** | Dose not given by either source | Effective prophylaxis in **one small series** ([[rome-v-2026-gastroduodenal-disorders]]) |

- **Psychiatric co-management** with psychology/psychiatry for refractory disease or extensive psychiatric comorbidity; combining evidence-based psychosocial intervention with pharmacotherapy may be necessary.
- **Pregnancy and lactation:** cannabis is used to relieve [[nausea-and-vomiting-of-pregnancy|nausea and vomiting of pregnancy]], especially in more severe cases, but there is also evidence it **aggravates** it. Given adverse perinatal outcomes with heavy use, advise refraining from cannabis during pregnancy and lactation.
- **Investigational:** preliminary evidence from 2 small placebo-controlled randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in cannabinoid use disorder suggests fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH) inhibitors and cannabidiol (CBD) can reduce cannabis use — larger studies needed.

**Strategies that lack scientific validation** — commonly suggested, but the 2024 CPU explicitly names them as unvalidated: switching to a **lower Δ⁹-THC / higher CBD** formulation · switching to **edible** forms · avoiding **Δ⁹-THC concentrates**.

**Course:** a single-centre experience reported **>40% of patients could stop all CHS treatment over time**, but recidivism was high. In survey data, many patients remain uncertain that cannabis is the cause and attribute symptoms to food or alcohol — expect this when counselling.
