Acute hepatic porphyrias (AHP) are rare inborn errors of heme metabolism in which partially defective heme-synthesis enzymes cause accumulation of the porphyrin precursors **δ-aminolevulinic acid (ALA)** and **porphobilinogen (PBG)**, producing acute neurovisceral attacks. Consider AHP in any patient — especially a woman aged 15–50 years — with unexplained recurrent severe abdominal pain not ascribable to common causes. ([[aga-2023-acute-hepatic-porphyria]])

## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis
- **Suspicion is the whole diagnosis** — average delay ~15 years from symptom onset to diagnosis in the US and Europe.
- Classic attack: **severe pain, usually abdominal and generalized, without peritoneal signs or abnormalities on cross-sectional imaging.**
- Other neurovisceral features: nausea, vomiting, constipation, muscle weakness, neuropathy, tachycardia, systemic arterial hypertension. Attacks may include hyponatremia, hypomagnesemia, seizures.
- ~90% of symptomatic patients are **women aged 15–50 y**; attacks rare before menses or after menopause. Some luteal-phase attacks are progesterone-triggered.
- **Whom to screen (Best Practice Advice [BPA] 1):** women 15–50 y with unexplained, recurrent severe abdominal pain without a clear etiology after an initial workup.
- Diagnose **biochemically first**, then confirm genetically.
- Low penetrance explains the gap between gene and disease: pathogenic-variant prevalence for acute intermittent porphyria (AIP) is **1 in 1300–1785**, vs symptomatic AHP ~**1 in 100,000**.

### Severity Assessment
- **>90% of symptomatic patients have only 1 or a few lifetime attacks.**
- **Recurrent attacks = ≥4 attacks/year**, seen in 3%–5% of symptomatic patients — the threshold that drives prophylaxis (BPA 7).
- Recurrent attackers: >50% report chronic neurologic symptoms, 35% carry a neuropathy diagnosis, quality of life is markedly impaired, and ALA/PBG are often elevated at baseline between attacks.
- **Asymptomatic high excretors** (pathogenic variant + elevated ALA/PBG, never an attack) may still be at increased risk of an induced attack, chronic renal or hepatic injury, and [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)]] — they merit annual monitoring.
- **Attack severity sets the treatment threshold:** an attack severe enough to **require hospital admission** is the trigger for intravenous (IV) [[hemin]] (BPA 4).

### Classification / Typing
Typing is by the deficient enzyme and its gene, confirmed by sequencing. What changes management: inheritance pattern, whether PBG is elevated (all but 5-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase deficiency porphyria [ALAD]), and cutaneous involvement (hereditary coproporphyria [HCP] and variegate porphyria [VP] only).

| AHP type | Gene | Inheritance | Age at presentation | Sex predominance | Symptoms and signs | Elevated precursor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **AIP** — most common | *HMBS* | Autosomal dominant | 15–50 y | F > M | Acute | ALA **and** PBG |
| **HCP** | *CPOX* | Autosomal dominant | 15–50 y | F > M | Acute, cutaneous | ALA **and** PBG |
| **VP** | *PPOX* | Autosomal dominant | 15–50 y | F > M | Acute, cutaneous | ALA **and** PBG |
| **ALAD** — very rare (<12 reported cases worldwide) | *ALAD* | Autosomal **recessive** | Childhood–adulthood | M > F | Acute | **ALA only** (PBG normal) |

*The four acute hepatic porphyrias. Inheritance, age, sex and symptom columns from Table 1 of the AGA 2023 Clinical Practice Update; gene and precursor columns from its text ([[aga-2023-acute-hepatic-porphyria]]).*

- AIP, HCP and VP pathogenic variants (collectively "AHP pathogenic variants") reduce the corresponding enzyme's activity by **≥50%**.
- Penetrance: ~**1% of AIP gene carriers** become symptomatic, rising to **>20% in families with symptomatic patients**; HCP and VP have lower penetrance than AIP.
- Four other porphyrias present primarily with cutaneous symptoms; the AGA update does not address them.

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[nausea-and-vomiting]].* AHP is considered only once an initial workup leaves recurrent severe abdominal pain unexplained.

- **Common causes of acute or recurrent abdominal pain** — AHP is suspected when severe pain occurs **without peritoneal signs or cross-sectional imaging abnormalities** and is not ascribable to a common etiology.
- **Lead poisoning and hereditary tyrosinemia** — if **only ALA is elevated**, check a **lead level and urine organic acids** to rule these out before accepting ALAD porphyria.
- **Secondary porphyrinurias** — mild, nondiagnostic elevations in urinary porphyrins are frequently misread as AHP and are a major source of **overdiagnosis**; urine porphyrins alone must never be the screening test.
- **Cutaneous porphyrias** — separated from the acute types by the biochemical pattern (marked ALA/PBG elevation).

### Diagnostic Workflow

### Biochemical Testing
- **Initial test (BPA 2): random (spot) urine ALA, PBG, and creatinine.** A 24-hour collection is **not** recommended — levels are high enough that a spot sample suffices.
- **Normalize to urine creatinine**, to adjust for differences in urinary concentration.
- **Diagnostic threshold:** during an acute attack both ALA and PBG are elevated **at least 5-fold the upper limit of normal (ULN)**; the workflow figure gives the cutoff as **urine ALA/PBG >10 mg/g creatinine**. The update's testing table words the same threshold as ">5-fold ULN".
- ALA and PBG can be measured in **urine or plasma**; urine is the practical test.
- **ALAD exception:** in ALAD porphyria **only ALA** is elevated (PBG normal). PBG is elevated in every other AHP type.
- **Never screen with urine porphyrins alone** — ALA and PBG are porphyrin *precursors* and are not captured by porphyrin assays.
- Both are measurable with high sensitivity and specificity, but assays run only at large reference laboratories (**results in 1–2 weeks**). Rapid qualitative urine PBG tests exist (Hoesch, Watson-Schwartz; a newer US-approved rapid PBG test, Teco Diagnostics).

### Timing of Testing
- Testing is **most informative during an acute attack**, while the patient is symptomatic.
- Unless hemin has already been given, ALA/PBG can be tested days after an attack: in **AIP** they remain elevated for **months to years**; in **HCP/VP** they fall quickly.
- In asymptomatic sporadic AIP, **15%–44% have normal urine ALA and PBG** (the same or higher for HCP/VP) → confirmation may require **repeat testing during an acute attack**.
- In patients with frequent symptoms suggestive of attacks, **normal ALA and PBG likely rule out AHP** as the cause.

> [!pitfall] Send the urine before the first dose of hemin
> A random urine for ALA, PBG and creatinine must be collected **before** hemin is started — hemin normalizes the precursors and the diagnostic window closes. In already-confirmed AHP, hemin may still be started on empirical grounds, since rapid ALA/PBG assays are rarely available ([[aga-2023-acute-hepatic-porphyria]]).

### Genetic Confirmation
- **BPA 3:** once biochemical testing is positive, confirm the specific type by genetic testing — sequence ***ALAD, HMBS, CPOX, PPOX*** → ALAD, AIP, HCP, VP respectively.
- Whole-gene sequencing identifies **95%–99%** of cases.
- **Screen first-degree relatives** by genetic testing once the familial variant is identified; counsel carriers.
- Gold standard for confirmation, but **not for initial screening** — most carriers never have an attack. Even with a confirmed variant, attributing symptoms to AHP benefits from biochemical confirmation.

## Therapeutics

### Acute Attack Management
- **IV [[hemin]] (BPA 4)** for attacks severe enough to require hospital admission:
  - **3–4 mg/kg body weight, once daily, typically 4 days** (the update's summary gives 3–5 days).
  - Give into a **high-flow central vein** — peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) or central port — because of potential thrombophlebitis. **Heme bound to human serum albumin** is preferred (heme stabilization, less venous irritation).
  - Rapidly down-regulates hepatic aminolevulinate synthase 1 (ALAS1) → falling ALA and PBG. **Symptom relief typically takes 48–72 h**; neurologic recovery varies. Timely hemin normalizes ALA/PBG, improves symptoms, and lowers the risk of long-term neurologic complications.
- **Supportive care (BPA 5):**
  - **Stop cytochrome P450 (CYP450)–inducing precipitants** — they directly up-regulate ALAS1 messenger RNA.
  - Treat severe pain and nausea **aggressively** with analgesics and antiemetics.
  - **IV carbohydrate loading ~300 g/day** in adults (fasting up-regulates ALAS1).
  - Monitor electrolytes; **correct hyponatremia slowly** (hypovolemia plus syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion [SIADH]) and correct **hypomagnesemia**; manage systemic arterial hypertension and tachycardia.

> [!pitfall] Most anticonvulsants are contraindicated in a porphyric seizure
> **Avoid barbiturates, hydantoins, carbamazepine and valproic acid.** **Magnesium sulfate, benzodiazepines and levetiracetam** appear safe in AHP ([[aga-2023-acute-hepatic-porphyria]]).

### Trigger Avoidance
- **BPA 6:** counsel all patients to avoid identifiable triggers — **alcohol**, **porphyrinogenic (CYP450-inducing) medications**, sex hormones (particularly progesterone), acute illness or infection, physical or psychological stress, tobacco, caloric deprivation.
- The update prints **no general list of safe and unsafe drugs**; it directs prescribers to the online databases at porphyria.org and drugs-porphyria.org.
- Menstrual-cycle–associated attacks may be treated with hormonal suppression (e.g., gonadotropin-releasing hormone [GnRH] agonists) — **limited success**.

### Prophylaxis for Recurrent Attacks
- **BPA 7:** for recurrent attacks (**≥4 per year**), consider outpatient **prophylactic heme therapy** or **[[givosiran]]**.
- **Prophylactic IV [[hemin]]** — off-label and commonly used. Effective at stopping attacks; **effectiveness at preventing them is less established.** Complications: indwelling central venous catheters, infection, **[[iron-overload-and-iron-metabolism|iron overload]]**.
- **[[givosiran]]** — an ALAS1-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) covalently linked to N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) and taken up selectively by hepatocytes.
  - **2.5 mg/kg subcutaneously once monthly**, dosed on actual body weight ([[givlaari-2025-givosiran-label]]).
  - Prescribe **only for recurrent attacks that are both biochemically and genetically confirmed**.
  - **Avoid in women who are pregnant or planning a pregnancy** (limited safety data).
  - Approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for **adults**; by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) for **age ≥12 y** in the European Union (EU).
  - Screen and monitor liver enzymes, serum blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine, homocysteine, amylase and lipase. May worsen estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and renal function in a subset of patients.
  - Dose reduction after a transaminase interruption, injection technique, the pancreatitis warning and the CYP1A2/CYP2D6 interaction are on the drug page.

> [!pitfall] Pregnancy cuts both ways in acute hepatic porphyria
> Givosiran is avoided in pregnancy, but untreated disease is not benign: **attacks occur in 24%–95% of pregnant AHP patients, with maternal mortality reported at 2%–42%**, and pregnancy carries higher rates of spontaneous abortion, hypertension and low birth weight ([[givlaari-2025-givosiran-label]]). Plan the pregnancy with hepatology and obstetrics rather than simply stopping prophylaxis.

### Liver Transplantation
- **BPA 8:** [[liver-transplantation|liver transplantation]] is limited to **intractable symptoms with significantly decreased quality of life, refractory to pharmacotherapy.**
- **Curative** — corrects the hepatic *HMBS* deficiency, normalizes urinary ALA and PBG, eliminates attacks; some improvement in chronic symptoms, including peripheral neuropathy.
- European Liver Transplant Registry (38 recipients): **1-year survival 92%, 5-year survival 82%**; poorer outcomes with severe neuropathy and/or renal dysfunction at baseline.
- **Genetically screen related living donors** — an asymptomatic *HMBS*-variant donor may result in a poor post-transplant outcome.
- Transplant at a center experienced with AHP, including preoperative management with hemin.

### Long-Term Monitoring & Surveillance

| When | Tests |
|---|---|
| **All patients on treatment, annually** | Liver enzymes; creatinine and eGFR |
| **From age 50 y, every 6 mo** | Liver ultrasound (the update's testing table also lists α-fetoprotein [AFP]) |
| **Add on prophylactic hemin** | Iron, ferritin |
| **Add on [[givosiran]]** | Comprehensive metabolic panel, plasma homocysteine, urinalysis, urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio, B12/folate, amylase/lipase |

*Monitoring and surveillance panel, from Table 2 of the AGA 2023 Clinical Practice Update ([[aga-2023-acute-hepatic-porphyria]]). Givosiran labs: before the first dose and again just before each monthly injection for 3 months; if results are stable and the drug is well tolerated, repeat every 3 months for the next year and at least every 6 months thereafter.*

- **Liver disease (BPA 9):** monitor **annually** in patients on treatment. Liver enzymes are elevated in ~13% of patients during acute attacks and in some without symptoms (28% at baseline in ENVISION). The cause of enzyme elevation and fibrosis in AHP is not well defined, so [[abnormal-liver-chemistries|abnormal liver enzymes]] should prompt investigation for an alternative etiology — [[nafld-masld|nonalcoholic steatohepatitis]], iron overload, other causes; [[liver-biopsy|liver biopsy]] may help.
- **On prophylactic hemin at least monthly** (hemin is 9% iron by weight → secondary iron overload with liver fibrosis): **ferritin and iron every 3–6 months**; **ferritin >1000 ng/mL → consider therapeutic phlebotomy.**
- **On [[givosiran]]:** alanine aminotransferase (ALT) elevation **>3× ULN** has been reported in the **first 3–6 months** → **monitor liver enzymes monthly** through that window, then taper to twice yearly if normal. The product label asks for liver tests before the first dose and monthly for the first 6 months ([[givlaari-2025-givosiran-label]]).
- **[[hcc-surveillance|HCC surveillance]] (BPA 10):** risk **1.5%–1.8%** → **liver ultrasound every 6 months beginning at age 50 y, regardless of symptom severity.** Female predominance 2.5×; HCC (and [[cholangiocarcinoma]]) may occur **without fibrosis or [[cirrhosis]]**, and has been reported in asymptomatic patients. The role of **α-fetoprotein (AFP)** is unclear — it is not routinely elevated even with high tumor burden.
- **Chronic kidney disease (BPA 11):** **serum creatinine and eGFR at least annually**, and more frequently on givosiran. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was reported in 29% and hypertension in 43% in the US Porphyrias Consortium; porphyria-associated kidney disease occurs in up to 59% of symptomatic AIP, with eGFR falling ~1 mL/min/1.73 m²/year. **Optimize control of systemic arterial hypertension.** Kidney transplantation is the treatment of choice for end-stage renal disease (ESRD) — ALA and PBG rise significantly between dialysis sessions, and transplant improves AIP symptoms through increased clearance.
- **Counseling (BPA 12):** cover neuropathy, CKD, hypertension, HCC and the need for long-term monitoring — including asymptomatic high excretors.
