Acute hepatic porphyrias (AHP) are rare inborn errors of heme metabolism in which partially defective heme-synthesis enzymes cause accumulation of the porphyrin precursors **δ-aminolevulinic acid (ALA)** and **porphobilinogen (PBG)**, producing acute neurovisceral attacks. Consider AHP in any patient — especially a woman aged 15–50 years — with unexplained recurrent severe abdominal pain not ascribable to common causes. ([[aga-2023-acute-hepatic-porphyria]])

## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis
- **Clinical suspicion is the key to diagnosis** — average delay ~15 years from symptom onset. ([[aga-2023-acute-hepatic-porphyria]])
- Classic presentation: **acute attacks of severe pain, usually abdominal and generalized, without peritoneal signs or abnormalities on cross-sectional imaging.**
- Associated neurovisceral features: nausea, vomiting, constipation, muscle weakness, neuropathy, tachycardia, systemic arterial hypertension; attacks may include hyponatremia, hypomagnesemia, and seizures.
- Epidemiology: ~90% of symptomatic patients are **women aged 15–50 y**; attacks rare before menses / after menopause. Some luteal-phase attacks are progesterone-triggered.
- Prevalence: symptomatic AHP ~1 in 100,000; population genetics put AIP pathogenic-variant prevalence at **1 in 1300–1785** (much higher than symptomatic prevalence → low penetrance).
- **Whom to screen (BPA 1):** women 15–50 y with unexplained, recurrent severe abdominal pain without a clear etiology after an initial workup.
- Diagnosis is made **biochemically first**, then confirmed genetically (see Diagnostics).

### Severity Assessment
- **Disease-course strata:** >90% of symptomatic patients have only 1 or a few lifetime attacks; **3%–5% have recurrent attacks, defined as ≥4 attacks/year** — this threshold drives prophylaxis and givosiran eligibility.
- Recurrent attackers: >50% report chronic neurologic symptoms and 35% carry a neuropathy diagnosis; markedly impaired quality of life; ALA/PBG often elevated at baseline between attacks.
- **Asymptomatic high excretors** (pathogenic variant + elevated ALA/PBG, no attacks) may still be at increased risk of induced attacks, chronic renal/hepatic injury, and HCC.
- Attacks requiring hospital admission define the threshold for IV [[hemin|hemin]] therapy (BPA 4).

### Classification / Typing
- Four AHP types; typing is by the deficient enzyme / gene and confirmed by sequencing. Clinically meaningful distinctions: inheritance pattern, whether PBG is elevated (all except ALAD), and cutaneous involvement (HCP/VP only).

| AHP type | Gene | Inheritance | Age at presentation | Sex predominance | Symptoms/signs | Elevated precursor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **AIP** (acute intermittent porphyria) — most common | *HMBS* | Autosomal dominant | 15–50 y | F > M | Acute neurovisceral | ALA **and** PBG |
| **HCP** (hereditary coproporphyria) | *CPOX* | Autosomal dominant | 15–50 y | F > M | Acute + cutaneous | ALA **and** PBG |
| **VP** (variegate porphyria) | *PPOX* | Autosomal dominant | 15–50 y | F > M | Acute + cutaneous | ALA **and** PBG |
| **ALAD** (5-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase deficiency) — very rare (<12 cases worldwide) | *ALAD* | Autosomal **recessive** | Childhood–adulthood | M > F | Acute | **ALA only** (PBG normal) |

*Table 1 — Summary of the acute hepatic porphyrias. ([[aga-2023-acute-hepatic-porphyria]])*

- AIP/HCP/VP pathogenic variants (collectively "AHP pathogenic variants") reduce the corresponding enzyme's activity by ≥50%.
- Penetrance: ~1% of AIP gene carriers become symptomatic (>20% in families with symptomatic patients); HCP and VP have lower penetrance than AIP.
- Four additional **cutaneous** porphyrias are not covered by this source.

## Differential Diagnosis

*The AGA CPU frames AHP as a consideration only after an initial workup excludes common causes of recurrent severe abdominal pain (no dedicated abdominal-pain diagnostic schema exists in this wiki).*

- **Common causes of recurrent/acute abdominal pain** — AHP is suspected when severe pain occurs **without peritoneal signs or cross-sectional imaging abnormalities** and is not ascribable to common etiologies.
- **Secondary porphyrinurias** — mild, nondiagnostic elevations in urinary porphyrins are frequently misread as AHP and cause **overdiagnosis**; urine porphyrins alone must not be used to screen.
- **Cutaneous porphyrias** and other causes of neuropathy/hyponatremia enter the differential but are distinguished by the biochemical pattern (marked ALA/PBG elevation) below.

## Diagnostics

### Biochemical Testing
- **Screening/initial test of choice (BPA 2):** **random (spot) urine ALA, PBG, and creatinine.** A 24-hour collection is **not** recommended.
- **Diagnostic cutoff:** during acute attacks both **ALA and PBG are elevated ≥5-fold the upper limit of normal**, normalized to urine creatinine (to adjust for urinary concentration).
- **ALAD exception:** in ALAD porphyria **only ALA is elevated** (PBG normal); PBG is elevated in all other AHP types.
- **Do NOT screen with urine porphyrins alone** — ALA and PBG are porphyrin *precursors* and are not captured by porphyrin assays; secondary porphyrinurias lead to erroneous overdiagnosis.
- Both ALA and PBG can be measured with high sensitivity and specificity, but assays run at large reference labs (results in 1–2 weeks). Rapid qualitative urine PBG tests exist (Hoesch / Watson-Schwartz; a newer rapid PBG test, Teco Diagnostics).

### Timing of Testing
- Testing is **most useful during an acute attack.**
- **Collect the random urine ALA/PBG/creatinine BEFORE starting IV [[hemin|hemin]]** — hemin normalizes the precursors.
- Unless already treated with hemin, ALA/PBG can be tested days after an attack: in **AIP** they remain elevated for **months–years**; in **HCP/VP** they fall quickly.
- In **asymptomatic sporadic AIP, 15%–44% have normal urine ALA/PBG** (similar or higher for HCP/VP) → confirmatory testing may require **repeat testing during an acute attack.**
- In frequent/recurrent attackers, **normal ALA and PBG levels likely rule out AHP** as the cause.

### Genetic Confirmation
- **BPA 3:** confirm AHP type by genetic testing after positive biochemistry — sequence **ALAD, HMBS, CPOX, PPOX** → ALAD, AIP, HCP, VP respectively.
- Whole-gene sequencing identifies **95%–99%** of cases.
- **Screen first-degree relatives** with genetic testing once the familial variant is identified; counsel carriers.
- Genetic testing is the gold standard for confirmation but **not for initial screening** (most carriers never have attacks; ~1% penetrance in AIP). Even in confirmed carriers, symptom attribution benefits from biochemical confirmation.

| Diagnostic biochemical testing | Confirmatory testing | Annual monitoring | On hemin (add) | On givosiran (add) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random urine PBG, ALA, and creatinine (normalize to creatinine; **>5-fold ULN** for diagnosis; ALAD = ALA only) | Genetic sequencing of *ALAD, HMBS, CPOX, PPOX* | Liver enzymes; creatinine and eGFR; liver ultrasound and α-fetoprotein **every 6 mo after age 50 y** | Iron, ferritin | Comprehensive metabolic panel, plasma homocysteine, urinalysis, urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio, B12/folate, amylase/lipase |

*Table 2 — Testing recommendations for AHP. Givosiran labs: before start and again just before each monthly injection for 3 months; if stable and well-tolerated, repeat every 3 months for the next year and at least every 6 months thereafter. ([[aga-2023-acute-hepatic-porphyria]])*

## Therapeutics

### Acute Attack Management
- **IV [[hemin|hemin]] (BPA 4)** for attacks severe enough to require admission:
  - **Dose: 3–4 mg/kg body weight, once daily, typically 4 days** (3–5 days).
  - Give into a **high-flow central vein** (PICC or central port) due to thrombophlebitis; **heme bound to human serum albumin** preferred (stabilization, less venous irritation).
  - Mechanism: down-regulates hepatic ALAS1 → reduced ALA/PBG. Symptom relief typically **48–72 h.**
  - Collect random urine ALA/PBG/creatinine **before** starting; in confirmed AHP, hemin may be started empirically (rapid ALA/PBG tests often unavailable).
- **Supportive care (BPA 5):**
  - Stop CYP450-inducing precipitants (directly up-regulate ALAS1 mRNA).
  - Aggressive **pain control (analgesics)** and **antiemetics.**
  - **IV carbohydrate loading ~300 g/day** in adults (fasting induces PGC-1α → ALAS1).
  - Correct **hyponatremia** (often SIADH-mediated — **correct slowly**) and **hypomagnesemia**; manage hypertension and tachycardia; monitor electrolytes.
  - **Seizures:** barbiturates, hydantoins, carbamazepine, and valproic acid are **contraindicated**; **magnesium sulfate, benzodiazepines, and levetiracetam** appear safe.

### Trigger Avoidance
- **BPA 6:** counsel all patients to avoid identifiable triggers — **alcohol**, **porphyrinogenic (CYP450-inducing) medications**, sex hormones (progesterone), acute illness/infection, physical/psychological stress, tobacco, caloric deprivation.
- Safe/unsafe drug databases: porphyria.org and drugs-porphyria.org.
- Menstrual-cycle–associated attacks may be treated with hormonal suppression (e.g., GnRH agonists) — limited success.

### Prophylaxis for Recurrent Attacks
- **BPA 7:** for recurrent attacks (**≥4/year**), consider outpatient prophylaxis with **[[hemin|prophylactic heme therapy]]** or **[[givosiran]]**.
- **Prophylactic IV [[hemin|hemin]]:** off-label; effectiveness at preventing attacks less established than for aborting them; complications include indwelling catheters, infection, and **[[iron-overload-and-iron-metabolism|iron overload]]** (monitor — see below).
- **[[givosiran]]:** ALAS1-specific siRNA-GalNAc, **subcutaneous monthly**; FDA-approved for adults (US), EMA for ≥12 y (EU). Prescribe **only for biochemically AND genetically confirmed** recurrent attacks. **Avoid in pregnancy / pregnancy planning.** Monitor liver enzymes, BUN/creatinine, homocysteine, amylase, lipase; may worsen eGFR in a subset.

### Liver Transplantation
- **BPA 8:** [[liver-transplantation|liver transplantation]] limited to **intractable symptoms with significantly decreased quality of life, refractory to pharmacotherapy.**
- **Curative** — corrects hepatic HMBS deficiency, normalizes ALA/PBG, resolves attacks; some improvement in chronic symptoms (including peripheral neuropathy).
- European Liver Transplant Registry (38 recipients): **1-year and 5-year survival 92% and 82%**; poorer outcomes with severe neuropathy and/or renal dysfunction at baseline.
- **Genetically screen related living donors** — asymptomatic *HMBS*-variant donors may cause poor post-transplant outcomes. Perform at centers experienced with AHP (including preoperative hemin).

### Long-Term Monitoring & Surveillance
- **Liver disease (BPA 9):** monitor **annually**. Aminotransferases elevated in ~13% during attacks (28% at baseline in ENVISION); abnormal LFTs → investigate alternative etiologies (e.g., [[nafld-masld|NASH]], iron overload); [[liver-biopsy|liver biopsy]] may help.
  - On ≥monthly prophylactic hemin (9% iron by weight): **ferritin and iron every 3–6 months**; if **ferritin >1000 ng/mL → therapeutic phlebotomy.**
  - On [[givosiran]]: **ALT >3× ULN** reported in first 3–6 months → **monitor liver enzymes monthly during that period**; if normal, reduce to twice/year.
- **[[hcc-surveillance|HCC surveillance]] (BPA 10):** risk **1.5%–1.8%**; **begin at age 50 y — [[hepatocellular-carcinoma|liver ultrasound every 6 months]], regardless of symptom severity.** Female predominance 2.5×; HCC (and [[cholangiocarcinoma]]) may occur **without fibrosis/[[cirrhosis|cirrhosis]]**; AFP role unclear (not reliably elevated even with high tumor burden).
- **CKD surveillance (BPA 11):** on treatment, **serum creatinine and eGFR at least annually** (more frequently on givosiran). CKD 29% and hypertension 43% (US Porphyrias Consortium); porphyria-associated kidney disease in up to 59% of symptomatic AIP (eGFR decline ~1 mL/min/1.73 m²/year); EXPLORE — 68% reduced eGFR, 28% stage 3a/3b/4 CKD. **Optimize blood-pressure control.** Kidney transplant is treatment of choice for ESRD (ALA/PBG rise between dialysis sessions; improves AIP symptoms via increased clearance).
- **Counsel on long-term complications (BPA 12):** neuropathy, CKD, hypertension, HCC, and the need for ongoing monitoring — including asymptomatic high excretors, who merit annual monitoring.
