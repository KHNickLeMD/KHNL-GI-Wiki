## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- **Suspect on any ONE of:** fever, chills, abdominal pain, [[jaundice]], nausea, vomiting, disturbance of consciousness — the same trigger set as [[acute-cholangitis|acute cholangitis]]; one symptom is enough to start the workup. ([[tg18-2018-cholangitis-flowchart]])
- **Take vital signs first** (blood pressure [BP], heart rate [HR], respiratory rate, temperature, urine volume, peripheral oxygen saturation [SpO₂], consciousness) to decide whether the situation is urgent. **If urgent, start treatment immediately — do not wait for the definitive diagnosis.**
- **Murphy's sign — the exam finding specific to acute cholecystitis, and TG18 says it must always be confirmed:** compression of the right upper quadrant makes the patient catch their breath from pain on deep inspiration. (Its absence points instead toward cholangitis or [[choledocholithiasis]].)
- **Blood tests** are drawn for diagnosis *and* grading: white blood cell count (WBC), platelets, C-reactive protein (CRP), albumin; alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), bilirubin; blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, prothrombin time (PT)/PT–international normalized ratio (PT-INR); blood gas; **blood culture preferably if high fever is present**.
- Common condition in the US; imaging (ultrasound/computed tomography [CT]) identifies cholecystitis and screens for **gallbladder perforation** — a key branch point in the drainage algorithm below. ([[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage]])

**TG18 diagnostic criteria** — unchanged from Tokyo Guidelines 2013 (TG13), adopted for TG18 without any modification ([[tg18-2018-cholecystitis-criteria]]):

| | Category | Items (any one counts) |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Local signs of inflammation | (1) Murphy's sign · (2) right upper quadrant (RUQ) mass / pain / tenderness |
| **B** | Systemic signs of inflammation | (1) Fever · (2) elevated CRP · (3) elevated WBC count |
| **C** | Imaging findings | Imaging findings characteristic of acute cholecystitis (below) |

- **Suspected diagnosis = one item in A + one item in B.**
- **Definite diagnosis = one item in A + one item in B + C.** Imaging is what converts suspected to definite — that is the whole point of the TG13 redesign.
- **Must be excluded first:** acute hepatitis, other acute abdominal diseases, chronic cholecystitis.
- TG18 attaches **no numeric cut-point** to fever, elevated CRP or elevated WBC in these criteria — any value above the local reference range counts. (A WBC cut-point appears only in the severity grading, below.)
- Definite vs suspected is not cosmetic: length of hospitalization and medical costs differ significantly between the two groups.

**Performance of the criteria** ([[tg18-2018-cholecystitis-criteria]]):

| Instrument | Sensitivity | Specificity |
|---|---|---|
| TG07 criteria (pre-2013, no imaging requirement) | 84.9% | 50.0% |
| Murphy's sign alone | 20.5% | 87.5% |
| **TG13/TG18 criteria — 451-patient multicenter validation** | **91.2%** | **96.9%** |
| TG13/TG18 criteria — second study, pathology as gold standard | 83.1% | 37.5% |

- Adding the imaging requirement is what fixed the TG07 specificity problem (50.0% → 96.9%). Diagnostic accuracy across studies with pathology as gold standard still ranges **94.0% down to 60.4%** — the criteria are robust for ruling in, less so for ruling out.
- **Procalcitonin: not usable.** Too few studies in acute cholecystitis; TG18 could not assess its value and made no recommendation.

### Severity Assessment

**TG18 severity grading** — read top-down; also unchanged from TG13 ([[tg18-2018-cholecystitis-criteria]]):

| Grade | Rule | Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| **Grade III (severe)** | **Any ONE** organ/system dysfunction | **Cardiovascular:** hypotension requiring dopamine **≥5 μg/kg per min**, or **any dose** of norepinephrine · **Neurological:** decreased level of consciousness · **Respiratory:** PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio **<300** · **Renal:** oliguria, creatinine **>2.0 mg/dL** · **Hepatic:** PT-INR **>1.5** · **Hematological:** platelet count **<100,000/mm³** |
| **Grade II (moderate)** | **Any ONE** of four conditions | WBC **>18,000/mm³** · **palpable tender mass** in the RUQ · **duration of complaints >72 h** · **marked local inflammation** = gangrenous cholecystitis, pericholecystic abscess, hepatic abscess, biliary peritonitis, emphysematous cholecystitis |
| **Grade I (mild)** | Meets **neither** Grade III nor Grade II | Also definable as acute cholecystitis in a healthy patient with no organ dysfunction and mild gallbladder inflammation, making cholecystectomy a safe, low-risk operation |

- ⚠ **Do not carry the [[acute-cholangitis|cholangitis]] grades across.** Cholecystitis Grade II is **1-of-4**; cholangitis Grade II is **2-of-5** with entirely different items. Grade III organ definitions are near-identical but are separate tables.
- **Gangrenous and emphysematous cholecystitis are Grade II (moderate), not Grade III** — they are "marked local inflammation". Both can still cause organ damage if diagnosis is delayed.
- **Measure serum total bilirubin** in Grade III — required to judge the predictive factors on the TG18 flowchart.
- **Severity drives outcome** ([[tg18-2018-cholecystitis-criteria]]): 30-day mortality **1.1% / 0.8% / 5.4%** for Grades I / II / III (n = 1,339 / 1,702 / 680; *P* <0.001). Length of stay, conversion from laparoscopic to open cholecystectomy (up to ~35% at Grade III vs ~6% at Grade I), complication rates, bile duct injury and medical costs all rise with grade. TG13 grade is an independent predictor of both length of stay and conversion.
- **Within Grade III, three findings predict death:** jaundice (odds ratio 6.47, 95% CI 2.45–17.11), respiratory dysfunction (5.84, 2.05–16.64), neurological dysfunction (4.35, 1.64–11.52). CCI ≥6 was not independently significant. TG18 raises subdividing Grade III on these factors as the way to select who can still go to surgery.
- ⚠ **The grading does not measure surgical difficulty or surgical risk.** TG18 states this explicitly and rejected two proposed preoperative severity scores (a German 9-point score with ≥7 = severe; an Italian 4-factor score) because neither supplies criteria for severity grading. **So TG18 does not supply the numeric "high surgical risk" definition that [[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage|AGA 2023]] leaves to judgment.**
- **General status is graded alongside severity** in both biliary infections, using the **Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI)** and the **American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) Physical Status classification**. ([[tg18-2018-cholangitis-flowchart]])

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: no diagnostic schema covers undifferentiated RUQ pain — the closest algorithms are [[jaundice]] (cholestasis) and [[biliary-stricture]] (obstruction).*

- [[acute-cholangitis|Acute cholangitis]] — the other acute biliary infection; obstructed duct rather than obstructed cystic duct, and **Murphy's sign is absent**. May coexist.
- [[choledocholithiasis|Choledocholithiasis]] — may coexist; a concurrent need for [[ercp|endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)]] changes the drainage route (below)
- [[gallbladder-cancer|Gallbladder cancer]] / malignant cystic duct obstruction — tumor occluding the cystic duct takeoff is a specific indication for endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided drainage
- [[acute-pancreatitis|Acute pancreatitis]] — overlapping upper-abdominal pain; gallstone acute pancreatitis (AP) shares the etiology
- Differential limited to the entities the cited guidelines address.

## Diagnostics

**Imaging — do at least one of abdominal ultrasound or CT; ultrasound first** (minimally invasive, widely available, simple, cheap; limited by operator skill and patient condition). ([[tg18-2018-cholangitis-flowchart]])

**Distinctive sonographic/CT signs of acute cholecystitis** ([[tg18-2018-cholangitis-flowchart]]):

- Enlargement of the gallbladder
- Gallbladder **wall thickening**
- Gallbladder **calculi**
- **Fluid retention around** the gallbladder (pericholecystic fluid)
- **Abscess around** the gallbladder
- **Sludge / debris** within the gallbladder
- **Sonographic Murphy's sign** — pain when the probe presses on the gallbladder

**Imaging also answers the two drainage questions** ([[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage]]): is there **free gallbladder perforation** (contraindicates transmural endoscopic drainage), and what is the gallbladder-to-gastrointestinal (GI)-wall distance (**<10 mm required** for safe lumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS) deployment)?

### Imaging findings of acute cholecystitis

The findings that satisfy **criterion C**, with the size cut-points TG18 prints ([[tg18-2018-cholecystitis-criteria]]):

- **Gallbladder wall thickening ≥4 mm**
- **Gallbladder enlargement — long axis ≥8 cm, short axis ≥4 cm**
- Gallstones or retained debris
- Fluid accumulation around the gallbladder
- Linear shadows in the fatty tissue around the gallbladder

### Which imaging test

| Modality | Pooled sensitivity (95% CI) | Pooled specificity (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|
| Cholescintigraphy (hepatobiliary scintigraphy, HIDA) | 0.94 (0.92–0.96) | 0.90 (0.85–0.93) |
| Ultrasound | 0.82 (0.75–0.87) | 0.81 (0.73–0.87) |
| [[mri-mrcp\|Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)]] | 0.86 (0.66–0.95) | 0.82 (0.69–0.90) |

- **Ultrasound first** — recommended as the first-choice imaging method for morphological diagnosis on grounds of **low invasiveness, availability, ease of use and cost**, explicitly *not* diagnostic yield: HIDA outperforms it in every comparative study. (Recommendation 1, level C; [[tg18-2018-cholecystitis-criteria]]) Reported real-world use rate 61.3%.
- **Ultrasound's blind spots:** a causative stone in the gallbladder or bile duct may not be visible, and **gangrenous cholecystitis is difficult to diagnose** — add contrast-enhanced CT or MRI when needed.
- **MRI / magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the second step when ultrasound is non-diagnostic** (Recommendation 2, level B). Non-contrast MRI/MRCP already shows wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid and gallbladder enlargement and was **not inferior to contrast-enhanced MRI** in one study; wall thickening + dense gallbladder-bed staining in the early contrast phase is **92% specific** for acute vs chronic cholecystitis. MRCP maps biliary anatomy without contrast (preoperatively useful). Downsides: cost, and degraded images in a patient who cannot hold still or breath-hold.
- **Color / power Doppler: do not use for this diagnosis.** No recent study supports it; flow assessment depends too heavily on device and body habitus to standardise. (Level D, future research question)

### Gangrenous cholecystitis

- **Contrast-enhanced CT or contrast-enhanced MRI is recommended** for the diagnosis (Recommendation 2, level C) — abdominal ultrasound **underestimates** wall irregularity and rupture.
- **Dynamic CT findings:** irregular wall thickening · poor wall contrast enhancement (**interrupted rim sign**) · increased density of pericholecystic fat · gas in the lumen or wall · intraluminal membranous structures (intraluminal flap/membrane) · peri-gallbladder abscess.

| Finding | Performance |
|---|---|
| Interrupted rim sign on contrast-enhanced CT | 73% sensitivity, 95% negative predictive value |
| Intraluminal membranous structures on contrast-enhanced MRI | 80% diagnostic accuracy |
| Wall perfusion defect **+** no identifiable calculi | 92% accuracy, 88.2% sensitivity, 100% specificity |

### Emphysematous cholecystitis

- Caused by aerogenic bacteria; **high perforation rate**, often extremely rapid course; complications include intra-abdominal abscess, generalized peritonitis, gas gangrene of the abdominal wall and sepsis.
- **Plain CT is the most useful test** (Recommendation 2, level D) — gas is clearly hypodense, usually near **−1,000 HU**. Use contrast-enhanced CT to evaluate complications (intraperitoneal abscess, peritonitis).
- **Ultrasound cannot reliably separate** intramural gas from intraluminal gas or porcelain gallbladder — all appear hyperechoic; intraluminal gas may also be post-surgical or post-sphincterotomy. MRI shows gas as a signal void but is inferior to CT for small volumes.

## Therapeutics

### Cholecystectomy — definitive treatment

- **Cholecystectomy is the definitive treatment** of acute cholecystitis. Nonoperative drainage is reserved for patients deemed inoperable. ([[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage]])
- **Timing: laparoscopic surgery should be performed within 96 h of symptom onset** ([[tg18-2018-cholecystitis-criteria]]). Note the two separate clocks — >72 h of complaints makes the episode **Grade II**, while 96 h from onset is the limit on the laparoscopic approach.
- **Grade III is not automatically inoperable.** Surgery is feasible in Grade III even where percutaneous cholecystostomy is not performed, with **conversion to open** or **subtotal cholecystectomy** as fallback procedures. Expect higher conversion and complication rates at higher grades.

### Who is a nonsurgical candidate?

Per [[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage]]:

- **High surgical risk** = significant medical comorbidities that would make laparoscopic cholecystectomy **excessively risky** (the conventional trigger for percutaneous drainage). No numeric score/criteria are given in the Clinical Practice Update (CPU) — judgment + surgical consultation.
- To be an **endoscopic** drainage candidate the patient must additionally: tolerate anesthesia and a therapeutic endoscopic procedure; for the transmural ([[endoscopic-ultrasound|EUS]]) route, have **no free gallbladder perforation**.
- **Future surgical candidacy must be clarified with the surgeon before EUS-guided gallbladder drainage (EUS-GBD)** — transmural drainage creates a cholecystoenteric fistula that requires repair at later cholecystectomy (postoperative leak risk); transpapillary drainage preserves normal anatomy.

### Gallbladder drainage in high-surgical-risk patients

Three routes ([[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage]]):

| | Percutaneous (PT-GBD) | Transpapillary (ET-GBD) | EUS-guided ([[eus-guided-gallbladder-drainage\|EUS-GBD]]) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **How** | Ultrasound/CT-guided percutaneous drain into gallbladder | [[ercp\|ERCP]]: cystic duct cannulation + transpapillary plastic stent | LAMS from duodenum/stomach into gallbladder |
| **Best for** | Perforation suspected; can't tolerate [[endoscopy-sedation\|sedation]]; conventional default | Needs ERCP anyway (choledocholithiasis, cholangitis); future surgical candidate; large-volume [[ascites]]; coagulopathy | In-dwelling uncovered metal biliary stent or tumor occluding cystic duct; large stone burden (cholecystoscopy); poor candidate for repeat interventions |
| **Advantages** | Widely available | Safer with ascites; treats common bile duct (CBD) stones/cholangitis same session; preserves anatomy | Least likely to need reintervention; lowest recurrent cholecystitis |
| **Disadvantages** | 50%–75% morbidity; drain exchanges/reinterventions; recurrent cholecystitis up to 15.4%; dyscosmesis | Lower technical (84% vs 98%) and lowest clinical success (91% vs 97% vs EUS-GBD); pancreatitis risk; stent exchanges | Fistula closure needed if later cholecystectomy; food occlusion; **contraindicated in gallbladder perforation** |

- **Comparative outcomes:** EUS-GBD vs PT-GBD (5 studies, 488 pts, incl. DRAC-1 randomized controlled trial [RCT]) — comparable technical/clinical success, but PT-GBD has longer stays, slower resolution, more reintervention, more pain, and more recurrent cholecystitis (20% vs 2.6%, P = .029). Network meta-analysis: recurrent cholecystitis lowest with EUS-GBD (relative risk [RR] 1.089 vs PT 2.02 vs ET 2.891); mortality lowest with ET-GBD (RR 1.29 vs EUS 2.62 vs PT 2.09). ([[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage]])

**[[asge-2024-therapeutic-eus-biliary|American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) 2024]] — the newer guideline, and it moves the default.** In patients with acute cholecystitis who are **not cholecystectomy candidates**, ASGE suggests **EUS-GBD over PT-GBD** (Rec 5a) **and EUS-GBD over ET-GBD** (Rec 5b) — both **conditional, moderate quality of evidence**. Its per-route carve-outs:

| Route | ASGE 2024 says prefer it when |
|---|---|
| **[[eus-guided-gallbladder-drainage\|EUS-GBD]]** (default) | Major papilla **cannot be accessed**; **indwelling metal stent occluding the cystic duct**; **large gallbladder stone burden** |
| **PT-GBD** | **Gallbladder perforation**; **emphysematous cholecystitis**; **clinical instability**; **unable to tolerate more than moderate sedation**; **EUS-GBD unavailable** (no expertise/training) |
| **ET-GBD** | **Ascites**; **malignant gallbladder infiltration**; **severe coagulopathy**; **refused rescue surgery**; **liver transplant candidates**; not a surgical candidate now but **may be later** |

- ⚠ **Avoid EUS-GBD in a potential [[liver-transplantation\|liver transplant]] candidate** — stated explicitly by ASGE 2024, and net-new versus [[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage|American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) 2023]]. The cholecystoenteric fistula it creates is the reason.
- ⚠ **Contradiction — the "may be a surgical candidate later" patient.** [[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage|AGA 2023]]'s algorithm routes this patient to **ET-GBD** first. ASGE 2024 is **internally inconsistent**: its Rec 5a general concepts list this group under **PT-GBD**, while its own Table 2 lists them under **ET-GBD**. Both guidelines agree the patient should **not** get EUS-GBD; neither guideline settles which non-transmural route is used. **Confirm future surgical candidacy with the surgeon either way.**
- ⚠ **Contradiction — the default route.** AGA 2023's figure sends several groups (uncontrolled ascites, ERCP needed anyway, gallbladder (GB) >10 mm from the GI wall) to **ET-GBD first**; ASGE 2024 makes **EUS-GBD** the general first choice with ascites/coagulopathy/malignant infiltration as the ET-GBD carve-outs. **ASGE 2024 governs** (newer, same tier, and a Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) guideline vs a CPU) — but the AGA carve-outs it does not contradict (needs ERCP for coexisting [[choledocholithiasis|CBD stones]] or [[acute-cholangitis|cholangitis]]; GB >10 mm from the enteral wall, which makes LAMS deployment unsafe) still stand as reasons to choose ET-GBD.

### Choosing the drainage route

![[cholecystitis-2023-highrisk-drainage-algorithm-05.png|700x420]]
*Figure 3 — Approach to cholecystitis in high-surgical-risk patients. ([[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage]])*

- **Perforation suspected on imaging OR unable to tolerate sedation → PT-GBD.**
- **No perforation AND tolerates sedation:**
  - **ET-GBD initially** if: uncontrolled ascites; potential future surgical candidate; undergoing ERCP for other reasons (choledocholithiasis, cholangitis); gallbladder **>10 mm** from the GI tract wall. If fails → EUS-GBD (after surgeon discussion).
  - **EUS-GBD initially** (after surgeon discussion) if: duodenal obstruction / [[gastric-outlet-obstruction|gastric outlet obstruction]]; in-dwelling metal stent occluding the cystic duct; large gallbladder stone burden. If fails → ET-GBD.
  - If both endoscopic routes fail → PT-GBD.
- EUS-GBD is also an option to **internalize an existing percutaneous cholecystostomy** in permanent non-surgical candidates, and a rescue for malignant biliary obstruction after failed ERCP + failed EUS-guided biliary drainage (EUS-BD). ([[aga-2023-eus-gallbladder-drainage]])

### Follow-up after EUS-GBD

- Either per-oral cholecystoscopy at **4–6 weeks** (stone clearance + exchange LAMS for double-pigtail plastic stents) or leave the LAMS permanently in very-high-risk patients — details on [[eus-guided-gallbladder-drainage]].

### Cholecystectomy in Pregnancy

*Full pregnancy-GI context: [[liver-disease-in-pregnancy]].*

- **Cholecystectomy is safe during pregnancy; laparoscopic approach is the standard of care regardless of trimester, ideally the 2nd trimester.** ([[aga-2024-pregnancy-gi-liver]] best practice advice [BPA] 9)
- Gallstone disease is the 2nd leading cause (after appendicitis) of nonobstetric acute abdominal pain in pregnancy. **Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is superior to conservative management** for symptomatic cholelithiasis (conservative → recurrent biliary symptoms in ~60%). Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons (SAGES): **laparoscopy safe in any trimester**; after the 1st trimester use left-lateral/partial-left-lateral decubitus.
- Initial management: intravenous (IV) hydration, analgesia, antiemetics, antibiotics. **Same-admission cholecystectomy** in [[acute-pancreatitis|acute biliary pancreatitis]] reduces early readmission by **85%**.
- If hemodynamically unstable or high surgical risk: **percutaneous cholecystostomy or gallbladder aspiration as a bridge** (1st→2nd trimester, or 3rd trimester→postpartum) until cholecystectomy can be performed. Late-3rd-trimester biliary pain may be managed by postponing surgery to postpartum if no maternal/fetal risk. ([[aga-2024-pregnancy-gi-liver]])
