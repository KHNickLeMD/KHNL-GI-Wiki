## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

Functional diarrhea (FDr) is a [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|disorder of gut–brain interaction]] — recurrent passage of loose or watery stools **in the absence of an identifiable organic etiology**, and **in the absence of predominant abdominal pain** ([[rome-v-2026-bowel-disorders]]). It is Rome V bowel disorder **C3**.

**Rome V criteria (C3)** — fulfilled for the past 3 months, symptom onset ≥6 months before diagnosis:

> 1. Loose or watery stools occurring in **more than 25% of stools**, and
> 2. **Hard stools are rarely present** without medication that can cause constipation
>
> Patients meeting criteria for [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|irritable bowel syndrome with diarrhea (IBS-D)]] **should be excluded**.

- **Stool form:** Rome V's bowel-disorder criteria score consistency on the [[bristol-stool-form-scale|Bristol Stool Form Scale (BSFS)]] — loose or watery = **types 6–7**, lumpy or hard = **types 1–2**. The C3 criteria do not restate these cut-points.
- **Change from Rome IV:** point 2 is new, added for consistency with the chronic constipation criteria ([[chronic-constipation]]).
- Abdominal pain, discomfort and/or bloating **may be present but must not be predominant** — predominance makes it IBS-D, which is the single most important distinction on this page.

> [!pitfall] FDr and IBS-D are the same illness minus the pain
> Pathophysiology overlaps IBS-D substantially — secretory, osmotic and motor disturbances, with bile acid diarrhea the most common identified mechanism. The clinical consequence: evaluate and treat FDr **as if it were IBS-D**, but do not start a neuromodulator, because the pain that neuromodulators treat is by definition not the problem ([[rome-v-2026-bowel-disorders]]).

### Severity Assessment

[[rome-v-2026-bowel-disorders|Rome V]] prints no severity instrument for FDr. Natural history: **94% of patients remain symptomatic at 12 to 20 months** and 29% at 12 years, so this is a chronic, relapsing condition rather than a self-limited one.

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[chronic-diarrhea]].*

- [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|IBS-D]] — the formal exclusion; separated only by whether abdominal pain/discomfort predominates
- [[bile-acid-diarrhea|Bile acid diarrhea]] — the most common identified pathophysiologic disturbance in FDr
- [[microscopic-colitis|Microscopic colitis]]
- [[celiac-disease]]
- [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]]
- [[giardiasis|Giardiasis]] and other enteric infection
- Carbohydrate maldigestion (lactose, fructose, sorbitol, sucrose, starch)
- Medication-induced diarrhea — [[rome-v-2026-bowel-disorders|Rome V]] names **magnesium** and [[proton-pump-inhibitors|proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)]] specifically, plus over-the-counter agents, supplements and alternative therapies
- Post-infectious diarrhea — FDr, like IBS, often follows acute gastroenteritis ([[postinfectious-ibs]])

## Diagnostics

**The diagnostic evaluation of suspected FDr should be like that of IBS-D** ([[rome-v-2026-bowel-disorders]]) — the utility of testing *specifically* in FDr has not been as well studied, so the yield of individual tests for identifying an etiology here remains uncertain.

That evaluation — the initial blood and stool panel, the explicit do-not-order list, the fecal calprotectin bands, the [[colonoscopy]] and bile acid malabsorption triggers — is on [[irritable-bowel-syndrome]] and on the chronic diarrhea schema linked above; it is not repeated here.

- **Alarm features** are the same as for the other bowel disorders: anemia; rectal bleeding (clarify the pattern — blood mixed with stool vs only on the toilet paper); unexplained objective weight loss; nocturnal diarrhea; onset of symptoms after age 50; [[fecal-incontinence|fecal incontinence]]; family history of [[colorectal-cancer|colorectal cancer (CRC)]], IBD or celiac disease.

## Therapeutics

Few studies have evaluated treatment in FDr specifically; most recommendations are **extrapolated from IBS-D**, and [[rome-v-2026-bowel-disorders|Rome V]] refers the reader to its IBS-D treatment section.

| Measure | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Medication review** | Stop or substitute contributors — magnesium, PPIs, over-the-counter agents, supplements, alternative therapies |
| **[[loperamide\|Loperamide]]** | **2–4 mg, up to 4 times a day**; slows colon transit and improves stool frequency and consistency; can be used routinely **without laboratory monitoring** |
| **Empiric bile acid sequestrant** | Cholestyramine, colesevelam or colestipol for **a minimum of 4 weeks** — reasonable when bile acid malabsorption testing is unavailable (see [[bile-acid-diarrhea]]) |
| **Ondansetron** | A 5-hydroxytryptamine-3 (5-HT₃) antagonist; in IBS-D improves stool consistency and frequency and reduces urgency **but not abdominal pain**. Not studied in FDr, but its antidiarrheal effect predominates, so it may be effective |

Rome V prints a dose only for loperamide; it names the bile acid sequestrants and ondansetron without doses.

> [!pitfall] Do not lead with a neuromodulator
> Because abdominal pain is not predominant in FDr, **central neuromodulators should not be used as initial therapy** — unlike in IBS, where they are a mainstay and tricyclic antidepressants are the best-studied class ([[rome-v-2026-bowel-disorders]]).
