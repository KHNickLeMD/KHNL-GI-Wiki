Postinfectious irritable bowel syndrome (PI-IBS) is [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)]] arising as a chronic sequela of [[acute-diarrhea|acute infectious gastroenteritis]], including traveler's diarrhea (TD). The defining idea is a paradigm shift: an **external event (the enteric infection) produces prolonged and permanent changes in gastrointestinal (GI) function that are *not* directly mediated by persistence of the infectious agent** ([[acg-2016-acute-diarrhea]]).

## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- **Apply conventional IBS criteria to a patient who has been sick with gastroenteritis or TD** ([[acg-2016-acute-diarrhea]], which framed the diagnosis around Rome III). There is **no separate criteria set for PI-IBS** — the antecedent infection plus the IBS criteria is the diagnosis.
- **Current criteria are [[rome-v-2026-dgbi|Rome V]] (2026)**: pain or discomfort **≥3 days/month in the last 3 months** (Rome IV required ≥1 day/week), "abdominal discomfort" re-admitted, and the pain must **not be continuous**. The looser frequency bar means more post-infection patients now qualify. Full criteria and the Rome IV→V diff live on [[irritable-bowel-syndrome]].
- **No commercially available serologic or other diagnostic test confirms PI-IBS** — the diagnosis rests on the temporal link plus the IBS criteria ([[acg-2016-acute-diarrhea]]).
- IBS can arise **within months** of the index infection ([[acg-2020-ibs]]).
- **≥14 days of diarrhea is the duration that should trigger the thought.** Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) **Recommendation 13**: in uncomplicated traveler's diarrhea, diagnostic testing is not recommended unless treatment is indicated, but **travelers with diarrhea lasting ≥14 days should be evaluated for intestinal parasitic infections**, *[[clostridioides-difficile|Clostridioides difficile]]* testing should be performed if antimicrobials were given in the **preceding 8–12 weeks**, and **[[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]] and PI-IBS should be considered** for evaluation *(strong, moderate)* ([[idsa-2017-infectious-diarrhea]]). Both infectious and inflammatory causes are excluded before settling on PI-IBS.
- IDSA also recommends that clinicians **evaluate people for postinfectious and extraintestinal manifestations** associated with enteric infections *(strong, moderate)* ([[idsa-2017-infectious-diarrhea]]).

### Triggers and Risk Factors

| Pathogen class | Triggers named | IBS after an infection of this class ([[acg-2020-ibs\|ACG 2020]]) |
|---|---|---|
| Bacterial | *[[campylobacter-infection\|Campylobacter jejuni]]*, *[[salmonella-infection\|Salmonella]]* ([[acg-2020-ibs]]); also *[[shigellosis\|Shigella]]* and [[shiga-toxin-producing-e-coli\|Shiga toxin–producing *Escherichia coli* (STEC)]] ([[idsa-2017-infectious-diarrhea]], Table 4) | **13.8%** |
| Viral | Norwalk ([[acg-2020-ibs]]) | — |
| Parasitic | *[[cryptosporidiosis\|Cryptosporidium]]* spp., *[[giardiasis\|Giardia]]* (*G. duodenalis* / *G. lamblia*) ([[acg-2020-ibs]], [[idsa-2017-infectious-diarrhea]]) | **41.9%** |

- **Overall risk after any of these infections** ([[acg-2020-ibs]]): pooled prevalence of postinfection IBS **11% (95% confidence interval [CI] 8.2–15.8)**, **4.2× higher** than in non-exposed individuals; odds ratio (OR) for IBS after bacterial, viral, or parasitic gastroenteritis **3.51 (95% CI 2.05–6.00)**. [[idsa-2017-infectious-diarrhea|IDSA 2017]] puts PI-IBS after bacterial diarrhea lower, at **3%–10%**; the newer American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) figures govern this page.
- Pathogen-specific risk figures for *Giardia* are on [[giardiasis]].
- **Recent data suggest IBS may also follow COVID-19** ([[rome-v-2026-bowel-disorders]]).
- **Host risk factors** ([[acg-2020-ibs]]): female sex, **antibiotic exposure**, and a history of **anxiety or depression**.
- **Acute-illness severity raises risk** — PI-IBS occurs in a sizeable proportion of those who have an episode of TD, **particularly after bacterial infection and with a more severe clinical presentation** ([[acg-2016-acute-diarrhea]]).
- **Childhood *Salmonella* infection can be an IBS risk factor in adulthood** ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]).

### Severity Assessment

- No severity score specific to PI-IBS. Severity is graded as for IBS generally (mild / moderate / severe clinical profile), and that grading sets treatment intensity — see [[irritable-bowel-syndrome]] ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]).

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[chronic-diarrhea]].*

- [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|IBS]] without an infectious trigger — same criteria, no antecedent gastroenteritis.
- Persistent infection — e.g. [[giardiasis]], [[cryptosporidiosis]], [[entamoeba-histolytica-infection|amebiasis]]; PI-IBS is by definition *not* agent persistence, so an unresolved pathogen must be excluded. Parasitic infections in particular range from asymptomatic to chronic [[abdominal-bloating-and-distention|bloating]], diarrhea, and abdominal pain — **clinically similar to IBS** ([[acg-2020-ibs]]).
- Brainerd diarrhea — the other cause IDSA pairs with PI-IBS for **chronic watery diarrhea often lasting a year or more**; its etiologic agent has not been identified ([[idsa-2017-infectious-diarrhea]], Table 3).
- [[celiac-disease|Celiac disease]], [[microscopic-colitis|microscopic colitis]], [[small-intestinal-bacterial-overgrowth|small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)]], [[bile-acid-diarrhea|bile acid diarrhea]] — organic mimics of postinfectious bowel symptoms.
- [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|IBD]] presenting after an enteric infection.

## Diagnostics

- No confirmatory test exists; testing is directed at **excluding persistent infection and organic mimics** rather than at establishing PI-IBS ([[acg-2016-acute-diarrhea]]).
- **Serologic testing for IBS** (the anti–cytolethal distending toxin B [anti-CdtB] and anti-vinculin antibody strategy proposed for post-infectious diarrhea-predominant IBS): the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) **makes no recommendation** — *"In patients presenting with chronic diarrhea, the AGA makes no recommendation for the use of currently available serologic tests for diagnosis of IBS"* **(no recommendation; knowledge gap)**. Specificity is around **90%**, but sensitivity of only **20%–40%** is not sufficient for routine use, and the case–control design in secondary/tertiary care likely inflates even those estimates ([[aga-2019-lab-evaluation-functional-diarrhea-ibs-d]]).

> [!pitfall] "No recommendation" is not a recommendation against
> A knowledge-gap statement means the confidence in the effect estimate is too low to say anything — it must never be cited as an AGA position for or against anti-CdtB/anti-vinculin serology ([[aga-2019-lab-evaluation-functional-diarrhea-ibs-d]]).

- **ACG recommends *against* routine stool testing for enteric pathogens in all patients with IBS** *(conditional recommendation, low quality of evidence)* — testing for bacterial and viral infections with subsequent treatment does not prevent IBS, and antibiotic exposure may itself be a risk factor ([[acg-2020-ibs]]).
- **Exception — test for *Giardia***. ACG tests only those with high pretest probability or definite risk factors; AGA is broader, **recommending *Giardia* testing in all patients with chronic diarrhea** *(strong, high)* ([[aga-2019-lab-evaluation-functional-diarrhea-ibs-d]]). Use a **fecal antigen immunoassay or polymerase chain reaction (PCR)**; the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) risk-factor list that triggers testing, and the test characteristics, live on [[giardiasis]].

## Therapeutics

- **Manage as [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|IBS]]** — no ingested guideline gives PI-IBS-specific pharmacotherapy. Treatment intensity follows symptom severity ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]); the full therapeutic ladder is on [[irritable-bowel-syndrome]].
- **Course:** symptoms attributable to PI-IBS **generally resolve within 1 year, but may persist for several years**; assessment and management by a gastroenterologist should be considered ([[idsa-2017-infectious-diarrhea]]).
- **Prevention — chemoprophylaxis, in high-risk travelers only.** PI-IBS being recognized as an important chronic consequence of TD is one of the two developments that reopened the TD chemoprophylaxis debate (the other being [[rifaximin]] as a safer alternative to fluoroquinolones). ACG's resulting position: **antibiotic chemoprophylaxis has moderate to good effectiveness and may be considered in high-risk groups for short-term use** *(strong recommendation, high level of evidence)* ([[acg-2016-acute-diarrhea]]).
- **Treating the infection does not prevent IBS.** Testing for bacterial and viral infections with subsequent treatment does not prevent IBS, and antibiotic exposure is itself a risk factor for postinfection IBS ([[acg-2020-ibs]]).

> **Contradiction surfaced:** [[acg-2016-acute-diarrhea]] invokes PI-IBS risk as part of the argument for antibiotic chemoprophylaxis in high-risk travelers; the newer [[acg-2020-ibs]] holds that treating bacterial/viral enteritis does not prevent IBS and that antibiotic exposure is itself a PI-IBS risk factor. Both are guidelines, so the newer publication date governs the page's stance on antibiotics for prevention.
