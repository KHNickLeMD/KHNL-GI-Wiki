## Assessment

**Anorectal sensory dysfunction disorders** are the Rome V **F4** group: abnormal rectal sensory thresholds on balloon distention in a patient whose symptoms already meet Rome V criteria for another anorectal diagnosis. Two entities — **F4a rectal hyposensitivity** (thresholds too high) and **F4b rectal hypersensitivity** (thresholds too low).

- **Layered diagnosis — symptoms *plus* physiology.** Unlike most [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|disorders of gut–brain interaction]], F4 cannot be made from symptoms alone: the patient must first fulfill Rome V symptom criteria for [[fecal-incontinence|fecal incontinence (FI)]] or [[defecation-disorders|dyssynergic defecation (DD)]], **and** have abnormal thresholds on rectal sensory testing.
- Rectal sensory dysfunction also occurs in [[chronic-constipation|chronic constipation]] and [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)]].

### Rectal Hyposensitivity (F4a)

Abnormally **high** sensory thresholds to rectal balloon distention. Awareness of stooling is essential for both defecation and continence; hyposensitivity may be **primary**, or secondary (see Differential Diagnosis).

**Must include all of the following:**

1. Patients fulfill Rome V symptom criteria for fecal incontinence or dyssynergic defecation
2. Demonstration of rectal hyposensitivity with rectal balloon distention sensory testing — **2 or more** threshold values (volume/pressure) of rectal sensation (first sensation, desire to defecate, urgency to defecate, or maximum tolerable sensation) that are **greater than 2 standard deviations (SDs) of the upper limit of the normal range**, using either a simple balloon ([[anorectal-manometry|anorectal manometry (ARM)]]) or rectal barostat
3. No evidence of structural disease on colonoscopy/barium enema, computed tomography (CT) scan, or metabolic abnormalities

> Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.

### Rectal Hypersensitivity (F4b)

Abnormally **low** sensory thresholds to rectal balloon distention, associated with urgency and/or rectal pain. Encompasses both **allodynia** (pain from normally non-painful stimuli) and **hyperalgesia** (exaggerated pain perception). May be primary or secondary to reduced rectal capacity.

**Must include all of the following:**

1. Patients fulfill Rome V symptom criteria for fecal incontinence or dyssynergic defecation
2. Demonstration of rectal hypersensitivity with rectal balloon distention sensory testing — **1 or more** threshold values (volume/pressure) of rectal sensation (desire to defecate, urgency to defecate, maximum tolerable sensation, or pain) that are **less than 2 SDs of the lower limit of the normal range**, using either a simple balloon (ARM) or rectal barostat
3. No history of rectal excision or evidence of structural disease, including **full-thickness [[rectal-prolapse|rectal prolapse]]**, on endoscopy or imaging studies

> Criteria fulfilled for the last 3 months with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.
> Thresholds for first or constant sensation should not be used.

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[chronic-constipation]] for the outlet/constipation limb and [[fecal-incontinence]] for the incontinence limb — F4 is diagnosed on top of one of those symptom diagnoses, not instead of it.*

| To exclude or consider | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Structural rectal/colonic disease (colonoscopy, barium enema, CT) | Excludes both F4a and F4b |
| Prior rectal excision; full-thickness rectal prolapse | Excludes F4b |
| Electrolyte and renal metabolic dysfunction | Must be excluded **before** diagnosing hyposensitivity |
| Enlarged rectum and/or increased rectal compliance | Secondary hyposensitivity |
| Opioids ([[opioid-induced-constipation\|opioid-induced bowel dysfunction]]) | Secondary hyposensitivity |
| Parkinson disease, spinal cord injury, diabetes | Secondary hyposensitivity |
| Prolonged stool retention | Secondary hyposensitivity |
| Reduced rectal capacity | Secondary hypersensitivity (urge FI: urgency tracks reduced capacity + hypersensitivity) |

## Diagnostics

### Sensory Testing Method

- Rectal balloon distention using a **handheld syringe** or an **electronic barostat**; record threshold volumes/pressures for **first sensation, desire or urgency to defecate, and maximum tolerance**.
- **Specify the method** — inflation rate, increments and volumes vary between techniques; rectal compliance, sex, age and body mass index all affect results. With an ARM balloon system (e.g. a latex balloon) thresholds are affected by rectal compliance — factor this into interpretation.
- **Barostat** (infinitely compliant balloon on a computer-driven pump) gives reproducible thresholds and measures rectal compliance directly.
- Familiarize the patient with the possible sensations first, ideally using sensory charts.
- Both **ramp** and **phasic** distention protocols are used; symptom severity correlates more strongly with phasic than ramp distention. Alternative: rate perception on a visual analog scale during phasic barostat distentions between **8 and 32 mm Hg**, which grades stimulus intensity.
- Other research modalities: cortical evoked potentials, functional magnetic resonance imaging, electrical stimulation. ARM carries Rome V's top diagnostic-test grade (A1) for identifying rectal sensory disorders.

> [!pearl] Stool he cannot feel means hyposensitivity
> On digital rectal examination, if stool is present in the rectum and the patient is unaware of it, suspect rectal hyposensitivity ([[rome-v-2026-anorectal-disorders]]).

### Interpreting the Thresholds

- **Hypersensitivity to the maximum tolerated threshold (or pain) is the most clinically relevant phenotype.**
- Rome V documents hypersensitivity from **1 or more** reduced thresholds; one study instead required **≥2** thresholds below 2 SDs of healthy participants, giving operative volumes of **first sensation ≤20 mL, desire to defecate ≤80 mL, urgency to defecate or maximum tolerable volume ≤150 mL**.
- Hyposensitivity symptoms: lack of awareness of stooling, excessive straining, incomplete evacuation, digital maneuvers, <3 bowel movements weekly, prolonged toileting. Hypersensitivity symptoms: urgency, lower abdominal pain or cramps during/after defecation, on a background of FI, DD or IBS.

> [!pearl] The two disorders count thresholds differently
> Hyposensitivity needs **≥2** abnormal thresholds and **may** use first sensation; hypersensitivity needs only **1** and must **not** use first sensation (that threshold is less reliable).

## Therapeutics

### Hyposensitivity

- Hyposensitivity often coexists with **stool retention** → improving rectal emptying (laxatives, enemas, suppositories, or [[biofeedback-therapy|biofeedback]] for dyssynergia or FI) may itself help.
- **Sensory biofeedback is particularly useful**, and sensory training is the active ingredient: rectal electrical stimulation also enhanced sensation, whereas biofeedback **without** sensory training did not.
- Syringe-assisted sensory biofeedback improves hyposensitivity in DD and in FI, especially in diabetes. In a randomized controlled trial (RCT), **barostat-assisted sensory training was superior to syringe-assisted training in patients with ≥2 abnormal sensory thresholds**; both improved symptoms and were rated equally by patients, while therapists preferred the barostat method.

> [!howto] Sensory biofeedback titration
> 1. Start with a balloon volume that induces the urge to defecate.
> 2. Reduce by **5–10 mL** with each subsequent inflation, training the patient to recognize progressively smaller volumes.
> 3. If a sensation is not perceived, **increase the volume slightly** and have the patient focus on the subtle feeling rather than ignore it.
> 4. Repeat; the titration establishes lower sensory thresholds.

### Hypersensitivity

- **Sensory adaptation therapy (SAT)** — intermittent barostat-mediated phasic distentions in **1- to 2-mm Hg increments**, starting at the **desire-to-defecate threshold** and continuing until pain is reported on **2 consecutive inflations**; sessions repeated at **1- to 2-week intervals for 6 sessions over 3 months**.
- In an RCT, **SAT but not escitalopram significantly improved hypersensitivity and bowel symptoms**; both improved quality of life and psychological profiles; 80% of SAT pain responders were also hypersensitivity responders, and **SAT was better tolerated**.
- Possible therapies, listed without comparative data: hypnotherapy, psychotherapy, secretagogues, antispasmodics, tricyclic antidepressants, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, α2 agonists (e.g. clonidine), nitrates, and pregabalin.
