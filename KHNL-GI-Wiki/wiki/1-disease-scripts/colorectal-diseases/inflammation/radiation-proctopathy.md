## Assessment

- **Definition:** epithelial damage to the rectum from pelvic radiation therapy (RT) — bladder, cervical, prostate, and rectal cancer. Up to **30%** of patients irradiated for a pelvic malignancy develop it ([[asge-2019-radiation-proctopathy]]).
- "Proctopathy" is preferred over "proctitis" for the chronic form, which is *not* primarily inflammatory.

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- **Diagnosis = history of pelvic RT + the characteristic endoscopic findings.** No laboratory or imaging test establishes it.
- **Timing from RT separates acute from chronic** — the split that drives management (table below).
- Chronic disease presents with rectal bleeding, [[fecal-incontinence|fecal incontinence]], or symptoms from [[colonic-stricture|stricture]] or fistula; it may also be acute symptoms persisting past 3 months.

### Severity Assessment

- **No validated severity grade exists.** [[asge-2019-radiation-proctopathy|American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) 2019]] states there is an urgent need for a well-defined diagnostic criterion and a **unified endoscopic grading system**. Two scoring systems appear in the literature but require further validation before use in practice, and the document does not print their criteria — do not grade this disease off a named score.

> [!pitfall] Anemia, not appearance, decides whether to treat
> There is **no mandate to treat "cosmetic" rectal bleeding** — minor bleeding *without* anemia — because no studies support endoscopic management for that indication. Bleeding **with** anemia is the treatable indication ([[asge-2019-radiation-proctopathy]]).

### Classification / Typing

| | Acute radiation proctopathy | Chronic radiation proctopathy |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing** | During or ≤3 months of RT | ≥3 months; median onset 8–12 months |
| **Histology** | Superficial epithelial cell depletion + **acute inflammatory infiltrate** in the lamina propria | Obliterative/ischemic endarteritis of submucosal arterioles, submucosal fibrosis, neovascularization; **relative lack of inflammatory infiltrate** |
| **Presentation** | Abdominal/pelvic pain, tenesmus, diarrhea, urgency; bleeding and incontinence less common; usually self-limiting | Rectal bleeding, fecal incontinence, stricture or fistula symptoms |

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[acute-lower-gi-bleeding]].*

- [[ulcerative-colitis]] / other [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]] proctitis ([[crohns-disease]])
- Infectious proctitis (e.g., [[clostridioides-difficile|C. difficile]], sexually transmitted proctitis)
- [[colon-ischemia|Ischemic proctitis]]
- Rectal neoplasia / [[colorectal-cancer]]
- [[hemorrhoids|Hemorrhoidal bleeding]]
- [[angioectasia|Rectal angioectasia]]

## Diagnostics

- **Endoscopy is the main tool** (flexible sigmoidoscopy or [[colonoscopy]]) — it establishes the diagnosis and helps determine the extent and severity of disease. Findings: mucosal **pallor, edema, friability**, spontaneous bleeding, and **telangiectasias**.
- **Biopsy is discouraged.** Specimens can be taken to rule out another cause of proctitis (infection, IBD), but not otherwise.
- **[[bowel-preparation|Bowel preparation]]:** a complete prep is required before any planned thermal therapy — see the explosion risk under [[#Endoscopic Therapy]].

> [!pearl] Biopsy can create a non-healing ulcer
> Biopsy sampling of irradiated rectum risks a non-healing ulcer or fistula — take specimens only to exclude another cause of proctitis ([[asge-2019-radiation-proctopathy]]).

## Therapeutics

- **Acute proctopathy:** most cases respond to hydration, [[loperamide|antidiarrheal medication]], and discontinuation of RT.
- **Chronic proctopathy with bleeding:** medical and supportive therapy is often not effective or sufficient, particularly for rectal bleeding → endoscopic therapy has become the widely used treatment.
- **Consent before endoscopic therapy must cover** (ungraded panel statement): natural history of the disease · treatment options · risks and benefits of each approach · **how many therapy sessions and how far apart** · duration of follow-up.
- Weigh patient preferences, comorbidities, life expectancy, and which endoscopic therapies are locally available. The choice of medical vs endoscopic management is individualized to patient and provider preference, local resources, and expertise.

### Endoscopic Therapy

- **Recommendation 1:** suggest [[argon-plasma-coagulation|argon plasma coagulation (APC)]], [[electrosurgery|bipolar electrocoagulation]], [[endoscopic-hemostasis|heater probe]], and [[radiofrequency-ablation|radiofrequency ablation (RFA)]] for bleeding from chronic radiation proctopathy. **There is insufficient evidence to recommend a specific modality over another.** *(Conditional recommendation, Low quality of evidence)*
- **Read the success rates with the document's own composite definition of clinical success:** *any* of bleeding cessation · hemoglobin improvement by 10% or normalization · bleeding-score improvement · eradication of telangiectasias. The pooled 87%–88% figures are that composite, **not** bleeding cessation alone.
- **Serious adverse event** was defined a priori across all modalities as colonic **fistula, perforation, explosion, or stricture**.

| Modality | Clinical success | Sessions (mean/median) | Interval | Settings as printed | Serious adverse events |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **APC** — noncontact argon gas; most widely used | **87%** (95% confidence interval (CI) 85%–90%); 33 studies, 957 patients | 1–3.7 | 2–8 wk; **most studies 3–4 wk** (lets injured mucosa heal) | Power **25–80 W** (median **50 W**); argon flow **0.6–2.5 L/min** (median **1.5 L/min**). Success similar at ≤1 vs >1 L/min (87% vs 86%) | Pooled **4%** (95% CI 3%–6%). Most common adverse event is abdominal, rectal, or anal pain |
| **Bipolar electrocoagulation** — contact, electrical current | **88%** (95% CI 68%–96%); 4 studies, 96 patients | 2.9–4 | 4–6 wk (one trial 30 days) | **50 W** in the 2 studies that reported a setting | Limited data. One randomized trial: worsening rectal bleeding during/after the procedure **33% (5/15)**, rectal stenosis **27% (4/15**, only 1 symptomatic). **No perforation or fistula reported** for this indication |
| **Heater probe** — contact, direct heat not current | 8/8 in a case series; **6/9 (67%)** in the one randomized comparison (vs bipolar 75%, 12-mo follow-up) | 1–4 | 4–6 wk | **200–400 J per session** in the case series; the randomized trial used **10–15 J** — ASGE 2019 does not reconcile the two | **None reported** |
| **RFA** — contact, single-use focal ablation catheter | 15/17 (**88%**) and 39/39 (**100%**); 3 case series, 66 patients; **no comparative trial or meta-analysis** | 1.5–1.9 | 1 month; **12–16 wk** in the largest series | Energy density **12–15 J/cm²** at power density **40 W/cm²** | **None reported**; mild anorectal pain in up to **12% of sessions** |
| **Cryoablation** — noncontact carbon dioxide or liquid nitrogen *(not part of Recommendation 1)* | 7/7 (**100%**) and 7/10 (**70%**); 2 case series, 17 patients; **no comparative trial** | 1–3.7 | 2–3 days in the refractory series | **Not reported** in either series | Up to **10%** — 1 perforation in 10 patients requiring colectomy, attributed to over-insufflation from a failed decompression tube rather than cryotherapy itself |

- **APC technique:** short **noncontact applications of 1–2 seconds**; **target individual vessels and avoid "painting"**, which causes large confluent ulcers; **suction the insufflated argon periodically** (bowel distension from rapid argon instillation is a main driver of the pain). Device-level detail across indications: [[argon-plasma-coagulation]].
- ⚠ **Complete bowel preparation before APC.** Colonic explosions were reported in **2 poorly prepped patients who received an enema preparation only**.
- Most published APC series used the **first-generation generator (ICC/APC 300)**, which may not be available in most endoscopy units; only 3 of the 33 studies used the second-generation machine.

> [!howto] Ablating the distal rectum with RFA
> 1. **2 applications per site.**
> 2. Ablate to **~1 mm proximal to the dentate line** — prevents sensory injury to the anal mucosa.
> 3. Restrict treatment to a **short length, <6 cm to the dentate line**.
> 4. Withdraw scope and device for cleaning **every 8 applications** to preserve electrode effectiveness.
> 5. **Leave the coagulum on the treated mucosa** — do not scrape it off; it promotes hemostasis. ([[asge-2019-radiation-proctopathy]])

- **Recommendation 3 (cryoablation):** insufficient evidence for or against the newer-generation cryoablation system. *(Printed with no strength and no quality rating — an insufficient-evidence statement, not a recommendation against.)* **Both systems actually studied have been discontinued**, and no data exist on new-generation devices.

### Topical Formalin

- **Recommendation 2:** suggest **against** 4% formalin compared with APC, because of higher adverse-event rates. *(Conditional recommendation, Low quality of evidence)*
- The two comparative studies disagree on efficacy, and the recommendation rests on adverse events alone:
  - Randomized trial, 30 patients with intractable bleeding (≥1 episode/week or transfusion-requiring despite medical therapy): bleeding ceased in **100% with formalin vs 94% with APC**, median 2 sessions each, no difference in efficacy or durability, and **no adverse events in either arm**.
  - Cohort, 25 patients: clinical success **27% (3/11) with formalin vs 79% (11/14) with APC** (P = .017), similar session numbers (1.8 vs 1.7), **adverse events 82% vs 36%** (P = .001) — nausea, vomiting, rectal pain, fever.
- ASGE 2019 gives the concentration (4%) but no application volume, contact time, or number of applications.

### Medical Therapy

- **Not systematically reviewed** in this document, which names the agents without any dose, concentration, or schedule, and makes no recommendation about any of them.
- Randomized trials exist for oral metronidazole, short-chain fatty acids, formalin, and sulfasalazine/[[mesalamine-5-asa|5-aminosalicylic acids]].
- Also used, **some with mixed success:** steroids, antioxidants, **sucralfate enemas**, and **hyperbaric oxygen**.

### Surgery

- **Usually the last therapeutic option** — fewer than **10%** of patients with chronic radiation proctopathy ultimately require it.
- **Indications:** perforation · intractable bleeding, or bleeding refractory to medical and/or endoscopic therapy · obstructing stricture · fistula formation.
