## Assessment

- **Rectal prolapse = intussusception of the rectal wall through the anal canal**, from loss of normal rectal attachments. [[aga-2026-hemorrhoids]]
- It is a **structural** disorder, not a [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|disorder of gut–brain interaction]] — [[rome-v-2026-dgbi|Rome V]] removed the "functional defecation disorders" category in part *because* that label swept in structural abnormalities such as rectal prolapse and perineal descent. [[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]
- **Pathogenesis is straining over time:** chronic excessive straining weakens the pelvic floor, producing excessive perineal descent, rectal intussusception, solitary rectal ulcer syndrome, and pudendal neuropathy. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]

**What comes with it** ([[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]]) — these are the symptoms that drive the operative choice, so ask about all of them:

| Association | Frequency |
|---|---|
| [[fecal-incontinence\|Fecal incontinence (FI)]] | **50%–75%** |
| [[chronic-constipation\|Constipation]] | **25%–50%** |
| Pudendal neuropathy | **up to 50%** — may cause denervation atrophy of the external sphincter |
| Urinary incontinence | **20%–35%** |
| Significant vaginal vault prolapse | **≈15%–30%** |
| Anterior solitary rectal ulcer on proctoscopy | **10%–15%** |

- **Multivisceral prolapse requires a multidisciplinary approach.** [[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]]

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- **Diagnosis is made by seeing the prolapse.** Examine in the left lateral position first.
- **Suspected but not shown in the left lateral position → repeat the examination squatting or sitting on a toilet/commode.** [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]
- **Still not shown** ([[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]], Evaluation Rec 1, Strong/low 1C): have the patient **strain on a toilet, with or without an enema or a rectal balloon**, and inspect sitting or squatting; failing that, the patient can **photograph it at home** or undergo echography. Expect a **patulous anus with diminished sphincter tone**.
- **Distinguish it from prolapsing [[hemorrhoids]] at inspection:** [[aga-2026-hemorrhoids]]

| Finding | Rectal prolapse | Prolapsed hemorrhoids |
|---|---|---|
| **Mucosal folds** | **Circular** folds of pink rectal mucosa | **Radial** folds |
| **Mechanism** | Intussusception of rectal wall through anal canal; loss of rectal attachments | Enlargement and displacement of the anal vascular cushions |

### Classification / Typing

**Full-thickness (overt) prolapse should be distinguished from mucosal prolapse and internal intussusception.** [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]

| Type | What the guideline says |
|---|---|
| **Full-thickness (overt) rectal prolapse** | Consider surgical correction **with or without defecatory symptoms and/or progressive FI**; leaving it untreated leads to **very significant morbidity** |
| **Mucosal prolapse** | **More benign course**; may be found in asymptomatic patients |
| **Internal intussusception** | **More benign course**; may be found in asymptomatic patients; ulceration at its lead point = [[#Solitary Rectal Ulcer Syndrome]] |

- Neither the [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders|American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) 2021]] nor the [[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse|American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (ASCRS) 2017]] guideline gives a severity grading or staging scale; the only length cut-off is **<5 cm**, which selects the Delorme procedure ([[#Operations Compared]]).

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup of the defecatory-symptom presentation: see [[chronic-constipation]]; the prolapse itself is diagnosed by positional examination ([[#Establishing the Diagnosis]]).*

- Prolapsing hemorrhoids — radial folds and engorged vessels vs. circular pink mucosal folds (table above). [[aga-2026-hemorrhoids]]
- **Mucosal prolapse** and **internal intussusception** — more benign course. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]
- **Rectocele** — also identified on defecography; commonly present in **asymptomatic** women and usually does **not** require surgery. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]
- **Enterocele / sigmoidocele**, bladder and uterovaginal prolapse — co-identified on defecography once the vagina and small intestine are opacified; the utility of repairing enteroceles/sigmoidoceles is unclear. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]], [[aga-2026-refractory-constipation]]
- [[defecation-disorders|Dyssynergic defecation]] — the functional mimic; may coexist. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]
- **Solitary rectal ulcer syndrome** — when found at endoscopy, it **indicates rectal prolapse**. [[asge-2014-constipation]]

## Diagnostics

Additional testing — defecography, colonoscopy, barium enema, urodynamics — **may be used selectively** to refine the diagnosis and find coexisting pathology ([[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]], Evaluation Rec 2, Strong/moderate 1B).

| Test | Role |
|---|---|
| **Defecography** (barium or magnetic resonance (MR)) | Identifies **internal intussusception, solitary rectal ulcers, rectoceles, and rectal prolapse**; also enteroceles, bladder and uterovaginal prolapse when vagina/small bowel are opacified. Consider when clinical features suggest a structural abnormality, or when [[anorectal-manometry\|anorectal manometry (ARM)]] and balloon expulsion are inconclusive [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]], [[aga-2026-refractory-constipation]] |
| **Anal physiologic testing** (ARM + balloon expulsion) | **May be considered** to assess and treat coexisting constipation or FI (Evaluation Rec 3, Weak/low 2C). **Decreased pudendal nerve terminal motor latency may predict more incontinence after repair** (needs confirmation) [[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]] |
| **[[colonoscopy\|Colonoscopy]]** | Not diagnostic of prolapse, but a **solitary rectal ulcer** found incidentally indicates prolapse [[asge-2014-constipation]]. **Should typically be done before prolapse surgery** — rarely a neoplasm is the lead point, the population is older, and a finding **may change the operative plan** [[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]] |
| **Urodynamics / urogynecologic examination** | Anterior-compartment or urinary-incontinence symptoms → allows concomitant repair of anterior and posterior compartments [[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]] |

- Caveat on defecography: **structural abnormalities of the pelvic floor occur commonly in asymptomatic subjects and usually do not require surgical correction**, and surgical restoration of structure often does not restore function. The named **exceptions** are **overt rectal prolapse** and a **sizeable, non-emptying rectocele** with typical vaginal-bulge/prolapse symptoms. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]

## Therapeutics

> **ACG 2021 Recommendation (defecation disorders, Table 2, #2):** "We suggest that **full-thickness rectal prolapse often requires surgical treatment with abdominal rectopexy or in selected cases a perineal procedure**." *(Conditional recommendation; moderate risk of harm; quality of evidence: very low. Consensus score: 30.)* [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]

- **Indication:** overt full-thickness prolapse, **with or without defecatory symptoms and/or progressive FI** — surgery relieves symptoms and improves continence; evidence is low-level, but the untreated alternative carries very significant morbidity. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]
- **Contrast with a [[defecation-disorders|defecation disorder]] without overt prolapse:** there the ACG algorithm reaches "consider surgery" only after [[biofeedback-therapy|biofeedback]] fails **and** defecography shows a structural abnormality (Figure 1). [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]

### Nonoperative Management

**Rectal prolapse cannot be corrected nonoperatively** — there are **no reports of it resolving on medical therapy alone**; associated FI, pain and constipation can be palliated ([[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]], Nonoperative Rec 1, Weak/low 2C):

- **Constipation** → fiber and stool softeners.
- **Incarcerated prolapse** → **table sugar applied topically** reduces edema and assists reduction.
- **Skin care** to avoid maceration.
- **Pre-treating incontinence symptoms before surgery** gave better postoperative continence (retrospective, 139 women).

> [!pitfall] Watchful waiting has a price, including in the elderly
> Patients managed medically only **develop irreversible FI**, and **prolapse left untreated beyond 4 years** may lead to **higher recurrence after eventual repair**. Surgical candidates — **including the elderly** — should avoid unnecessary delay and occasional bowel incarceration ([[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]]).

### Choosing the Operation

**Abdominal vs perineal** ([[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]]):
- **Acceptable-risk patients → the operation should typically incorporate transabdominal rectal fixation** (Abdominal Rec 1, Weak/moderate 2B).
- Approach is usually dictated by **comorbidities, surgeon preference and experience, patient age and bowel function**; ASCRS asks for careful weighing of comorbidity and bowel dysfunction rather than a fixed rule.
- Evidence: abdominal recurrence ≈ **one fourth** of perineal in retrospective series, but Cochrane reviews and a 293-patient randomized controlled trial (RCT) found **no significant difference** (trials underpowered). Perineal mortality may be underestimated: **4-fold mortality in high-ASA patients** treated perineally (1469 patients).
- [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders|ACG 2021]]: **laparoscopic rectopexy (posterior or ventral) is the most appropriate operation for most patients**, on recurrence; **perineal (Altemeier, Delorme) is a reasonable option for the elderly or frail**.

| Category | Options | Effect on constipation | Global improvement / quality of life (QOL) | Harm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rectal suspension** | Open or laparoscopic posterior or ventral rectopexy | Variable, often not measured; up to 86% (ventral mesh rectopexy) | **73%–91% "some benefit"** | **5%–15% serious**; 0%–4% mesh erosion; 2%–7% anatomic recurrence |
| **Perineal** | E.g., Altemeier | Not reported | "All reported improvement in QOL" | **26.7% recurrence**; 9.1% early complications |

- Ventral mesh rectopexy improves preoperative constipation in **66%–86%**; ACG's text rounds Altemeier recurrence to **27%**. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]
- **Minimally invasive over open when feasible, by experienced surgeons** (Additional Abdominal Rec 1, Strong/moderate 1B): equivalent recurrence (4%–8%) and morbidity (10%–33%), better pain control, shorter stay, faster return of bowel function. **Robotic appears comparable to laparoscopic**, with longer operating time and higher cost. [[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]]
- **Posterior vs anterior repair is not ranked** (Abdominal Rec 2, Strong/low 1C: insufficient evidence; both <10% recurrence at 10 years) — choose by surgical expertise, surgical history, comorbidity and patient preference. [[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]]

**Bowel function changes the operation** ([[ascrs-2017-rectal-prolapse]]):

> [!pearl] Prolapse plus incontinence — correct the prolapse first
> Many patients' FI improves once the prolapse is treated, so ASCRS corrects the prolapse as a first step; pre-existing FI, or FI from another process, is evaluated per the ASCRS FI guideline. Here constipating manoeuvres may help: **lateral stalk division lowers recurrence but worsens constipation** (Weak/moderate 2B) — "especially attractive in the patients presenting with fecal incontinence without constipation."

- **Constipation and/or dyssynergia on testing → may not be ideal candidates** for manoeuvres known to worsen constipation: posterior rectal mobilization, lateral-ligament transection during suture rectopexy, levatorplasty during perineal proctectomy.
- **Severe constipation → should probably undergo more than a suture rectopexy:** resection rectopexy, ventral rectopexy, or a perineal repair.
- ⚠ **Suture rectopexy can create or worsen constipation:** **≥50%** of constipated patients worsen; **≈15%** without prior constipation develop it.

### Operations Compared

| Operation | ASCRS recommendation (grade) | Recurrence | Decision notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Posterior mobilization without rectopexy** (± anterior resection) | **Typically not recommended** — higher recurrence and complications (Strong/moderate 1B) | With low anterior resection: 3% / 6% / 12% at 2 / 5 / 10 y | Morbidity 29% incl. anastomotic leaks; low pelvic anastomosis may cost function in borderline continence |
| **Suture rectopexy** (posterior) | **Rectopexy is a key component of the abdominal approach** (Strong/high 1A) | 3%–9% at 2 y; may rise to **29% at 10 y** | RCT: **8-fold recurrence** with mobilization alone vs rectopexy; worsens constipation (above) |
| **Resection rectopexy** (+ sigmoid resection) | **May be added for preoperative constipation** (Strong/moderate 1B) | 2%–5% | Major complications 0%–20%; less postoperative constipation than rectopexy alone (2 RCTs). ⚠ **Avoid after a failed perineal rectosigmoidectomy — ischemic bowel.** ⚠ **FI resolves less often**; some experts would not offer it with **markedly reduced anal pressures on manometry or severe baseline incontinence**. Not usually combined with mesh |
| **Posterior mesh rectopexy** (McMahan–Ripstein modification) | **May be used, but higher morbidity** (Strong/low 1C) | 2%–5% | Morbidity **20%**, mostly minor; **FI improves in 20%–60%**. Original anterior-wall Ripstein: excessive complications (obstruction, mesh erosion, ureteral injury, [[rectovaginal-fistula\|rectovaginal fistula]], fecal impaction) |
| **Modified Wells** (posterior fixation with foreign material) | **May be used** (Weak/moderate 2B) | — | No data that mesh beats sutures alone; Ivalon sponge abandoned |
| **Ventral mesh rectopexy** (D'Hoore) | **Acceptable alternative** — short- and long-term complication rates acceptable (Strong/low 1C) | 3.4% (728 patients); **8.2% at 10 y** (919 patients) | **Limited anterior mobilization, no posterior dissection** → **84% improvement in constipation, no de novo constipation**; mesh complications **4.6%**. Consensus panel suggested it for **pre-existing constipation and anterior-compartment abnormalities (enterocele)**. ⚠ **"Has yet to gain full acceptance in the United States"** |
| **Delorme** (perineal mucosal sleeve resection) | **Short-segment full-thickness prolapse can be treated with mucosal sleeve resection** (Strong/low 1C) | 10%–15% | **Short (<5 cm) prolapse**; mucosal sleeve resection + muscularis imbrication. Early complications **4%–12%**; urgency and tenesmus occur |
| **Altemeier** (perineal rectosigmoidectomy) | **May be used** (Strong/low 1C) | **16%–30% at 2 y** | **Can be done without general anesthesia**; shorter stay, lower complications than transabdominal. **Adding levatorplasty cut recurrence 21% → 7%** (1 study) — but levatorplasty may not suit constipated patients (above) |

### Who Does Worse After Surgery

Added caution — worse outcomes reported in: psychiatric disorders; chronic pain or [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)]]; morbid [[obesity]]; joint hypermobility; connective tissue disorders; women planning pregnancy; and those at high risk for pelvic surgery from previous surgery, infection, or radiotherapy. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]

### Solitary Rectal Ulcer Syndrome

- **Surgery (e.g. laparoscopic ventral mesh rectopexy) may be considered when biofeedback and behavioral interventions are not effective** — especially with ulceration at **the lead point of an internal rectal intussusception**. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]
- Across 2 studies (75 patients), **the ulcer healed in 78% of cases**. [[acg-2021-anorectal-disorders]]
