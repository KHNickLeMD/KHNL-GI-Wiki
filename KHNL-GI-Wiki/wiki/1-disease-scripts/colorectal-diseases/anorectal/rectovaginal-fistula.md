A rectovaginal fistula (RVF) is a **tract between the rectum or anal canal and the vagina** — a unique subset of [[anal-fistula|fistula-in-ano]] in many respects, classified by **level** (low / middle / high) and as **simple or complex**, and most commonly the result of **obstetric injury** ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula|American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (ASCRS) 2022]]).

## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- Initial evaluation should assess the **underlying cause** — obstetric trauma, [[crohns-disease|Crohn's disease]], cryptoglandular infection, or malignancy.
- **Examination under anesthesia (EUA) and radiologic assessment are often necessary** to define the anatomy of the fistula tract and evaluate the tissues involved ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]]).

> [!pearl] Sphincter status drives the choice of repair
> The status of the anal sphincter complex plays an integral role in the choice of repair, so assessment of anal sphincter anatomy and function is a key step in evaluating every patient with an RVF ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]]).

### Classification by Level

| Level | Tract |
|---|---|
| **Low** | Between the **distal anal canal** (at or below the dentate line) and the inside of the **posterior fourchette** |
| **Middle** | A tract lying between the low and high levels |
| **High** | Connects the **upper vagina** (at the level of the cervix) with the **rectum** |

- The terms **"anovaginal fistula" and "low rectovaginal fistula" may be used interchangeably**.

### Simple vs Complex

| | Criteria ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]]) |
|---|---|
| **Simple** | **Low**, **small-diameter (<2 cm)** communication between the anal canal and vagina; typically results from **obstetrical injury or infection** |
| **Complex** | **Higher** tract between rectum and vagina · **larger** diameter · or results from **radiation, cancer, or complications of pelvic surgical procedures** |

### Etiology

- **Obstetric injury** is the most common cause.
- Also: Crohn's disease · malignancy · infection · complication of a **failed colorectal anastomosis** · complication of an **anorectal operation** · **radiation therapy**.
- Fistulization of a colorectal anastomosis to the vagina was reported in up to **2.2%** of cases in earlier studies; more recent publications report **higher rates of RVF after failed anastomoses**.

## Differential Diagnosis

## Diagnostics

- **Imaging** — the modalities used for anorectal abscess and fistula and their reported performance live on [[anal-fistula#Diagnostics|fistula-in-ano]].
- **Anal sphincter anatomy and function** — [[anorectal-manometry]] and endoanal ultrasound.
- Routine diagnostic imaging is **not** typically necessary for anorectal abscess or fistula. Imaging **may be considered** in selected patients with an occult anorectal abscess, **recurrent or complex** anal fistula, **immunosuppression**, or **anorectal Crohn's disease** ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]] rec 2, *strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence, 1B*).

## Therapeutics

**Not one technique of repair is appropriate for all RVFs**, but the available evidence can help determine effective treatment strategies. **Fibrin glue therapy and the fistula plug are not included in the ASCRS 2022 recommendations because the success of these interventions has proven prohibitively poor for RVF.**

### Choosing the Repair

| Repair | Typically used for | Grade | Reported fistula healing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nonoperative care** | **Initial** care of obstetrical RVF; also considered for other benign, minimally symptomatic fistulas (rec 12) | Weak, low-quality, **2C** | **52–66%** |
| **Draining seton** | Resolution of acute inflammation or infection associated with an RVF (rec 13) | Strong, low-quality, **1C** | Adjunct — no healing rate given |
| **Endorectal advancement flap ± sphincteroplasty** | **Most patients** with an RVF (rec 14) | Strong, low-quality, **1C** | **41–78%**; repeat flap after a failed repair **55–93%** |
| **Episioproctotomy** | Obstetrical or cryptoglandular RVF **with anal sphincter defects** (rec 15) | Strong, low-quality, **1C** | **78–100%** |
| **Gracilis or bulbocavernosus (Martius) flap** | **Recurrent or otherwise complex** RVF (rec 16) | Strong, low-quality, **1C** | Gracilis **50–92%**; Martius reported only as small series (below) |
| **Transabdominal repair** | RVF from **colorectal anastomotic** complications (rec 17) | Strong, low-quality, **1C** | Diversion alone **6 of 16 (37%)** |
| **Completion proctectomy** ± colonic pull-through or coloanal anastomosis | **Radiation-related or recurrent complex** RVF (rec 18) | Weak, low-quality, **2C** | Sleeve excision **75%** and **11 of 14 (79%)** |

### Nonoperative Management

- **Components:** baths · wound care · debridement as needed · antibiotics in cases of infection · stool-bulking fiber supplements.
- **Duration: usually 3 to 6 months**, aiming to resolve acute inflammation and infection.
- Benign, minimally symptomatic RVFs **unrelated to obstetrical injury** may also be successfully managed nonoperatively, although follow-up is limited.

### Draining Seton

- Helpful in treating or **preventing formation of an abscess within the rectovaginal septum**, particularly with a **narrow fistula, small-diameter vaginal side opening, or multiple tracts**.
- **Long-term symptomatic relief** for patients who are poor candidates for definitive repair, and for those with an **active inflammatory or neoplastic process** needing treatment before definitive repair.
- In candidates for definitive repair, a seton relieves acute inflammation, edema and infection and may **improve the likelihood of success of the subsequent definitive repair**.

### Endorectal Advancement Flap

- Healing depends on the cause of the fistula, operative technique, and the definition of fistula healing used.
- **Factors associated with failure:** endosonographic and manometric abnormalities of the anal sphincter complex · Crohn's disease · previous pelvic radiation · recurrent fistula. A **history of failed attempts at repair** also increases the risk of flap failure.
- **Sphincter defect → add sphincteroplasty.** Results of a flap alone in patients with RVF and [[fecal-incontinence|fecal incontinence]] with a known sphincter defect are historically poor: among 52 patients with "simple" obstetrical RVFs (48% with varying degrees of fecal incontinence) undergoing 62 procedures, healing was **11 of 27 (41%)** after endorectal advancement flap versus **28 of 35 (80%)** after sphincteroplasty ± levatorplasty. Others report >80% healing with sphincteroplasty.
- **Diverting stoma** has **not** been shown to significantly improve outcomes of a flap for RVF; diversion should be considered on an individual case basis.

> [!pitfall] A rectal flap on a low RVF causes ectropion
> With a low RVF, an endorectal advancement flap may cause **mucosal ectropion** — harvest the flap from the **anoderm and perianal skin** instead. Combined with sphincteroplasty, this gave 100% healing at mean 24 months in 7 consecutive patients with obstetrical low RVFs ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]]).

### Episioproctotomy

- **Transperineal technique:** division of various degrees of the **anterior anal sphincter complex and rectovaginal septum**, then repair. **Differs from sphincteroplasty** in the amount of perineal skin, external anal sphincter and rectovaginal septum that must be divided to reach and repair the RVF.
- **Functional outcome is acceptable.** In 50 patients with obstetrical or cryptoglandular RVFs, postoperative fecal incontinence was "rare" in 46 (92%) at median follow-up 49 months; of the 25 patients (50%) with **preoperative** fecal incontinence, only **4 (8%)** had postoperative incontinence. In a smaller series of 8 obstetrical fistulas, all healed and none reported fecal incontinence (follow-up 6 months to 8 years).
- **A stoma is not required.** Of 42 patients with mostly obstetrical RVFs and significant anterior anal sphincter defects (recurrence in 11, 26%), 23 (55%) had a stoma at the time of episioproctotomy, but **fecal diversion was not significantly associated with outcomes**. In another cohort of 50 patients, temporary diversion was reserved for those with a **recurrent fistula or extensive scarring** (36 patients, 72%). Where a stoma preceded repair, most were closed within **3 months** (median 3.4 mo).

### Tissue Interposition Flaps

| Flap | Reported outcomes |
|---|---|
| **Gracilis muscle** | Largest series **19 of 24 (79%)** healed. Postoperative complications **28–47%** — most commonly surgical site infection, thigh numbness, hematoma. Quality of life improves (36-Item Short-Form Survey scores and sexual function) |
| **Martius (bulbocavernosus)** | 24 patients with low RVF (42% obstetrical): success **91%** at mean 42 months · 20 consecutive patients (70% diverted): healing **13 of 20 (65%)** at mean 35 months, minor complications in 3 (15%) · 14 patients (6 with Crohn's disease), all diverted: **13 of 14 (93%)** · radiation-related RVF: **11 of 12** and **13 of 14** |

- **Diverting ostomy is generally recommended as an adjunct** to Martius and gracilis flap repairs — reported in **63–100%** of these patients — **although supporting evidence is lacking**.
- Contrary data: 176 patients with RVFs at multiple centers (including 9 Martius flaps) had **81%** healing with a variety of repairs and a nonspecified "low rate" of stoma formation, mainly attributed to repairs being performed by urogynecologists; 16 women treated with Martius flaps using **selective** diversion in 38% had **94%** success at mean 1.5 years; a small series of Crohn's-related RVFs repaired with Martius flaps **without** a covering stoma reported **100%** success at mean 18 months.

### Transabdominal Repair

- **Fecal diversion is generally recommended as the initial step**, to resolve the acute inflammation and associated symptoms; in selected cases with an **immediate or early postoperative** RVF, reoperation and repeat colorectal anastomosis may be preferable.
- **Healing with diversion alone** occurred in 6 of 16 patients (37%) with an RVF from a failed colorectal anastomosis; persistent fistulas were then treated with **repeat colorectal anastomosis, endorectal advancement flap, or a transperineal interposition flap**.

### Completion Proctectomy

- Recurrent complex RVFs and fistulas developing after **pelvic radiation** may be amenable to **muscle flap interposition** (above) or proctectomy with primary or staged coloanal anastomosis.
- **Sleeve excision technique** — a variation of conventional proctectomy: resection of the rectum proximal to the fistula tract, mucosectomy of the rectum at and distal to the fistula, pull-through of healthy colon through the remaining muscular tube of the rectum, and a sutured coloanal anastomosis. In women with RVF after pelvic radiotherapy for cervical cancer: healing **11 of 14 (79%)**, "good" functional results in all who healed. In a more recent study of 34 patients: early postoperative complications **51%**, late complications **32%**, fistula healing **75%**, normal postoperative continence in **18 of 25 (72%)** surveyed.
- **Restoring continuity.** In 67 patients operated for a variety of indications (only 3 with RVF), the **Turnbull-Cutait** staged coloanal anastomosis had lower anastomotic leak (**3% vs 7%**) and pelvic abscess (**0% vs 5%**) than primary coloanal anastomosis, with similar functional outcomes. Of 79 patients with RVF (43% from Crohn's disease), 19 underwent resection with primary (n = 8) or delayed (n = 11) coloanal anastomosis — overall success **91%**. In 10 patients with radiation-related RVF treated by pull-through and straight coloanal anastomosis with a diverting loop [[ostomy-management|ileostomy]], **all healed without an anastomotic leak**; fecal incontinence quality-of-life index and depression, lifestyle and embarrassment scores improved, with no significant change in reported continence.
- **Proctectomy vs stoma alone.** Many patients report symptomatic relief from a diverting stoma that leaves the affected rectum in situ, but in radiation-related RVF, proctectomy plus diverting stoma (n = 10) gave **significantly improved quality of life** and **significantly less tenesmus and anal discharge at 6 and 12 months** than stoma alone (n = 16).
