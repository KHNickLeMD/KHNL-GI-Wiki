A rectovaginal fistula (RVF) is a **tract between the rectum or anal canal and the vagina** — a subset of [[anal-fistula|fistula-in-ano]] in many respects, but classified by **level** rather than by sphincter relationship, and most often caused by **obstetric injury** rather than cryptoglandular infection ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula|American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (ASCRS) 2022]]).

**The anal sphincter complex plays an integral role in the choice of repair** — assessment of sphincter anatomy and function is a key step in the evaluation of every patient with an RVF.

## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- Initial evaluation should assess the **underlying cause** — obstetric trauma, [[crohns-disease|Crohn's disease]], cryptoglandular infection, or malignancy.
- **Examination under anesthesia (EUA) and radiologic assessment are often necessary** to define the anatomy of the tract and evaluate the tissues involved.
- **Assessment of anal sphincter anatomy and function is a key step** in every patient, because it determines whether the repair needs a concomitant sphincteroplasty ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]]).

### Classification by Level

| Level | Tract |
|---|---|
| **Low** | Between the **distal anal canal** (at or below the dentate line) and the inside of the **posterior fourchette** |
| **Middle** | A tract lying between the low and high levels |
| **High** | Connects the **upper vagina** (at the level of the cervix) with the **rectum** |

- The terms **"anovaginal fistula" and "low rectovaginal fistula" may be used interchangeably**.

### Simple vs Complex

| | Criteria ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]]) |
|---|---|
| **Simple** | **Low**, **small-diameter (<2 cm)** communication between the anal canal and vagina; typically results from **obstetrical injury or infection** |
| **Complex** | **Higher** tract between rectum and vagina · **larger** diameter · or results from **radiation, cancer, or complications of pelvic surgical procedures** |

### Etiology

- **Obstetric injury** is the most common cause.
- Also: [[crohns-disease|Crohn's disease]] · malignancy · infection · complication of a **failed colorectal anastomosis** · complication of an **anorectal operation** · **radiation therapy**.
- Fistulization of a colorectal anastomosis to the vagina was reported in up to **2.2%** of cases in earlier studies; more recent publications report **higher rates of RVF after failed anastomoses**.

## Differential Diagnosis

[[anal-fistula|Cryptoglandular fistula-in-ano]] · [[perianal-fistulizing-crohns-disease|perianal fistulizing Crohn's disease]] · anastomotic leak after colorectal resection · radiation injury ([[radiation-proctopathy]]) · anorectal or gynecologic malignancy · [[anorectal-abscess]] with vaginal extension ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]]).

## Diagnostics

- **Examination under anesthesia** to define tract anatomy and the quality of surrounding tissue.
- **Radiologic assessment** to define the tract and evaluate the tissues involved; the imaging modalities and their performance are the same as for [[anal-fistula#Diagnostics|fistula-in-ano]].
- **Assessment of anal sphincter anatomy and function** — see [[anorectal-manometry]] and endoanal ultrasound on [[anal-fistula]].
- Routine diagnostic imaging is **not** typically necessary for anorectal abscess or fistula generally, but **is** considered in recurrent or complex fistula ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]] rec 2, *1B*).

## Therapeutics

**No single technique is appropriate for all RVFs** — the available evidence determines effective treatment strategies, and the repair is chosen from the cause, the level, and the state of the sphincter complex. **Fibrin glue therapy and the fistula plug are not included in the ASCRS 2022 recommendations because the success of these interventions has proven prohibitively poor for RVF.**

### Nonoperative Management

**Nonoperative management is typically recommended for the initial care of obstetrical RVF and may also be considered for other benign and minimally symptomatic fistulas** ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]] rec 12, *weak recommendation, low-quality evidence, 2C*).

- **Components:** baths · wound care · debridement as needed · antibiotics in cases of infection · stool-bulking agents.
- **Duration: usually 3 to 6 months.** The aim is to resolve acute inflammation and infection.
- **Healing rates 52–66%** with a nonoperative approach, from an older meta-analysis and more recent studies.
- Benign, minimally symptomatic RVFs **unrelated to obstetrical injury** may also be successfully managed nonoperatively, although follow-up is limited.

### Draining Seton

**A draining seton may facilitate resolution of acute inflammation or infection associated with rectovaginal fistulas** ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]] rec 13, *strong recommendation, low-quality evidence, 1C*).

- Helpful in treating or **preventing formation of an abscess within the rectovaginal septum**, particularly in patients with a **narrow fistula, small-diameter vaginal side opening, or multiple tracts**.
- May provide **long-term symptomatic relief** for patients who are poor candidates for definitive repair.
- In patients who **are** candidates for definitive repair, a draining seton reduces acute inflammation, edema and infection and may **improve the likelihood of success of subsequent definitive repair**.

### Endorectal Advancement Flap

**Endorectal advancement flap with or without sphincteroplasty is the procedure of choice for most patients with a rectovaginal fistula** ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]] rec 14, *strong recommendation, low-quality evidence, 1C*).

- **Success 41–78%**, depending on the cause of the fistula, operative technique, and the definition of fistula healing used.
- **Factors associated with failure:** endosonographic and manometric abnormalities of the anal sphincter complex · Crohn's disease · previous pelvic radiation · recurrent fistula.
- **A history of failed attempts at fistula repair increases the risk for endorectal advancement flap failure**, although success with repeat flaps has been reported in **55–93%**.
- **Add sphincteroplasty when there is a known sphincter defect.** In 52 patients with "simple" obstetric RVFs (48% with varying degrees of fecal incontinence) undergoing 62 procedures, healing was reported in **11 of 27 (41%)** who underwent endorectal advancement flap alone versus **28 of 35 (80%)** who underwent flap ± levatorplasty with sphincteroplasty. Higher rates (>80%) with sphincteroplasty have also been reported by others.
- **Low RVF caveat:** an endorectal advancement flap may cause **mucosal ectropion** — under these circumstances an alternative flap harvested from the **anoderm and perianal skin** instead of the rectum should be considered. This technique combined with sphincteroplasty achieved **100% healing** at mean 24 months in 7 consecutive patients with obstetrical low RVFs.
- **Diverting stoma:** has **not** been shown to significantly improve outcomes of endorectal advancement flap for RVF; diversion should be considered on an individual case basis.

### Episioproctotomy

**Episioproctotomy may be used to repair obstetrical or cryptoglandular rectovaginal fistulas in patients with anal sphincter defects** ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]] rec 15, *strong recommendation, low-quality evidence, 1C*).

- **A transperineal technique involving division of various degrees of the anterior anal sphincter complex and rectovaginal septum**, then repair.
- **Differs from sphincteroplasty** in the amount of perineal skin, external anal sphincter, and rectovaginal septum that needs to be divided to reach and repair the RVF.
- **Fistula healing 78–100%** with acceptable functional outcomes.
  - 42 patients, mostly obstetrical RVFs associated with significant anterior and sphincter defects: recurrent fistulas in only **11 (26%)**; 23 (55%) had a stoma at the time of episioproctotomy, but **fecal diversion was not significantly associated with outcomes**.
  - 8 patients with obstetrical fistulas: fistula healing in **all 8**, **none** reporting fecal incontinence, follow-up 6 months to 8 years.
  - 50 patients with obstetrical or cryptoglandular RVFs: fistula healing in **39 (78%)**, "rare" postoperative fecal incontinence in 46 (92%), median follow-up 49 months. Of 36 patients (72%) with a stoma before episioproctotomy, most underwent closure within **3 months** of the fistula repair (median 3.4 mo). Of the 25 with preoperative fecal incontinence, only **4 (8%)** experienced postoperative fecal incontinence.

### Tissue Interposition Flaps

**A gracilis muscle or bulbocavernosus (Martius) flap is typically recommended for recurrent or otherwise complex rectovaginal fistula** ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]] rec 16, *strong recommendation, low-quality evidence, 1C*).

| Flap | Outcome |
|---|---|
| **Gracilis muscle** | Fistula healing **19 of 24 (79%)** in the largest series; other retrospective studies report **50–92%**. Postoperative complication rates **28–47%** — most commonly surgical site infection, thigh numbness, hematoma. Quality of life improves (36-Item Short-Form Survey scores and sexual function) |
| **Martius (bulbocavernosus)** | 24 patients with low RVF: overall success **91%** at mean follow-up 42 months; 42% had fistulas of obstetrical origin. 20 consecutive patients (70% with fecal diversion): minor complications in 3 (15%), healing in 13 (65%) at mean 35 months. 14 patients (6 with Crohn's disease), diverting ostomy in all: healing in 13 (**93%**). In radiation-related RVFs: healing in 11 of 12 and 13 of 14 |

- **Diverting ostomy is generally recommended as an adjunct** to Martius and gracilis muscle flap repairs — fecal diversion has been reported in **63–100%** of these patients.
  - Contrary data: in 176 patients with RVFs treated at multiple centers (including 9 Martius flaps), an **81%** fistula healing rate was reported with a variety of repairs and a nonspecified "low rate" of stoma formation, mainly attributed to repairs being performed by urogynecologists. In 16 women treated with Martius flaps using **selective** fecal diversion in 38%, overall success was **94%** at mean 1.5 years. A small series of patients with RVF from Crohn's disease who underwent Martius flaps **without** a covering stoma reported **100%** success at mean 18 months.

### Transabdominal Repair

**Rectovaginal fistulas that result from colorectal anastomotic complications often require a transabdominal approach for repair** ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]] rec 17, *strong recommendation, low-quality evidence, 1C*).

- **Fecal diversion is generally recommended as the initial step** to facilitate resolution of the acute inflammation and associated symptoms; in selected cases with an **immediate or early postoperative** RVF, reoperation and repeat colorectal anastomosis may be preferable.
- **Fistula healing with diversion alone** has been reported — 6 of 16 (**37%**) with an RVF from a failed colorectal anastomosis healed with diversion alone; those with persistent fistulas were treated with **repeat colorectal anastomosis, endorectal advancement flap, or a transperineal interposition flap**.

### Completion Proctectomy

**Completion proctectomy with or without colonic pull-through or coloanal anastomosis may be required to treat radiation-related or recurrent complex rectovaginal fistula** ([[ascrs-2022-anorectal-abscess-fistula]] rec 18, *weak recommendation, low-quality evidence, 2C*).

- Recurrent complex RVFs and fistulas developing after **pelvic radiation** may be amenable to repair with a **muscle flap interposition** (above) or proctectomy with primary or staged coloanal anastomosis.
- **Sleeve excision technique** — a variation of conventional proctectomy: resection of the rectum proximal to the fistula tract, mucosectomy of the rectum at and distal to the fistula, pull-through of healthy colon through the remaining muscular tube of the rectum, and creation of a sutured coloanal anastomosis. In women with RVF secondary to pelvic radiotherapy for cervical cancer, healing in **11 of 14 (79%)** with "good" functional results in all who healed. In a more recent study of 34 patients: early postoperative complications **51%**, late complications **32%**, fistula healing **75%**, and **18 of 25 (72%)** surveyed reported normal postoperative fecal continence.
- **Turnbull-Cutait (staged) coloanal anastomosis** may be used to restore continuity. In a retrospective comparison of 67 patients operated for a variety of indications (only 3 with RVF), the Turnbull-Cutait procedure had decreased rates of anastomotic leak (**3% vs 7%**) and pelvic abscess (**0% vs 5%**) versus primary coloanal anastomosis, with similar functional outcomes.

### Fecal Diversion

- Many patients with RVF report **symptomatic relief after a diverting stoma that leaves the affected rectum in situ**. In 10 patients who underwent proctectomy and diverting stoma compared with 16 who had stoma alone for radiation-related RVF, the proctectomy group had **significantly less tenesmus and anal discharge at 6 and 12 months**.
- In 79 patients with RVF (43% secondary to Crohn's disease), 19 underwent resection with primary (n = 8) or delayed (n = 11) coloanal anastomosis — overall success **91%**.
- In 10 patients with RVF secondary to pelvic radiation who underwent pull-through and straight coloanal anastomosis with diverting loop ileostomy, **all healed without an anastomotic leak**, and fecal incontinence quality-of-life index, depression, lifestyle and embarrassment scores improved after surgery with **no significant changes in continence**.
