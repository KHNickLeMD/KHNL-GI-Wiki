## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- **Definition:** centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome (CAPS) = continuous severe abdominal pain with loss of daily functioning, not explained by any structural or metabolic disorder; believed to have a **dominant central component** not explained by peripheral sensitization alone ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]). Previously called *chronic idiopathic abdominal pain* and *functional abdominal pain syndrome*.
- **Rome V placement:** a disorder of gut–brain interaction (DGBI) — **D1** among the centrally mediated disorders of gastrointestinal (GI) pain, alongside [[abdominal-migraine]] (D2) and [[narcotic-bowel-syndrome]] (D3) ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]).

**Rome V D1 criteria — must include all of the following:**

1. **Continuous abdominal pain**
2. Relationship of pain with physiological events — **category A:** no association, **or category B:** variable association
3. **Pain limits some aspect of daily functioning**
4. Pain is **not factitious**
5. Another GI or non-GI disorder does not explain the continuous abdominal pain

**Timing:** criteria fulfilled for the **last 3 months**, with symptom onset **at least 6 months** before diagnosis.

**In clinical practice — Rome Clinical Criteria** ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]], Table 3): Rome V recommends these over the research criteria above. The qualitative features must be met and symptoms must be bothersome enough to seek care or interfere with daily activity and quality of life; the **6-month duration is not required — 8 weeks is suggested** to exclude other diagnoses (shorter when the clinician is satisfied that evaluation excludes other disorders). Full framework: [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction#Rome Clinical Criteria (New in Rome V)]].

**Operative definitions printed with the criteria:**

| Term | What counts |
|---|---|
| Physiological events | **Eating, defecation, menstruation, positioning**, or other visceral or somatic activities |
| Limitations of daily functioning | **Work, intimacy, social and leisure, family life, caregiving** for self or others |
| "Continuous" | Pain **in the awake state**. Continuity is what distinguishes CAPS from other DGBI such as [[irritable-bowel-syndrome\|irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)]]; Rome V deleted "or nearly continuous" to reduce ambiguity and keep specificity |
| Psychosocial profile | CAPS is typically associated with psychosocial comorbidity, but **no specific profile characterizes the diagnosis** |

- **Pain character:** constant, may wax and wane in severity; colicky, cramping, burning, dull or aching; often diffuse. **Constancy** separates it from IBS, [[functional-dyspepsia|functional dyspepsia]] and functional biliary pain, and **abdominal location** from chronic pelvic pain — although it may overlap with these.
- Patients commonly have other extraintestinal symptoms such as musculoskeletal pain.
- Chronic abdominal pain affects up to approximately **20%** of DGBI patients in some series referred to specialty practices and emergency facilities. Most have severe, nearly constant pain linked to eating or defecation, so the constancy excludes IBS and dyspepsia — the group Rome V widened the definition to capture.

> [!pearl] Category B is the group that used to fall through the cracks
> Half of patients referred for chronic continuous abdominal pain failed Rome IV CAPS criteria only because eating, defecation or menses modified the pain — yet they were otherwise indistinguishable from strict CAPS. Rome V admits them as **category B**, which is five times as common as the traditional diagnosis ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]).

### Severity Assessment

- Rome V gives **no severity score or staging** for CAPS; the criteria themselves require that pain limit daily functioning. Treatment and referral are matched to severity and disability ([[#The Consultation Itself]]).
- Markers of burden to probe:
  - **Suicidal ideation** — chronic abdominal pain may be an independent risk factor, particularly in women; risk rises with **unemployment, poor sleep, a feeling of helplessness, and use of illicit drugs for pain control**.
  - **Opioid exposure** — up to **two-thirds** of patients with CAPS have been or are being treated with opioids.
  - Anxiety is present in up to **40%** of patients with a DGBI; depression and anxiety independently predict pain reporting, especially in women.
  - **Post-traumatic stress disorder** is critical to probe during the interview — a history of abuse predicts poorer health status, greater health care use, greater disability and lower quality of life.

### Classification / Typing

| | **Category A** | **Category B** |
|---|---|---|
| Relationship to physiological events | **No association** | **Variable association** — pain intensity is modified by eating, defecation, menses |
| Pain | Continuous | Continuous (**unlike IBS**) |
| Prevalence | 0.02% | 0.11% |
| Presumed drug response | **Centrally acting neuromodulators** | Central **and** peripheral neuromodulators (antispasmodics, laxatives) |
| Gut-directed drugs (probiotics, antibiotics, secretagogues, bile acid sequestrants, serotonin antagonists) | **Likely ineffective** | **Possible partial effect** |

- The two categories are **similar in virtually all measured variables** and both differ from non-CAPS individuals ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]); the Rome V overview states the **diagnostic and treatment approach for category B is the same as for category A** ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]).

**Where CAPS sits on the DGBI pain spectrum:**

| | IBS end — peripheral dominance | CAPS end — central dominance |
|---|---|---|
| Severity | Mild | Severe |
| Dominant influencing factors | **Peripheral:** food, acute GI infection, mucosal inflammation, abdominal/pelvic surgery, menses | **Central:** life stress, GI-specific anxiety, anxiety disorder, depression, poor coping skills, poor social support, maladaptive cognitions, abuse |
| Mechanism | Gut and brain components; peripheral factors enhance visceral signalling → visceral hypersensitivity | Central sensitization with nociplastic pain features — disinhibited pain signals, reorganized central pathways and networks, neuroinflammation |

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|DGBI]].*

- IBS — favoured when pain is **intermittent** and there is major relief with defecation. Rome V IBS criteria now require that pain and discomfort **not be continuous**, precisely to separate IBS from CAPS ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]).
- Functional dyspepsia or functional biliary pain — favoured when pain is mainly **intermittent and epigastric or right upper quadrant**.
- [[abdominal-migraine]] — paroxysmal and stereotypical with symptom-free weeks to months, not continuous.
- [[narcotic-bowel-syndrome]] — abdominal pain that develops or increases on continuous or increasing opioid doses; CAPS patients on opioids are at risk of developing it.
- **Chronic mesenteric ischemia** — **postprandial pain, fear of eating, weight loss**; an important differential. (Its acute counterpart is [[acute-mesenteric-ischemia]].)
- **Vascular and compressive entrapment syndromes:** median arcuate ligament syndrome, superior mesenteric artery syndrome, nutcracker syndrome, May-Thurner syndrome.
- **Abdominal wall pain from anterior cutaneous nerve entrapment syndrome** — a more frequent cause of chronic abdominal pain than previously expected; abdominal wall pain is separated from visceral pain by Carnett's test ([[#Diagnostics]]).
- Systemic illness involving GI organs: **acute porphyria, diabetes mellitus, malignancy, thoracic disc herniation, spinal cord injury**.
- Gynecological causes including **endometriosis**.
- **Feigned pain or malingering** — intentional production of false or exaggerated symptoms for secondary gain (avoiding work, disability or malpractice compensation, obtaining drugs). Distinct from **factitious disorder**, where external incentives are not the motivation.

> [!pitfall] Test only when a specific condition is genuinely likely
> These alternative conditions should be ruled out by testing **only if there is corroborating evidence that the specific condition has a strong likelihood of causing the symptoms** ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]). Extensive and repeated testing to exclude alternatives should not be undertaken routinely.

**Common comorbidities — "organic" and "functional" (they coexist, not differentials):** fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, chronic back or knee pain, pseudoseizures or chronic regional pain syndrome, hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, quiescent [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]], diverticular disease (see [[diverticulitis]]), endometriosis, gallstones, [[hemorrhoids]], abdominal hernia, [[chronic-pancreatitis]], peripheral neuropathy, IBS, [[cyclic-vomiting-syndrome|cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS)]] or [[gerd|gastroesophageal reflux disease]], depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, chronic pelvic pain syndrome, bladder pain syndrome, pelvic congestion.

## Diagnostics

- **Symptom-based diagnosis plus an alarm-feature screen** — the "red flag" approach that helped validate symptom-based criteria for IBS can be applied to CAPS.
- **Minimal workup: routine laboratory tests to exclude inflammation and signs of GI bleeding** (anemia, fecal blood loss). Alarm features on history, examination or screening labs → investigate for causes other than CAPS.
- **Physical examination is not optional** — there is no substitute for examining the patient to clarify pain location and radiation. Include a **digital rectal examination and visual inspection of the perineum**. Of inspection, auscultation, percussion and palpation, **palpation is the most useful**.

**Examination findings that support CAPS:**

| Sign | What is observed |
|---|---|
| **No dominant autonomic arousal** | No tachycardia, raised blood pressure or diaphoresis during the pain |
| **Unexplained surgical scars** | Multiple scars without clearly understood indications |
| **Closed eyes sign** | On abdominal palpation the patient winces **with eyes closed** |
| **Stethoscope sign** | Palpating the abdomen with a stethoscope **reduces the behavioural response** seen with manual palpation |

**Carnett's test — visceral vs abdominal wall pain:** identify the site of maximal abdominal pain, then ask the patient to tense the abdominal muscles by raising the head and trunk, or the lower extremities, off the examining table. **Identical or increased tenderness = positive = abdominal wall pain**, not visceral.

- Other manoeuvres for regions hard to reach anteriorly: **obturator and psoas signs**; rebound tenderness for peritoneal inflammation; palpation can detect aneurysms.
- **Quantitative sensory testing** can give an understanding of the pain mechanisms.
- **Symptom-related behaviors typify CAPS but have limited diagnostic value** — they also occur in patients with structural disease. Those described are: focusing on complete symptom relief; frequent health care seeking; taking limited personal responsibility for self-management; requesting narcotics; requesting diagnostic studies; variable verbal and non-verbal pain expression; urgent reporting of intense symptoms; minimizing the role of psychological factors.

## Therapeutics

**Nothing in this section is graded:** Rome V gives narrative guidance with no numbered or evidence-rated recommendations, and the American Gastroenterological Association (AGA) 2021 Best Practice Advice statements are ungraded.

### Pharmacotherapy

- **Central neuromodulators are the primary pharmacologic agents** for chronic moderate-to-severe pain, and CAPS in general requires centrally targeted medication — including in category B.
- **Counsel on the time course up front:** adverse effects are usually immediate over the first several days and diminish over a week or so, while **the pain benefit may take several weeks**.

| Line | Agent | Rome V 2026 dose (CAPS) | AGA 2021 dose (painful DGBI) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **First** | Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) | **10–25 mg, building to 75 or 100 mg** depending on agent | Start 10 mg at night; titrate by 10 mg/wk or 10 mg/fortnight to a **maximum of 30–50 mg at night** | Most extensively studied. **Secondary amines (desipramine, nortriptyline) favoured** over tertiary amines (amitriptyline, imipramine) — fewer anticholinergic and antihistaminic effects; may be used at higher dosages for pain |
| **First** | Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) — duloxetine | **30–60 mg/d or 90 mg/d** | Start 30 mg once daily; **maximum 60 mg once daily** | Fewer adverse effects than TCAs; usable in patients **with constipation**. Nausea in **15%–20%**, minimised by taking with meals |
| **First** | SNRI — venlafaxine | **>150 mg/d** | — | Lower doses do not achieve noradrenergic activation |
| **Second (augmenting)** | Quetiapine | **25–100 mg once daily at bedtime** (vs 400–800 mg for psychiatric disorders) | — | Augments the pain benefit of a TCA or SNRI; in 1 study, up to **50% benefit** in painful IBS or CAPS unresponsive to 4 weeks of TCA/SNRI. Also reduces anxiety and enhances sleep |
| **Second (augmenting)** | Olanzapine | **2.5–10 mg once daily at bedtime** — approximately **10% of the quetiapine dose** | — | Metabolic and/or motor adverse effect risk, as with all antipsychotics |
| Additional | Buspirone | **10–20 mg twice daily** | — | Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine [5-HT]) 1A partial agonist; augments antidepressants |
| Additional | Tandospirone | **10–20 mg three times daily** | — | Same class |
| Additional | Mirtazapine | **7.5–30 mg at night** | Start 15 mg once daily; **maximum 45 mg once daily** | Antihistaminergic at low dose; noradrenergic and 5-HT1A serotonergic at higher dose. **Weight gain is frequent**; sedating |
| Additional | Gabapentin, pregabalin, mirogabalin | No dose given | — | α2δ ligands; may dampen dorsal horn signalling |
| Ancillary | Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) | — | — | **Typically not helpful for CAPS pain**, but can improve anxiety-related symptoms to augment a low-dose TCA or SNRI, and can help constipation |

- **Two dose sources disagree.** AGA 2021 sets lower TCA and duloxetine ceilings and a higher mirtazapine range for painful DGBI in general ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]], Table 2); Rome V 2026 gives the CAPS-specific doses ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]). AGA's start-low titration table and side-effect profiles: [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction#Gut–Brain Neuromodulators]].
- **Augmentation** = add a second centrally acting agent, a peripherally acting agent, or a brain–gut behavioural treatment, then the antidepressant dose may be lowered. Know each agent's adverse-effect profile and watch for **serotonin syndrome**.
- **Relapse prevention:** continuing a neuromodulator **beyond achieving clinical benefit** reduces relapse or recurrence, as does continuing or starting a brain–gut therapy.

> [!pitfall] Do not reach for analgesics or opioids
> Acetaminophen, aspirin and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs **offer little benefit** in chronic pain conditions. **Narcotic analgesics must be avoided in CAPS** — the risks are addiction and the development of [[narcotic-bowel-syndrome]] ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]).

- **Already on opioids at referral:** prescribe responsibly via multidisciplinary collaboration — monitoring efficacy, side effects and abuse potential — until other pain management is in place and they can be discontinued; tramadol counts as an opioid ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]], Best Practice Advice 3).

### Brain–Gut Behavioral Therapies

Candidacy rules, referral pathway and modality detail live on [[brain-gut-behavioral-therapy]]. AGA 2021 (Best Practice Advice 4) says to consider them routinely and raise them early in care, across painful DGBI including CAPS. What Rome V adds for CAPS:

- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** — well supported in refractory DGBI (most robust data in IBS and several chronic pain conditions); two components: **psychoeducation** (how cognitive-affective processes and behaviors amplify pain) then **skills training** (relaxation to modify arousal and muscle tension, attentional shifting, distraction). Targets pain catastrophizing and unhelpful worry. **Digital delivery is showing promise** for access.
- **Psychodynamic interpersonal therapy** — focuses on emotional state rather than thought process, and on how prior experience shapes coping; **particularly helpful in patients reporting a history of sexual abuse**.
- **Gut-focused hypnotherapy** — accumulating evidence of effect in functional abdominal pain or IBS; postulated mechanisms: relaxation, induced somatosensory experiences, a sense of control over the gut, and altered perceptual experience of symptoms via guided imagery and post-hypnotic suggestion.
- **Mindfulness-based stress reduction** and acceptance-based approaches — greater acceptance of pain is associated with lower pain intensity, less pain-related anxiety and avoidance, less disability and better work status.

### Neuromodulation and Procedures

- **Neurolytic celiac plexus blockade should be abandoned in benign diseases.**
- **Spinal cord stimulation** may have a role after failed medical therapy, but **a specific clinical recommendation cannot be made**.
- **Transcranial direct current stimulation** (1–2 mA via scalp electrodes in a bipolar montage) can be tried — inexpensive, no major adverse effects.
- **Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation** — few reports, uncontrolled results indeterminate. Transcutaneous (auricular or cervical) vagal nerve stimulation: effects on motility, nausea and pain in experimental studies; percutaneous electrical nerve field stimulation: effect on abdominal pain in one study.
- **Acupuncture** is well documented in better-defined diseases such as [[acute-pancreatitis|acute]] and chronic pancreatitis.
- **Deep diaphragmatic breathing** can be used partly as a vagal activator and may benefit selected patients.

### The Consultation Itself

- Treatment principles: base treatment on **symptom severity and degree of disability**; know when to refer to a **mental health professional**; know when to refer to a **multidisciplinary DGBI or pain treatment centre**; be aware of sociocultural bias; **recommend face-to-face rather than virtual appointments**.
- Eleven physician behaviours are named: listen actively · accept CAPS as a true disorder · offer empathy · use a questioning style and non-verbal messages · validate · set realistic treatment goals · educate · reassure · negotiate · maintain boundaries · be aware of time constraints.
- Patient-side requirements: **realistic expectations** about managing a chronic disorder, readiness to enter a therapeutic relationship, and **readiness to share responsibility for care** — shared responsibility improves clinical outcomes.
- **Close follow-up is central to reducing unnecessary health care utilization.**
- No response to these measures → a **pain management specialist** may be required ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]).
