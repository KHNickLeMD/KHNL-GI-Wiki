## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- **Definition:** continuous severe abdominal pain with loss of daily functioning, not explained by structural or metabolic disease, in which **central mechanisms predominate** over peripheral sensitization ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]). Previously called *chronic idiopathic abdominal pain* and *functional abdominal pain syndrome*.
- **Rome V placement:** disorder **D1** among the centrally mediated disorders of gastrointestinal (GI) pain, alongside [[abdominal-migraine]] (D2) and [[narcotic-bowel-syndrome]] (D3).

**Rome V D1 criteria — must include all of the following:**

1. **Continuous abdominal pain**
2. Relationship of pain with physiological events — **category A:** no association, **or category B:** variable association
3. **Pain limits some aspect of daily functioning**
4. Pain is **not factitious**
5. Another GI or non-GI disorder does not explain the continuous abdominal pain

**Timing:** criteria fulfilled for the **last 3 months**, with symptom onset **at least 6 months** before diagnosis.

**Operative definitions printed with the criteria:**

| Term | What counts |
|---|---|
| Physiological events | **Eating, defecation, menstruation, positioning**, or other visceral or somatic activities |
| Limitations of daily functioning | **Work, intimacy, social and leisure, family life, caregiving** for self or others |
| "Continuous" | Pain **in the awake state** — Rome V deleted "or nearly continuous" to remove ambiguity and preserve specificity against [[irritable-bowel-syndrome\|irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)]] |
| Psychosocial profile | CAPS is typically associated with psychosocial comorbidity, but **no specific profile characterizes the diagnosis** |

- **Pain character:** constant, may wax and wane in severity; colicky, cramping, burning, dull or aching; often diffuse. The **constancy** is what separates it from IBS, [[dyspepsia|functional dyspepsia]] and functional biliary pain — although it may overlap with all three.
- Patients commonly have other extraintestinal symptoms such as musculoskeletal pain.
- **Prevalence:** **0.02%** worldwide for category A and **0.11%** for category B — but up to approximately **20%** of DGBI patients in some specialty series have chronic abdominal pain, which is why Rome V widened the definition.

> [!pearl] Category B is the group that used to fall through the cracks
> Half of patients referred for chronic continuous abdominal pain failed Rome IV CAPS criteria only because eating, defecation or menses modified the pain — yet they were otherwise indistinguishable from strict CAPS. Rome V admits them as **category B**, which is five times as common as the traditional diagnosis ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]).

### Severity Assessment

- **No severity score or staging system exists for CAPS.** The diagnosis itself requires that pain limit daily functioning.
- Treatment is nonetheless **based on symptom severity and the degree of disability** — that judgement also sets when to refer to a mental health professional or to a multidisciplinary DGBI or pain centre.
- Markers of burden that should raise concern:
  - **Suicidal ideation** — chronic abdominal pain may be an independent risk factor, particularly in women; risk rises with **unemployment, poor sleep, a feeling of helplessness, and use of illicit drugs for pain control**.
  - **Opioid exposure** — up to **two-thirds** of patients with CAPS have been or are being treated with opioids.
  - Anxiety is present in up to **40%** of patients with a DGBI; depression and anxiety independently predict pain reporting, especially in women.
  - **Post-traumatic stress disorder** is critical to probe during the interview — a history of abuse predicts poorer health status, greater health care use, greater disability and lower quality of life.

### Classification / Typing

| | **Category A** | **Category B** |
|---|---|---|
| Relationship to physiological events | **No association** | **Variable association** — pain intensity is modified by eating, defecation, menses |
| Pain | Continuous | Continuous (**unlike IBS**) |
| Prevalence | 0.02% | 0.11% |
| Expected drug response | **Centrally acting neuromodulators** | Central **and** peripheral neuromodulators (antispasmodics, laxatives) |
| Gut-directed drugs (probiotics, antibiotics, secretagogues, bile acid sequestrants, serotonin antagonists) | **Likely ineffective** | **Possible partial effect** |

- The two categories are **similar in virtually all measured variables** and both differ from non-CAPS individuals — the split exists to capture patients, not to separate prognoses.

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI)]].*

- [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|IBS]] — favoured when pain is **intermittent** and there is major relief with defecation.
- [[dyspepsia|Functional dyspepsia]] or functional biliary pain — favoured when pain is mainly **intermittent and epigastric or right upper quadrant**.
- [[abdominal-migraine]] — paroxysmal and stereotypical with symptom-free weeks to months, not continuous.
- [[narcotic-bowel-syndrome]] — pain escalating with the opioid dose; CAPS patients on opioids are at risk of developing it.
- **Chronic mesenteric ischemia** — **postprandial pain, fear of eating, weight loss**; the chapter names it as an important differential. (Its acute counterpart is [[acute-mesenteric-ischemia]].)
- **Vascular and compressive entrapment syndromes:** median arcuate ligament syndrome, superior mesenteric artery syndrome, nutcracker syndrome, May-Thurner syndrome.
- **Abdominal wall pain from anterior cutaneous nerve entrapment syndrome** — a more frequent cause of chronic abdominal pain than previously expected; identified by Carnett's test.
- Systemic illness involving GI organs: **acute porphyria, diabetes mellitus, malignancy, thoracic disc herniation, spinal cord injury**.
- Gynecological causes including **endometriosis**.
- **Feigned pain or malingering** — intentional production of false or exaggerated symptoms for secondary gain (avoiding work, disability or malpractice compensation, obtaining drugs). Distinct from **factitious disorder**, where external incentives are not the motivation.

> [!pitfall] Test only when a specific condition is genuinely likely
> These alternative conditions should be ruled out by testing **only if there is corroborating evidence that the specific condition has a strong likelihood of causing the symptoms** ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]). Extensive and repeated testing to exclude alternatives should not be undertaken routinely.

**Common comorbidities (not differentials — they coexist):** fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, chronic back or knee pain, hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, quiescent [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]], diverticular disease (see [[diverticulitis]]), endometriosis, gallstones, [[hemorrhoids]], abdominal hernia, [[chronic-pancreatitis]], peripheral neuropathy, IBS, [[cyclic-vomiting-syndrome|cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS)]], [[gerd|gastroesophageal reflux disease]], chronic pelvic pain syndrome, bladder pain syndrome.

## Diagnostics

- **CAPS is a positive clinical diagnosis.** No confirmatory test, laboratory marker or imaging finding exists.
- **Minimal workup: routine laboratory tests to exclude inflammation and signs of GI bleeding** (anemia, fecal blood loss). Alarm features on history, examination or screening labs → investigate for causes other than CAPS.
- **Physical examination is not optional** — there is no substitute for examining the patient to clarify pain location and radiation. Include a **digital rectal examination and visual inspection of the perineum**. Of inspection, auscultation, percussion and palpation, **palpation is the most useful**.

**Examination findings that support CAPS:**

| Sign | What is observed |
|---|---|
| **No dominant autonomic arousal** | No tachycardia, raised blood pressure or diaphoresis during the pain |
| **Unexplained surgical scars** | Multiple scars without clearly understood indications |
| **Closed eyes sign** | On abdominal palpation the patient winces **with eyes closed** |
| **Stethoscope sign** | Palpating the abdomen with a stethoscope **reduces the behavioural response** seen with manual palpation |

**Carnett's test — visceral vs abdominal wall pain:** identify the site of maximal abdominal pain, then ask the patient to tense the abdominal muscles by raising the head and trunk, or the lower extremities, off the examining table. **Identical or increased tenderness = positive = abdominal wall pain**, not visceral.

- Other manoeuvres for regions hard to reach anteriorly: **obturator and psoas signs**; rebound tenderness for peritoneal inflammation; palpation can detect aneurysms.
- **Quantitative sensory testing** can characterise the pain mechanism, and the chapter maps the available human assessment methods onto each level of the pain pathway (conditioned pain modulation, the RIII nociceptive reflex, electroencephalography, magnetoencephalography, functional magnetic resonance imaging, positron emission tomography). None of these is a diagnostic test for CAPS.
- **Symptom-related behaviors typify CAPS but have limited diagnostic value** — they also occur in patients with structural disease. Those described are: focusing on complete symptom relief; frequent health care seeking; taking limited personal responsibility for self-management; requesting narcotics; requesting diagnostic studies; variable verbal and non-verbal pain expression; urgent reporting of intense symptoms; minimizing the role of psychological factors.

## Therapeutics

**Nothing in this section is graded.** The Rome V chapter gives narrative treatment guidance with no numbered recommendations and no evidence ratings.

### Pharmacotherapy

- **Central neuromodulators are the primary pharmacologic agents** for chronic moderate-to-severe pain, and CAPS in general requires centrally targeted medication — including in category B.
- **Counsel on the time course up front:** adverse effects are usually immediate over the first several days and diminish over a week or so, while **the pain benefit may take several weeks**.

| Line | Agent | Dose as printed | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First** | Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) | **10–25 mg, building to 75 or 100 mg** depending on agent | Most extensively studied. **Secondary amines (desipramine, nortriptyline) favoured** over tertiary amines (amitriptyline, imipramine) — fewer anticholinergic and antihistaminic effects; may be used at higher dosages for pain |
| **First** | Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) — duloxetine | **30–60 mg/d or 90 mg/d** | Fewer adverse effects than TCAs; usable **when constipation is a problem**. Nausea in **15%–20%**, minimised by taking with meals |
| **First** | SNRI — venlafaxine | **>150 mg/d** | Lower doses do not achieve noradrenergic activation |
| **Second (augmenting)** | Quetiapine | **25–100 mg once daily at bedtime** (vs 400–800 mg for psychiatric disorders) | Augments the pain benefit of a TCA or SNRI; up to **50% benefit** in patients with painful IBS or CAPS unresponsive to 4 weeks of TCA/SNRI. Also reduces anxiety and improves sleep |
| **Second (augmenting)** | Olanzapine | **2.5–10 mg once daily at bedtime** — approximately **10% of the quetiapine dose** | Metabolic and/or motor adverse effect risk, as with all antipsychotics |
| Adjunct | Buspirone | **10–20 mg twice daily** | 5-HT1A partial agonist, augments antidepressant effect |
| Adjunct | Tandospirone | **10–20 mg three times daily** | Same class |
| Adjunct | Mirtazapine | **7.5–30 mg at night** | Antihistaminergic at low dose, noradrenergic and 5-HT1A serotonergic at higher dose. **Weight gain is frequent**; sedating |
| Adjunct | Gabapentin, pregabalin, mirogabalin | No dose given | α2δ ligands; may dampen dorsal horn signalling |
| Ancillary | Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) | — | **Typically not helpful for CAPS pain**, but can improve anxiety-related symptoms to augment a low-dose TCA or SNRI, and can help constipation |

- **Augmentation** = add a second centrally acting agent, a peripherally acting agent, or a brain–gut behavioural treatment, then the antidepressant dose may be lowered. Know each agent's adverse-effect profile and watch for **serotonin syndrome**.
- **Relapse prevention:** continuing a neuromodulator **beyond achieving clinical benefit** reduces relapse or recurrence, as does continuing or starting a brain–gut therapy.

> [!pitfall] Do not reach for analgesics or opioids
> Acetaminophen, aspirin and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs **offer little benefit** in chronic pain of this kind. **Narcotic analgesics must be avoided in CAPS** — the risks are addiction and the development of [[narcotic-bowel-syndrome]] ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]).

**Contradiction to be aware of — the TCA ceiling.** [[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]] caps TCAs at **30–50 mg at night** across painful DGBI; Rome V 2026 gives **10–25 mg building to 75 or 100 mg** for CAPS specifically. Both are society-level documents; the **newer Rome V figures are what this page states** for CAPS, while the general dose table on [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]] remains AGA's.

### Brain–Gut Behavioral Therapies

Candidacy rules, referral pathway and modality detail live on [[brain-gut-behavioral-therapy]]. What this chapter adds for CAPS:

- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** — best supported; two components: **psychoeducation** (how cognitive-affective processes and behaviors amplify pain) then **skills training** (relaxation to modify arousal and muscle tension, attentional shifting, distraction). Targets pain catastrophizing and unhelpful worry. **Digital delivery is showing promise** for access.
- **Psychodynamic interpersonal therapy** — focuses on emotional state rather than thought process, and on how prior experience shapes coping; **particularly helpful in patients reporting a history of sexual abuse**.
- **Gut-focused hypnotherapy** — relaxation, induced somatosensory experiences, a sense of control over the gut, and altered perceptual experience of symptoms via guided imagery and post-hypnotic suggestion.
- **Mindfulness-based stress reduction** and acceptance-based approaches — greater acceptance of pain is associated with lower pain intensity, less pain-related anxiety and avoidance, less disability and better work status.

### Neuromodulation and Procedures

- **Neurolytic celiac plexus blockade should be abandoned in benign diseases.**
- **Spinal cord stimulation** may have a role after failed medical therapy, but **a specific clinical recommendation cannot be made**.
- **Transcranial direct current stimulation** (1–2 mA via scalp electrodes in a bipolar montage) can be tried — inexpensive, no major adverse effects.
- **Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation** — few reports, uncontrolled results indeterminate. Transcutaneous (auricular or cervical) vagal nerve stimulation and percutaneous electrical nerve field stimulation have experimental support only.
- **Acupuncture** is best documented in better-defined diseases such as acute and [[chronic-pancreatitis]].
- **Deep diaphragmatic breathing** can be used partly as a vagal activator and may benefit selected patients.

### The Consultation Itself

- Treatment principles: base treatment on **symptom severity and degree of disability**; know when to refer to a **mental health professional**; know when to refer to a **multidisciplinary DGBI or pain treatment centre**; be aware of sociocultural bias; **recommend face-to-face rather than virtual appointments**.
- Eleven physician behaviours are named: listen actively · accept CAPS as a true disorder · offer empathy · use a questioning style and non-verbal messages · validate · set realistic treatment goals · educate · reassure · negotiate · maintain boundaries · be aware of time constraints.
- Patient-side requirements: **realistic expectations** about managing a chronic disorder, readiness to enter a therapeutic relationship, and **readiness to share responsibility for care** — shared responsibility improves clinical outcomes.
- **Close follow-up is central to reducing unnecessary health care utilization** — the alternative is repeat emergency presentations and repeat testing.
