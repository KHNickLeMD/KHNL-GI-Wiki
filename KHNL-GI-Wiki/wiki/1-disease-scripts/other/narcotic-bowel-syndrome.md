## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- **Definition:** chronic or frequently recurring **paradoxic increases in abdominal pain despite continued or escalating dosages of opioids** ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]).
- **Rome V name and placement:** *narcotic bowel syndrome / opioid-induced gastrointestinal (GI) hyperalgesia*, disorder **D3** among the centrally mediated disorders of GI pain, alongside [[centrally-mediated-abdominal-pain-syndrome|centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome (CAPS)]] (D1) and [[abdominal-migraine]] (D2) ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]).

**Rome V D3 criteria — must include all of the following** ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]):

1. **Chronic or frequently recurring abdominal pain that is treated with narcotics**
2. The nature and intensity of the pain are **not explained by a current or previous GI diagnosis**
3. **Two or more** of:
   - a. The pain **worsens or incompletely resolves with continued or escalating dosages** of narcotics
   - b. **Marked worsening of pain when the narcotic dose wanes and improvement when narcotics are reinstituted** — "soar and crash"
   - c. **Progression of the frequency, duration, and intensity** of pain episodes

**Timing:** criteria fulfilled for the **last 3 months**, with symptom onset **at least 6 months** before diagnosis. Printed footnotes: **pain must occur most days**; a structural diagnosis may coexist (e.g. [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|IBD]], [[chronic-pancreatitis]]) provided **the character or activity of that disease is not sufficient to explain the pain**.

- The Rome V criteria set **no opioid dose or exposure-duration threshold** — only that the pain is being treated with narcotics. The dose and duration figures below come from the AGA clinical description, not from the criteria.
- **Descriptive criteria** ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]):

| Element | What is required |
|---|---|
| Pain severity | Moderate to severe |
| Pain pattern | Persistent, daily |
| Duration | More than 3 months |
| Opioid requirement | More than 100 mg morphine equivalent per day |
| Response to dose escalation | Pain does not respond, and may actually increase, in response to escalating doses |

- **Presentation that suggests it:** patient on chronic opiates with **chronic generalized, colicky abdominal pain** despite escalating doses, plus **worsening of pain when the dose is tapered**; the pain may be associated with nausea and vomiting ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]).
- Occurs in approximately **6%** of patients on long-term opioids among those seen with chronic GI conditions, including disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI), and is **often under-recognized** ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]). A 2009 population-based study estimated the prevalence at 4% ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]); Rome V cites **4%, 3% and 6%** from three studies of people prescribed regular opioids, against a community prevalence of **0.17%**, and does not reconcile them.
- **Female preponderance; incidence peaks in the fifth decade.** Associated with comorbid chronic pain syndrome and higher educational attainment ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]).
- **Which opioid matters.** Opioid-induced hyperalgesia is reported most often with the **fast-acting opioids — fentanyl, remifentanil, sufentanil** — though it also follows morphine and oxycodone; this has not been confirmed specifically for narcotic bowel syndrome. **Methadone may prevent opioid-induced hyperalgesia** (through an agonist effect at the N-methyl-D-aspartate [NMDA] receptor) and has been used in treatment; **buprenorphine may be antihyperalgesic** and may be less likely to produce the syndrome, with the patch formulation an advantage where GI function is disturbed.
- **Tramadol counts as an opioid** — it carries the potential for addiction and other opioid-associated adverse events.
- Patients with overlapping [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]] and a DGBI are more likely to be using opioids than those without a DGBI, as are patients with a DGBI compared with those with structural diagnoses.
- **Risk is psychosocial as well as pharmacologic:** psychosocial factors **coupled with opiate use** increase the risk ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]).
- **Why the pain escalates** — five postulated mechanisms, whose interaction is unclear ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]):
  - Descending inhibition of pain signals from the medulla becomes a *facilitator* of those signals through neuroplastic change.
  - Chronic narcotic use inflames spinal glial cells via toll-like receptor activation → increased neuropathic pain.
  - Abnormal N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor function at the spinal cord.
  - Opioid activation of G-protein coupled receptors may excite dorsal root ganglia → increased pain signals.
  - Abnormal central processing of pain (psychological traits and traumatic exposures were prevalent in a 39-patient series).

> [!pitfall] Rising pain on a rising dose
> Continued opioid treatment leads to clinical worsening and repeated medical evaluations ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]), and pain that worsens as the dose is tapered is part of the syndrome rather than evidence the opioid is needed ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]).

### Severity Assessment

- No severity score or staging system is given for this diagnosis. The definition itself requires **moderate to severe** daily pain ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]); the clinically graded consequence recorded is **significant impairment in quality of life (QoL)** ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]).

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|disorders of gut–brain interaction]] for the painful-DGBI frame this overlaps.*

- [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)]] — symptom overlap is the reason the syndrome is hard to diagnose.
- [[centrally-mediated-abdominal-pain-syndrome|CAPS]] — same overlap; IBS itself **can progress into severely disabling CAPS** ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]). CAPS is also a common reason the opioid was started in the first place.
- **Opioid use disorder** — a subset of patients reporting abdominal pain while on opioids have a substance use disorder instead. **Refer them to a substance abuse program; do not treat them for narcotic bowel syndrome** ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]).
- Narcotic bowel syndrome may **co-exist with, and complicate the management of**, painful DGBI rather than replace those diagnoses.
- [[opioid-induced-constipation]] — the other opioid-related bowel disorder. Narcotic bowel syndrome is a **disorder of nociception, independent of the opioid's effects on gut motility and secretion** ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]); it is defined by pain, not by stool frequency or consistency.
- Structural causes of abdominal pain in a patient on chronic opioids — **kidney stones, bowel obstruction** — are what emergency evaluation of these patients looks for ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]).

## Diagnostics

- Diagnosis is **clinical** — the descriptive criteria above plus the pain trajectory read against the opioid dose. Neither AGA update gives a confirmatory test, laboratory marker, or imaging finding.
- **An extensive negative evaluation usually ensues**; emergency evaluation is commonly sought both for pain control and to look for the structural causes above ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]).
- **What the tests show:** laboratory studies are typically normal; **radiologic studies may show colonic fecal retention and/or signs of "ileus" or "incomplete bowel obstruction."** Cross-sectional imaging is often performed to exclude obstruction, pancreatitis, and inflammatory or ischemic bowel. **These negative results, a focused history and examination, and satisfaction of the criteria are adequate to make the diagnosis** ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]).
- It is a **positive diagnosis**: it can occur alone (pain developing after an operation or during treatment for back pain) or **alongside structural GI disease, when the pain is out of proportion to what that disease would explain**.
- **Current Opioid Misuse Measure** — used to gauge the severity of opioid misuse and the likelihood of successful detoxification. A **low score** (few aberrant drug-related behaviours) predicted **responder status and the ability to remain off opioids** at follow-up.
- Psychosocial disturbance is typical (anxiety, depression, somatization, post-traumatic stress disorder, personality disorders) and is useful for treatment planning, but is **not part of the diagnostic criteria**.
- Practical approach: review the opioid history alongside the pain trajectory in any patient with painful DGBI whose pain worsens while the opioid dose rises.

## Therapeutics

- **Primary treatment: cessation of opioids**, if possible ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]). Best handled through specialists and/or centers with expertise in opiate dependence ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]).
- **Behavioral and psychiatric approaches are needed for long-term management and reduced recidivism** — stopping the opioid alone does not hold.

### Detoxification Protocol

The only published protocol, as Rome V prints it ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]). **Ungraded** — this chapter carries no numbered recommendations and no evidence ratings.

| Step | What it specifies |
|---|---|
| **Set up before starting** | Effective communication with the patient about pain management · **add a tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) or serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) *before* detoxification** · add augmenting agents as needed · psychological treatment for psychosocial support and pain management |
| **Convert and taper** | Convert the **total daily dosage of all opioids to a morphine equivalent**; give that dose as a **continuous intravenous drip on day 1**, then reduce by a **fixed, predetermined proportion — 10%–33% — each day until off narcotics (usually 4–11 days)** |
| **Clonidine for withdrawal** | **Start at the point of 50% opioid withdrawal · 0.1 mg twice or three times daily · titrate up to 0.6 mg/d as needed · taper off at the end of detoxification** |
| **Hold the line on dose** | By prior agreement, **once detoxification starts the narcotic dosing does not change** — augmenting agents are used instead |
| **Constipation** | Polyethylene glycol, a chloride channel activator, or a **peripherally acting µ-opioid receptor antagonist** ([[pamoras\|PAMORA]]) as needed — see [[opioid-induced-constipation]] |
| **Anxiety** | Benzodiazepine intravenously or orally as needed on day 1, adjusted as needed; **an atypical antipsychotic — quetiapine or aripiprazole — may be substituted** |
| **Symptom cover** | **Mirtazapine for nausea · quetiapine for sleep and pain** |
| **Psychological support** | Mental health provider available **during** detoxification; augment with hypnosis, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) or medication |

**What to expect — the one outcome study (39 inpatients, detoxification lasting approximately 7 days):**

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Successfully detoxified | **89.7%** |
| "Responders" by predefined criteria | **60%** |
| Reported **worse** pain after detoxification | **11%** |
| Had restarted opioids at **3 months** | **43.2%** (more likely to have high Current Opioid Misuse Measure scores) |
| Had restarted by end of follow-up | **more than two-thirds** |

- Patients who **remained off opioids at 3 months reported significantly lower abdominal pain scores** than those who resumed — opioid withdrawal relieves the syndrome, and the pain falls further the longer the patient stays off.
- **Continue neuromodulators after detoxification** to raise the central pain threshold, and treat comorbid anxiety and depression concurrently. Doses are on [[centrally-mediated-abdominal-pain-syndrome]].
- **An integrated model of care is needed** — medicine/gastroenterology plus psychology/psychiatry delivering CBT, hypnosis or other brain–gut therapies. A combination of **detoxification, psychotherapy and medical management** gave superior pain tolerance in opioid-induced hyperalgesia.
- **Low-dose ketamine**, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is an effective opioid substitute in chronic pain with positive effects in opioid-induced hyperalgesia — but **has not been systematically studied in narcotic bowel syndrome**.
- The clinician may refer to a **pain management program** for further treatment including detoxification.
- Build an **open, collaborative patient–provider relationship** and use **patient-friendly language about the pathogenesis** of the disorder; this is what gains the patient's acceptance of the diagnosis and collaboration in management.
- If a patient is referred already on opioids, prescribe responsibly in a **multidisciplinary setting with monitoring for efficacy, side effects, and potential for abuse**, until other forms of pain management can be implemented.
- **No response to these measures → involve a pain management specialist** ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]).
- Prevention is the wider rule: opioids **should not be prescribed for chronic GI pain caused by a DGBI** at all.
- Pain management for the underlying painful DGBI — brain–gut psychotherapies and gut–brain neuromodulators — is on [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]]; the therapies themselves, their candidacy rules and the referral pathway are on [[brain-gut-behavioral-therapy]]; pain in IBD specifically is on [[ibd-pain-management]].

> [!pearl] Detoxification without the behavioral work relapses
> Recidivism after detoxification is approximately **50%**, but patients who remain off narcotics report improvement in pain ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]) — which is why behavioral and psychiatric treatment, not cessation alone, is the long-term plan ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]).
