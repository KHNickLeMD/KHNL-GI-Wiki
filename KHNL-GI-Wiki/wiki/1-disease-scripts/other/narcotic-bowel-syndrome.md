## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- **Definition:** chronic or frequently recurring **paradoxic increases in abdominal pain despite continued or escalating dosages of opioids** ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]).
- **Rome V name and placement:** *narcotic bowel syndrome / opioid-induced gastrointestinal (GI) hyperalgesia*, disorder **D3** among the centrally mediated disorders of GI pain ([[rome-v-2026-dgbi]]). The Rome V overview article does not print the D3 symptom items.
- **Descriptive criteria** ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]):

| Element | What is required |
|---|---|
| Pain severity | Moderate to severe |
| Pain pattern | Persistent, daily |
| Duration | More than 3 months |
| Opioid requirement | More than 100 mg morphine equivalent per day |
| Response to dose escalation | Pain does not respond, and may actually increase, in response to escalating doses |

- **Presentation that suggests it:** patient on chronic opiates with **chronic generalized, colicky abdominal pain** despite escalating doses, plus **worsening of pain when the dose is tapered**; the pain may be associated with nausea and vomiting ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]).
- Occurs in approximately **6%** of patients on long-term opioids among those seen with chronic GI conditions, including disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI), and is **often under-recognized** ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]). A 2009 population-based study estimated the prevalence at 4% ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]).
- **Tramadol counts as an opioid** — it carries the potential for addiction and other opioid-associated adverse events.
- Patients with overlapping [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)]] and a DGBI are more likely to be using opioids than those without a DGBI, as are patients with a DGBI compared with those with structural diagnoses.
- **Risk is psychosocial as well as pharmacologic:** psychosocial factors **coupled with opiate use** increase the risk ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]).
- **Why the pain escalates** — five postulated mechanisms, whose interaction is unclear ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]):
  - Descending inhibition of pain signals from the medulla becomes a *facilitator* of those signals through neuroplastic change.
  - Chronic narcotic use inflames spinal glial cells via toll-like receptor activation → increased neuropathic pain.
  - Abnormal N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor function at the spinal cord.
  - Opioid activation of G-protein coupled receptors may excite dorsal root ganglia → increased pain signals.
  - Abnormal central processing of pain (psychological traits and traumatic exposures were prevalent in a 39-patient series).

> [!pitfall] Rising pain on a rising dose
> Continued opioid treatment leads to clinical worsening and repeated medical evaluations ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]), and pain that worsens as the dose is tapered is part of the syndrome rather than evidence the opioid is needed ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]).

### Severity Assessment

- No severity score or staging system is given for this diagnosis. The definition itself requires **moderate to severe** daily pain ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]); the clinically graded consequence recorded is **significant impairment in quality of life (QoL)** ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]).

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|disorders of gut–brain interaction]] for the painful-DGBI frame this overlaps.*

- [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)]] — symptom overlap is the reason the syndrome is hard to diagnose.
- Centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome (CAPS) — same overlap; IBS itself **can progress into severely disabling CAPS** ([[aga-2018-psychogastroenterology]]).
- Narcotic bowel syndrome may **co-exist with, and complicate the management of**, painful DGBI rather than replace those diagnoses.
- [[opioid-induced-constipation]] — the other opioid-related bowel disorder. Narcotic bowel syndrome is a **disorder of nociception, independent of the opioid's effects on gut motility and secretion** ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]); it is defined by pain, not by stool frequency or consistency.
- Structural causes of abdominal pain in a patient on chronic opioids — **kidney stones, bowel obstruction** — are what emergency evaluation of these patients looks for ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]).

## Diagnostics

- Diagnosis is **clinical** — the descriptive criteria above plus the pain trajectory read against the opioid dose. Neither AGA update gives a confirmatory test, laboratory marker, or imaging finding.
- **An extensive negative evaluation usually ensues**; emergency evaluation is commonly sought both for pain control and to look for the structural causes above ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]).
- Practical approach: review the opioid history alongside the pain trajectory in any patient with painful DGBI whose pain worsens while the opioid dose rises.

## Therapeutics

- **Primary treatment: cessation of opioids**, if possible ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]).
- **Detoxification with substitution of non-opiate medications** to control pain, anxiety, and opiate withdrawal symptoms — **including clonidine**. Best handled through specialists and/or centers with expertise in opiate dependence ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]). No taper schedule and no clonidine dose are given.
- **Behavioral and psychiatric approaches are needed for long-term management and reduced recidivism** — stopping the opioid alone does not hold.
- Build an **open, collaborative patient–provider relationship** and use **patient-friendly language about the pathogenesis** of the disorder; this is what gains the patient's acceptance of the diagnosis and collaboration in management.
- If a patient is referred already on opioids, prescribe responsibly in a **multidisciplinary setting with monitoring for efficacy, side effects, and potential for abuse**, until other forms of pain management can be implemented.
- **No response to these measures → involve a pain management specialist** ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]).
- Prevention is the wider rule: opioids **should not be prescribed for chronic GI pain caused by a DGBI** at all.
- Pain management for the underlying painful DGBI — brain–gut psychotherapies and gut–brain neuromodulators — is on [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]]; the therapies themselves, their candidacy rules and the referral pathway are on [[brain-gut-behavioral-therapy]]; pain in IBD specifically is on [[ibd-pain-management]].

> [!pearl] Detoxification without the behavioral work relapses
> Recidivism after detoxification is approximately **50%**, but patients who remain off narcotics report improvement in pain ([[aga-2017-opioids-in-gastroenterology]]) — which is why behavioral and psychiatric treatment, not cessation alone, is the long-term plan ([[aga-2021-chronic-gi-pain-dgbi]]).
