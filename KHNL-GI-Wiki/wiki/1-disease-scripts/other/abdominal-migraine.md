## Assessment

### Establishing the Diagnosis

- **Definition:** stereotypical paroxysmal episodes of intense, recurrent abdominal pain with associated features such as pallor, headache, nausea and vomiting; episodes last **hours to days** and **resolve completely for weeks to months** ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]).
- **Rome V placement:** disorder **D2** among the centrally mediated disorders of gastrointestinal (GI) pain. **New adult diagnosis in Rome V** — first described in adults more than 100 years ago, then carried from Rome II onward as a pediatric entity only.

**Rome V D2 criteria — must include all of the following:**

1. **Paroxysmal, stereotypical episodes of intense abdominal pain lasting 1 hour or more, up to several days**
2. The episodes are **separated by weeks or months**
3. The episodes occur **at least 3 times per year and 2 times in the previous 6 months**
4. The pain is **incapacitating and interferes with everyday activities**
5. The pain is associated with **2 or more** of:
   - History of migraine headaches
   - Family history of migraine headaches
   - Anorexia, nausea, or vomiting with episodes
   - Photophobia
   - Pallor
   - Aura or prodrome preceding the full episode
6. After appropriate evaluation, **no other medical condition can fully explain the symptoms**

**Timing:** criteria fulfilled for the **last 3 months**, with symptom onset at **6 months** before diagnosis.

**Clinical features beyond the criteria:**

| Feature | As described |
|---|---|
| Episode duration | **2 to 72 hours, averaging approximately 17 hours** |
| Pain quality | **Noncolicky**; dull; periumbilical, epigastric, midline or difficult to localize; **nonradiating** |
| Relation to meals/stool | **Not related to eating or defecation** |
| Migraine history | **70%** have a history of migraine headaches; relief with antimigraine therapy further supports the diagnosis |
| Prodrome / aura | Behaviour or mood change; flashing lights; photophobia; vasomotor pallor and flushing |
| Other symptoms | Anorexia and nausea common; **vomiting less severe than in CVS**; diarrhea may be present |
| Triggers | Psychological stress, lack of sleep, infection, possibly histamine/tyramine-containing foods |

- **Pediatric data** (no adult epidemiology existed before the Rome V Global Epidemiology and Validation Study): prevalence **1.5%–23%** depending on criteria; first-degree relative with migraine headache in **34%–90%**; personal history of migraine in **24%–57%**.

### Severity Assessment

- **No severity score or staging system exists.** The criteria themselves require that the pain be **incapacitating and interfere with everyday activities**, and episodes frequently drive emergency department presentation.

## Differential Diagnosis

*Workup: see [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction|disorders of gut–brain interaction (DGBI)]].*

- [[cyclic-vomiting-syndrome|Cyclic vomiting syndrome (CVS)]] — **the key distinction: in abdominal migraine the pain, not the vomiting, is dominant, and the vomiting is not repetitive.** Where episodic pain accompanies severe vomiting the two overlap, and **the predominant symptom directs the primary diagnosis and the treatment approach**.
- [[irritable-bowel-syndrome|Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)]] — abdominal migraine is **not associated with constipation or change in bowel function**, which is part of why the committee judged it a distinct entity.
- [[centrally-mediated-abdominal-pain-syndrome|Centrally mediated abdominal pain syndrome (CAPS)]] — continuous rather than paroxysmal; the two may overlap.
- [[inflammatory-bowel-disease|Inflammatory bowel disease]] and other chronic GI disease — **rarely follow this episodic, distinct pattern**; they are usually daily and ongoing, and **weeks to months of complete freedom from symptoms with a negative evaluation between episodes is uncommon in chronic GI disorders**.
- Migraine headache — co-occurs rather than competes.

> [!pearl] Episodic, stereotypical, and completely well in between
> All three episodic syndromes share pallor, aura, anorexia and a personal or family history of migraine. What makes the triad recognisable is the **pattern**: identical attacks, long symptom-free intervals, and a normal evaluation between episodes ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]).

## Diagnostics

- Diagnosis = **fulfilling the Rome V criteria plus an appropriate evaluation** to exclude other explanations. The **paroxysmal nature and stereotypical features usually point to the diagnosis, precluding expensive or invasive investigation**.
- **Screening laboratory investigations:** complete blood count, complete metabolic profile, C-reactive protein, **fecal calprotectin** — to evaluate for inflammatory conditions.
- **Red flags requiring further evaluation:** weight loss · abnormal screening laboratory studies · abnormal physical examination · blood in the stool · protracted vomiting.
- **Cross-sectional imaging** — ultrasound, computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging — is usually performed given the severity of episodes, and **is recommended if it has not been done**.

> [!pitfall] Do not keep re-imaging a stereotypical attack
> **Repeat evaluation is not recommended unless the clinical features change** ([[rome-v-2026-centrally-mediated-gi-pain]]). Each identical attack re-presenting to the emergency department is not a reason to repeat cross-sectional imaging.

## Therapeutics

Treatment is aimed at **aborting acute episodes and at prophylaxis to prevent future attacks** — the same two-track approach used for migraine headache and [[cyclic-vomiting-syndrome|CVS]]. **The chapter grades none of this**; the agents are those used for cephalic migraine, applied to abdominal migraine, and **no doses are given**.

### Aborting an Acute Episode

- **Triptans** — empirically show benefit in aborting an acute attack.
- **Intravenous hydration.**
- **Antiemetic medication** where there is nausea and vomiting.
- **Pain relief:** intravenous paracetamol, nefopam, **short-term opioids when needed**.
- **Histamine H1 receptor antagonists.**

### Prophylaxis

| Class | Example named |
|---|---|
| β-blockers | Propranolol |
| Calcium channel blockers | Flunarizine |
| Serotonin antagonists | Cyproheptadine, pizotifen |
| γ-aminobutyric acid agonists | Valproate |
| Neuromodulators | **TCA — desipramine or amitriptyline; SNRI — duloxetine** |

- **Pizotifen** is the one agent with a double-blind controlled trial — **in children with abdominal migraine**, showing significant improvement in pain severity and days of abdominal pain.
- **Brain–gut behavioral therapies including cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** are recommended as for any DGBI — detail on [[brain-gut-behavioral-therapy]]; neuromodulator dosing for painful DGBI is on [[centrally-mediated-abdominal-pain-syndrome]] and [[disorders-of-gut-brain-interaction]].
- Neuromodulators have been found effective for **migraine, abdominal migraine and CVS alike**, which is why the overlap matters therapeutically.
- Trials of newer antimigraine agents (lasmiditan, galcanezumab, erenumab, fremanezumab) in abdominal migraine are **expected but not yet available**.
