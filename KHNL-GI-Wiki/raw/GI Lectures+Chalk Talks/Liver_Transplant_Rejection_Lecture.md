## Summary

### Immune Response in Transplantation
- The adaptive immune response recognizes foreign peptides presented with MHC molecules, similar to responses against pathogens
- T-cells recognize foreign MHC molecules on donor cells (direct pathway) or donor MHC peptides presented by recipient cells (indirect pathway)
- Two signals are required for immune activation: foreign protein presented by MHC and co-stimulation amplified by danger signals
- MHC molecules are the primary targets because they represent the greatest genetic difference between donor and recipient tissues

### Acute Cellular Rejection (ACR)
- Occurs in 10-30% of liver transplant recipients, most commonly 5-30 days post-transplant
- Risk factors include:
  - Younger recipient age (stronger immune response)
  - History of autoimmune liver disease
  - Retransplantation
  - Significant ischemia-reperfusion injury
- Clinical presentation typically includes elevated liver enzymes (AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin)
- Lab abnormalities are necessary but not sufficient for diagnosis
- Definitive diagnosis requires liver biopsy
- Histological features include portal inflammation, bile duct damage, and endothelitis
- Treatment:
  - Standard approach: high-dose steroid pulse (3 grams)
  - For steroid-resistant rejection (10% of cases): thymoglobulin
- Unlike other organs, early ACR in liver transplants does not reduce long-term graft survival due to the liver's regenerative capacity

### Late-Onset Acute Cellular Rejection
- Occurs between 3-6 months post-transplant
- Often associated with weaning of immunosuppression or medication non-adherence
- More clinically significant than early ACR, associated with chronic rejection and reduced graft survival
- Requires vigilant monitoring throughout the first year post-transplant

### Chronic Rejection
- Results from ongoing, inadequately treated acute rejection
- Characterized by elevated bilirubin with potentially normal AST/ALT levels
- Pathophysiology involves damage to small blood vessels supplying bile ducts, leading to "vanishing bile duct syndrome"
- Occurs in 3-17% of liver transplant recipients
- Treatment involves increasing baseline immunosuppression (tacrolimus levels or adding mTOR inhibitors)
- Early chronic rejection may be reversible with treatment intensification
- Persistent graft dysfunction from chronic rejection is an indication for retransplantation

### Liver Biopsy Techniques for Diagnosis
- Percutaneous biopsy:
  - Performed under ultrasound guidance
  - Main risk is bleeding, particularly in patients with ascites
  - More technically challenging in partial grafts
- Transjugular biopsy:
  - Fluoroscopic-guided approach via jugular vein
  - Reduces risk of bleeding into peritoneum but not risk-free
  - Provides additional benefits: venogram and pressure measurements

### Antibody-Mediated Rejection (AMR)
- Less well understood than cellular rejection in liver transplantation
- Antibodies target donor MHC molecules on endothelial cells, causing vasculitis
- Diagnosis based on:
  - Presence of donor-specific antibodies (DSAs)
  - Evidence of endothelial injury
  - C4d deposition (less specific in liver than kidney transplants)
- DSAs can be detected using flow cytometry and single antigen bead assays
- Treatment approaches:
  - IVIG (intravenous immunoglobulin)
  - Plasma exchange to remove circulating antibodies
  - Rituximab to prevent formation of new antibodies (targets B cells)
