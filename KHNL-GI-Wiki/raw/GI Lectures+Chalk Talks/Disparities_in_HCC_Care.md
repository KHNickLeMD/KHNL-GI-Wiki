## Summary

### Research Framework and Approach
- Dr. Nephew's research journey moves through three phases: detecting disparities, understanding disparities, and intervening on disparities in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) care
- Shifted from a medical model of disease to a social ecological model that recognizes upstream factors like biased policies, neglected communities, and socioeconomic instability as key contributors to health outcomes
- The social ecological model considers interventions at policy, community, patient, and provider levels rather than focusing solely on biological factors

### Detection of Disparities in HCC Outcomes
- Since 2015, overall HCC incidence and mortality have decreased in the U.S., but this improvement has not been experienced equally by all populations - particularly non-Hispanic Black patients and American Indian/Alaska Native persons
- A major contributor to mortality disparities is lack of access to curative therapies (resection, transplant, and ablation)
- Previous SEER database analysis showed Black and Hispanic patients were less likely to receive curative therapies even after adjusting for race, gender, marital status, and income
- Indiana University study (2017) conducted ambitious chart review of over 1,000 patients over 10 years, controlling for MELD score, Milan criteria, liver disease etiology, and other clinical factors
- Results showed Black patients were still over 50% less likely to receive curative therapies compared to white patients, even after controlling for all clinical variables
- Among patients within Milan criteria, the primary difference was that Black patients were more likely to have alcohol and substance use documented as barriers to transplant

### Understanding Disparities: Policy Level
- Studied impact of Medicaid expansion in Indiana on hepatitis C screening and treatment across 18 hospitals
- Found significant increases in patients treated per month across policy periods, confirming that expansion increased access
- However, Medicaid patients were still 50% less likely to be treated than those with private insurance
- Key lesson: Policy change is necessary but not sufficient to eliminate healthcare disparities on its own

### Understanding Disparities: Neighborhood/Community Level
- Analyzed statewide cancer registry data to examine whether neighborhood-level social deprivation affects HCC outcomes
- Living in the most deprived neighborhoods was associated with mortality in HCC, independent of income, race, and insurance type
- The cumulative burden of adverse social determinants matters - patients with multiple adverse factors (uninsured/underinsured, never married, high deprivation neighborhoods) had worse survival

### Understanding Disparities: Patient and Provider Level
- Enrolled patients in interview/survey study using the Levesque conceptual framework to understand social determinants and social needs across five domains: ability to perceive, seek, reach, pay, and engage
- Black patients had significantly higher burden of adverse social determinants (average 5.4 vs 3.2 in white cohort)
- Approximately 70% of both Black and white patients had poor health literacy, measured by the brief three-question screen
- Significant differences by race in education level, ability to reach and pay for care, insurance status, and marital status
- About 25% of patients overall reported inadequate social support
- Cumulative burden of adverse social determinants was associated with reduced access to transplant
- Low health literacy was associated with 2.5 times higher mortality in HCC patients

### Key Lessons from Detection and Understanding Phases
- Health outcomes result from more than biology alone
- Health policy is important but not sufficient on its own
- Where you live matters, independent of income, race, and insurance
- Poor health literacy is critical for HCC given the complexity of navigating both cancer care and transplant care cascades
- High burden of adverse social determinants makes it difficult to navigate complex care systems
- These findings help identify highest-risk groups to target for interventions

### Intervention Development: Characteristics of Successful Interventions
- Interventions need to be multi-level - addressing multiple levels of the social ecological model simultaneously
- Should be culturally tailored to specific populations
- Team-based care and patient navigation have been proven effective across multiple disease states
- Important to work with families/caregivers and non-healthcare partners

### Human-Centered Design Methodology
- Used human-centered design research - a methodology that engages stakeholders and partners at every step of the design process
- Conducted three types of sessions: Explore, Co-design, and Test
- Explore sessions: Talked separately with Black patients and low-SES patients to understand barriers to care
- Co-design sessions: Brought barriers and patient-suggested solutions to providers (transplant hepatologists, surgeons, oncologists, social workers) to develop solutions
- Test sessions: Brought intervention prototypes back to patients for feedback
- Replicated process at both Indiana University and Johns Hopkins to ensure interventions would work across different settings
- Used journey mapping activity where patients placed emoji stickers along phases of care and described challenges
- Used group ideation activity asking patients to design solutions without constraints

### Key Findings from Human-Centered Design Sessions
- Both Black and low-SES patients experienced issues around information/education, lack of support, and side effects of treatment
- Unique to Black patients: Significant psychological and emotional concerns and distrust of healthcare system
- Distrust emerged when analyzing why patients made certain decisions, even when they expressed love for their doctors
- Providers wanted to create better triage pathways from community to transplant centers
- Both Indianapolis and Baltimore patients agreed on core needs: mental health support throughout, help understanding medical information, caregiver inclusion, peer support, and acknowledgment that everyone's journey is different

### Unexpected Finding: Mental Health Needs
- Mental health emerged as a critical need that was completely missing from the original intervention framework
- This finding fundamentally changed the intervention design

### The Liver Link Intervention
- Core components include: education, social support, milestone checks, social needs screening, and 90-day inpatient alerts
- Multimodal education based on patient preference for different learning styles
- Liver Link Library: Three 10-minute videos featuring patient stories from participants in explore sessions
- Liver Link Workbook: Interactive companion to videos with questions about which stories resonate, stage of cancer understanding, etc.
- Virtual support groups: 75-minute sessions twice monthly, alternating between Hopkins and IU social workers
- Support group topics: advocating for yourself with physicians, addressing past negative healthcare experiences, and tools for managing mental health
- Patients preferred support groups over individual peer navigators

### Parallel Projects Spawned by Qualitative Research
- Provider concerns about late referrals led to development of clinical decision support tool for community providers (separate R21 project)
- Created Roku Health Tracker - a shared decision-making tool to help patients and community providers discuss transplant referrals
- Demonstrates how qualitative research can be hypothesis-generating and lead to unexpected but valuable research directions

### Current Status and Future Directions
- Currently in pilot phase with 40 patients comparing intervention to usual care
- R01 grant pending scoring
- Planning to submit another R01 in June pending pilot results
- Intervention has been designed to be tested in other populations if successful

### Lessons for Conducting Disparities Research
- Full spectrum of research methods required: large database analysis, prospective studies, and qualitative methods to engage communities
- Requires multidisciplinary teams with expertise in literacy, qualitative methods, statistics, and other areas
- Policy change is necessary but access to care requires interventions at multiple levels
- Important to progress through all phases of disparities research: identifying/describing, understanding determinants, and intervening
- For human-centered design work, essential to work with experts in community-engaged research and qualitative analysis

### Provider vs Patient Perspectives Study
- Separate study interviewed community gastroenterology providers and patients about barriers to transplant referral
- Providers blamed patients: attributed lack of referral to continued drinking, poor social support, inability to get transportation
- Patients blamed communication gaps: didn't know disease was severe, didn't know transplant was an option, didn't understand why referral was important or timely
- Finding reveals disconnect between provider and patient perspectives and opens multiple areas for intervention
