## Summary

### Esophageal Biopsies for Suspected Eosinophilic Esophagitis
- Multiple biopsies should be taken throughout the esophagus (proximal, mid, distal) and placed in separate containers
- Focus on the presence of eosinophils rather than fixating on the exact count (>25)
- High eosinophil counts in just the distal esophagus could still represent severe reflux esophagitis

### H. pylori Gastritis Identification
- Classic histologic features: expansion of lamina propria by plasma cells
- Addition of active inflammation (neutrophils infiltrating gastric epithelium) makes H. pylori gastritis diagnosis 99.9% certain
- Immunohistochemical staining is preferred over special stains for detecting H. pylori organisms

### Intestinal Metaplasia and Autoimmune Atrophic Gastritis
- Biopsies from body of stomach in patients with autoimmune atrophic gastritis can look identical to antral biopsies due to "enteralization"
- Critical to separate stomach biopsies by location (antrum vs. body) in different containers
- Without separation, pathologists must use expensive immunohistochemical stains (gastrin, synaptophysin) to determine biopsy location
- Separating biopsies saves money compared to the cost of additional immunohistochemical staining

### Gastric Carcinoma Detection
- Diffuse-type gastric carcinoma is notoriously difficult to diagnose
- Providing clinical history is crucial, especially family history or history of lobular carcinoma of the breast
- CDH1 mutations (E-cadherin loss) are important to identify, particularly in Asian populations
- Family members should be informed about CDH1 mutations due to increased risk of lobular carcinoma in females

### Gastric Lymphoma
- MALT lymphoma is most common in stomach, often associated with H. pylori gastritis
- Eradication of H. pylori can cause MALT lymphoma to resolve unless it has undergone transformation
- Pathologists may recommend follow-up after H. pylori treatment to confirm resolution of atypical lymphoid aggregates

### Polyps and Dysplasia
- Fundic gland polyps:
  - Usually benign but can harbor dysplasia in patients with familial adenomatous polyposis (AFAP)
  - Communicate AFAP diagnosis to pathologists, especially if patient has numerous polyps (>10)
- Gastric hyperplastic polyps:
  - Can harbor intestinal metaplasia with dysplasia
  - Take deeper and multiple biopsies from different areas of these polyps
- Adenomas:
  - By definition represent low-grade dysplasia - "adenoma with low-grade dysplasia" is redundant terminology
  - In small bowel, terminology differs from colon (terms like tubular/villous not applicable)
- Hyperplastic polyps vs. sessile serrated lesions (SSL):
  - Specify suspicion of SSL on requisition form so pathologists can obtain deeper sections if needed
  - Hyperplastic polyps show changes primarily in upper third; SSLs show changes throughout the crypts

## Notes

### IBD Pathology
- Fundic gland metaplasia suggestive of IBD in the right clinical context
  - Deeper biopsies help find these
- Granuloma in only 10% of cases
- Severity:
  - Mild inflammation equals neutrophils in crypt
  - Moderate equals crypt abscesses
  - Severe equals ulcers
- Paneth cell metaplasia on left side suggests chronic colitis
  - Normal on right side
- Cannot tell if UC vs Crohn's disease
  - Pyloric gland metaplasia or granulomas suggestive of Crohn's disease but otherwise without these findings you can't tell
