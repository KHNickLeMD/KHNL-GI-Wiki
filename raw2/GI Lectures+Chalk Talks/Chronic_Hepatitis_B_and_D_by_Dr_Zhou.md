## Summary

### Epidemiology and Global Impact
- HCC is the third most common cause of cancer deaths globally, with Hep B as one of the biggest contributors
- US prevalence stable at 0.3% since 1999, primarily due to immigration from endemic countries
- Prevalence among Asians in the US is up to 3.1%
- Without treatment, up to 40% can develop cirrhosis, acute liver failure, or liver cancer, with 25% mortality
- Majority of infected patients are unaware and untreated - only 30% aware and 30% treated
- Among high-risk populations, awareness remains suboptimal at 30% for foreign-born and 50% for those with abnormal ALT

### Screening Guidelines
- USPSTF 2020 guidelines remain risk-based for persons from high-prevalence regions (Asia, Africa, parts of South America)
- CDC 2023 updated recommendations now support universal screening - all adults 18+ at least once in lifetime using triple panel test (surface antigen, core antibody total, surface antibody)
- Screen during each pregnancy regardless of vaccination status or testing history
- Expanded periodic risk-based testing for anyone with active risks

### Phases of HBV Infection
- **Immune tolerant phase**: E antigen positive, very high DNA (>10^7 IU/mL), normal ALT, typically under age 40
- **Immune active (chronic active hepatitis)**: E antigen positive with DNA >20,000 or E antigen negative with DNA >2,000, plus ALT >2x upper limit of normal
- **Inactive carrier**: E antigen negative, DNA <2,000, normal ALT
- **Indeterminate**: 42% have low DNA but high ALT (not 2x ULN), 5% have high DNA but normal ALT

### Updated Treatment Guidelines (2023)

**Immune tolerant patients:**
- Recommend treatment if over age 40
- Treat if significant liver inflammation or fibrosis on biopsy (grade 2+ or F2+)
- FibroScan stiffness >7 kPa (F2) is key indication for treatment
- Shared decision-making approach for all patients

**Indeterminate phase patients:**
- Consider treatment based on: fibrosis stage (F2+), age >40, male sex, low platelet count
- Systematic review showed 60% reduction in HCC incidence with treatment (0.38 incidence rate)
- Monitor every 6 months if not treating

**Inactive carriers:**
- Do not treat, but monitor for indications
- Assess for cirrhosis or advanced fibrosis

### First-Line Medications
- **Entecovir**: 0.5 mg dose, pregnancy class C
- **Tenofovir (TDF)**: 300 mg dose, pregnancy class B, risk of bone density loss and nephropathy
- **Tenofovir alafenamide (TAF)**: 25 mg dose, pregnancy class B, fewer side effects than TDF due to lower dose delivery
- All three can cause lactic acidosis (rare, primarily in decompensated patients)
- Monitor creatinine, phosphate annually for TDF; consider DEXA scan for bone loss risk

### Medication Selection Considerations
- Age and renal function (prefer TAF or Entecovir if renal dysfunction)
- Postmenopausal status (avoid TDF due to osteoporosis risk)
- Prior treatment history (if previous lamivudine, use tenofovir due to cross-resistance)
- Family planning (avoid Entecovir, use TDF or TAF)
- Cost and insurance coverage (Entecovir often more accessible)

### Treatment Duration
- Treat indefinitely until surface antigen loss achieved
- No longer recommend withdrawal after E antigen seroconversion (previous guideline removed)
- Surface antigen clearance required for stopping: loss of surface antigen, undetectable DNA, normal ALT for at least 1 year
- Spontaneous surface antigen clearance ~1% per year, higher in inactive carriers
- Treatment reduces HCC risk by 75% compared to no treatment, but surface antigen loss provides additional benefit

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy:**
- Treat if immune-active disease or DNA >200,000 (maternal transmission threshold)
- Start treatment at 28 weeks gestational age
- Use TDF or TAF (pregnancy class B, safe for breastfeeding)
- Discontinue at delivery, monitor for ALT flares every 3 months for 6 months
- TAF shown to be safe in recent studies from China

**Cirrhosis:**
- Treat all patients with compensated cirrhosis regardless of ALT
- Treat all decompensated patients indefinitely, monitor for lactic acidosis
- Early treatment can lead to recompensation - 34% delisted for transplant, 60% vs 46% 5-year transplant-free survival
- All HCC patients should be treated to prolong survival and reduce recurrence

**HIV co-infection:**
- Antiretroviral regimen must include TDF or TAF (TAF preferred in modern regimens)
- Entecovir alternate if tenofovir cannot be used, but risk of HIV resistance
- Must continue Hep B treatment if stopping antiretrovirals

**Hep C co-infection:**
- Must treat Hep B during Hep C DAA therapy due to reactivation risk
- Black box FDA warning after 24 cases (2 deaths) reported
- Start Hep B treatment regardless of infection phase if HBsAg positive

### HCC Surveillance Guidelines
- All patients with cirrhosis
- Men from endemic countries age >40, women age >50
- Persons from Africa at earlier age (median age 46 in US)
- Family history of HCC - start immediately
- Hep D co-infection without cirrhosis
- HIV co-infection: men >18, women >40
- Surface antigen loss: continue surveillance if cirrhosis or if lost after age 40 (men) or 50 (women)
- African immigrants show similar HCC risk to Asian cohorts (~1% at 5 years, 2.4% at 10 years)

### Hepatitis D Co-infection

**Screening:**
- Screen all Hep B positive patients, especially those from Mediterranean, Mongolia, Russia, Sub-Saharan Africa
- High-risk groups in US: injection drug users, MSM
- Screen if elevated ALT but low/undetectable HBV DNA
- If Hep D antibody positive, send Hep D RNA to confirm viremia

**Treatment:**
- Only FDA-approved treatment: peginterferon (normal ALT/negative RNA in ~25% of patients)
- Bulevirtide approved in EU 2020, but FDA denied US approval in 2022 (80% undetectable RNA in trials)
- Available through clinical trials or compassionate use in US
- Treat both active infections with peginterferon plus nucleos(t)ide or clinical trial

### Key Discussion Points from Q&A
- FibroScan frequency: Every 3 years recommended, annually doesn't show enough progression
- FibroScan especially important for patients with concurrent MASLD
- TAF preferred over TDF but cost/insurance barriers significant - county requires prior authorization demonstrating osteopenia
- Considering switching to Entecovir as default due to cost and fewer side effects than TDF
- Reactivation prophylaxis: threshold of 40 mg prednisone for 3 months
- Entecovir preferred for prophylaxis due to cost; TDF acceptable for short-term use
