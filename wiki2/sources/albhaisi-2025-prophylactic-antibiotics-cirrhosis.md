## Summary

This journal club, presented by Somaya Albhaisi, reviews a systematic review examining whether shorter or absent antibiotic prophylaxis is non-inferior to the standard 5–7 day course in cirrhotic patients with upper GI bleeding (UGB). Current guidelines universally recommend 5–7 days of prophylactic antibiotics (typically ceftriaxone or norfloxacin) to prevent bacterial infections that drive early mortality in this population.

The systematic review included 14 RCTs with 1,322 participants, the majority (90.9%) with variceal bleeding. The primary finding was that shorter/no prophylaxis carries a 97.3% probability of non-inferiority for all-cause mortality (risk difference only 0.9%), challenging the evidence base for prolonged courses. However, shorter durations were associated with a 15.2% higher bacterial infection rate, and the overall body of evidence was low quality (8 studies at high risk of bias; most non-blinded; conducted outside the US).

The journal club highlighted that post-2004 data showed stronger non-inferiority signals, consistent with improvements in endoscopic hemostasis and critical care reducing the benefit of extended prophylaxis. The discussion noted practical barriers to changing practice: high 6-week mortality (~15%) in variceal hemorrhage, difficulty obtaining consent in encephalopathic patients, and limited US-based data.

## Key Findings / Claims
- 14 RCTs, 1,322 participants; 90.9% variceal source
- All-cause mortality: 97.3% probability of non-inferiority for shorter/no antibiotics (risk difference 0.9%)
- Early re-bleeding: 73.8% probability of non-inferiority
- Bacterial infections: 15.2% higher rate with shorter durations
- 8 of 14 studies at high risk of bias; 12 of 14 lacked blinding
- Post-2004 studies showed stronger non-inferiority — modern endoscopy/ICU care may have reduced need for extended prophylaxis
- Most studies conducted in Asia and Europe; US applicability uncertain

## Contradictions / Open Questions
- Current ACG/AASLD guidelines recommend 5–7 days; this review challenges that duration without providing a definitive alternative
- The 15.2% higher infection rate with shorter courses is a meaningful signal that complicates "shorter is fine" messaging
- No high-quality RCTs with standardized infection definitions, blinding, and modern treatment context exist — the review exposes an evidence gap rather than closing it
