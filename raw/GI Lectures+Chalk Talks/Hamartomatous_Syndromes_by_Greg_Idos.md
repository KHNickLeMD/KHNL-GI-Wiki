## Key Elements of Family History Collection
- Minimum essential information: types of cancers, age at diagnosis, maternal vs paternal side, relevant ancestry (especially Ashkenazi Jewish), and prior genetic testing in the family
- Family history is particularly important for patients with unusual polyps, multiple polyps, or early onset cancers

## Threshold for Genetic Referral
- Refer patients with 2+ lifetime hamartomatous polyps for genetic evaluation
- Refer those with family history of hamartomatous polyps with pattern of associated cancers in first or second degree relatives
- Multi-gene panel testing will screen for all hamartomatous polyposis syndrome genes, adenomatous polyposis genes, and Lynch syndrome genes
- The threshold for referral is intentionally low

## Peutz-Jeghers Syndrome (PJS)

### Genetics and Pathology
- Autosomal dominant condition caused by STK11 mutation
- Defined by mucocutaneous pigmentation and characteristic hamartomatous polyps, especially in small bowel
- Polyps have hallmark arborizing, branching smooth muscle bands creating brain-like architecture

### Clinical Features
- 95% of patients have mucocutaneous pigmentation around perioral region, digits, fingertips, and genital areas
- Dark freckles around lips, orbits, or within mouth mucosa

### Childhood vs Adult Risks
- For children/adolescents, most immediate risk is small bowel complications, especially intussusception - not cancer
- Lifetime intussusception risk: 50-68% by age 18
- Adult concerns shift to cancer risk

### Genetic Evaluation Criteria
- 2+ histologically confirmed Peutz-Jeghers polyps
- Any number of PJ polyps with family history of PJS
- Mucocutaneous pigmentation with family history

### Cancer Risks
- Multi-organ cancer predisposition: breast, small bowel, colon, stomach, pancreas, ovarian, testicular, and lung
- Average age of diagnosis is much earlier than general population
- Requires multidisciplinary surveillance

### Surveillance Recommendations
- Colon/stomach: colonoscopy and upper endoscopy every 2-3 years
- Small bowel: video capsule endoscopy or MRI enterography every 2-3 years
- Pancreas: MRI/MRCP alternating with EUS annually, starting age 35
- Baseline endoscopic and small bowel evaluation begins at ages 8-10
- If initial baseline evaluations in childhood are normal, can halt surveillance and restart at age 18

## Juvenile Polyposis Syndrome (JPS)

### Genetics and Epidemiology
- Autosomal dominant, extremely rare (1 in 100,000-160,000)
- Caused by mutations in SMAD4 or BMPR1A genes

### Important Teaching Point
- Solitary juvenile polyps are NOT syndromic - common and benign
- Multiple juvenile polyps with distribution in different GI organs and family history raise concern for JPS

### High-Yield Genotype-Phenotype Correlation
- Massive gastric polyposis only occurs with SMAD4 mutations
- SMAD4 pathogenic variants should be evaluated for hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT)

### Genetic Evaluation Criteria
- 5+ juvenile polyps in colon/rectum
- 2+ juvenile polyps in other GI tract locations
- Any number of juvenile polyps with first-degree relative having juvenile polyps

### Cancer Risks and Surveillance
- Main risks: colon and stomach cancer
- Colonoscopy every 1-3 years starting age 12-15
- Upper endoscopy every 1-3 years starting age 12-15

## PTEN Hamartoma Tumor Syndrome (Cowden Syndrome)

### Genetics and Features
- Caused by germline PTEN mutation
- Major feature: macrocephaly (>58cm men, >57cm women)

### Polyp Characteristics
- Mixed polyposis: hamartomas, hyperplastic polyps, inflammatory polyps, ganglioneuromas, and adenomas

### Endoscopic Findings
- Glycogenic acanthosis in esophagus - very characteristic of Cowden syndrome
- Multiple slightly elevated, pale, whitish nodules
- Completely benign and asymptomatic

### Cancer Risks
- Significant risks: breast cancer, thyroid cancer, endometrial cancer, kidney cancer
- Colon cancer risk is lower compared to other organs

## GREM1-Related Polyposis
- Very rare autosomal dominant syndrome
- Characterized by attenuated colonic polyposis

## Distinguishing Between Syndromes
- Consider: number and histology of polyps, distribution, age of presentation, associated extra-colonic findings, and family history
- When seeing multiple hamartomatous polyps, unusual pathology, and suggestive family history, consider rare cancer syndrome
- Low threshold for referral - if unsure, refer to genetics
