## Summary

### Introduction to GERD
- Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD) is the most prevalent gastrointestinal disorder in the United States
- Characterized by the retrograde movement of gastric contents into the esophagus due to a breakdown of the antireflux barrier
- Complex pathophysiology involves the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) complex and diaphragm crura

### Treatment Approaches for GERD

**Medical Management:**
- Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are the mainstay of medical therapy
- Long-term PPI use raises concerns regarding side effects and potential complications

**Surgical and Endoscopic Options:**
- In refractory cases or when patients are concerned about long-term medical therapy, surgical options should be considered
- Recent advancements have made endoscopic management viable for many patients

### Endoscopic Techniques

**Transoral Incisionless Fundoplication (TIF 2.0):**
- Creates a 270-degree omega-shaped gastroesophageal flap valve (GEFV) to restore the antireflux barrier
- Has FDA approval and is supported by level 1 evidence
- Significant improvement in reflux symptoms; 100% esophagitis healing
- 82% of patients able to discontinue PPI therapy at 6 months
- Long-term results maintained up to 10 years

**Antireflux Mucosectomy (ARMS):**
- Involves mucosectomy at the esophagogastric junction to create a new flap valve through submucosal fibrosis
- Pooled success rates indicate 61.5% of patients could discontinue PPIs

**Antireflux Mucosal Ablation (ARMA):**
- Utilizes a dissection knife in spray coagulation mode for mucosal ablation along the gastric cardia
- Can be performed regardless of existing fibrosis from previous therapies

**Resection and Plication (RAP):**
- Involves semicircumferential mucosal resection followed by full-thickness plication at the gastric cardia
- Initial studies show 80% of patients discontinuing PPI use at 9 months

**Mucosal Ablation and Suturing of the GE Junction (MASE):**
- Involves mucosal ablation followed by suturing to create a new valve at the GEJ
- Shows promise in improving GERD symptoms and reducing PPI dependence

### Safety and Efficacy
- Reported complications include bleeding, perforation, and dysphagia, with rates varying between 6% to 16%
- Most complications are managed conservatively or endoscopically
- Current studies indicate limited follow-up periods, necessitating further research

### Conclusion
- Careful assessment of the hiatus grade and patient anatomy is crucial in determining the appropriate approach
- The evolution of techniques highlights the importance of tailoring treatments to individual patient needs
- Continued research will enhance efficacy and safety in managing GERD
