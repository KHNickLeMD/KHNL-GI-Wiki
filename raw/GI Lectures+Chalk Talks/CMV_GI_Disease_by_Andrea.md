## Summary

### Overview
- Tissue-invasive CMV disease most commonly affects the GI tract, comprising 30% of cases
- Colon is the most frequently involved site, affected in up to 94% of GI cases
- CMV presents with a wide range of non-specific GI symptoms including abdominal pain, upper and lower GI symptoms, GI bleeding, and dysphagia
- Approximately 10% of patients can present with acute pancreatitis
- Hepatic involvement can present with jaundice, elevated liver enzymes, and rarely fulminant hepatic failure

### Endoscopic Presentations
- CMV demonstrates diverse endoscopic findings, from mild mucosal inflammation or edema to severe ulcerations or pseudotumors that can mimic malignancy
- Most common endoscopic feature across all GI manifestations is ulceration
- Other findings include inflammation and polyploid masses, particularly in the small intestine and colon
- Distribution: most common in colon, followed by esophagus, stomach, and rarely small intestine

### Diagnosis
- Requires histopathologic evidence of CMV involvement in the affected organ
- CMV targets stromal and vascular endothelial cells in the GI tract
- Rarely seen in epithelial cells, which is why biopsies should target the ulcer base
- Cytopathic effects are diagnostic but only seen in 65% of cells with positive immunohistochemistry staining
- PCR can be used when cytopathic effects are not visible
- True CMV disease (vs. infection) requires assessment of patient's immune status and viral burden

### CMV in Inflammatory Bowel Disease
- High percentage of UC patients can develop CMV colitis, making it an important differential in IBD flares
- Should especially consider CMV in UC patients who are treatment-refractory or have failed multiple biologics
- Treatment with antiviral therapy reduces surgery rates in patients with histologic CMV and steroid-refractory disease

### CMV in Malignancy Patients
- Consider CMV in patients with active malignancies, particularly hematologic malignancies
- Higher risk in patients with low BMI and lymphopenia
- CMV can cause polyploid and tumor-like lesions

### Differential Diagnoses
- Pseudomembranous colitis from C. diff
- Other viral infections causing esophagitis in immunocompromised patients
- Graft versus host disease in stem cell transplant patients
- Various other conditions depending on GI tract location involved

### Treatment
- Mainstays are IV ganciclovir and oral valganciclovir with >80% treatment efficacy
- Important side effects include myelosuppression, nephrotoxicity, and electrolyte imbalances
- No demonstrated benefit in immunocompetent but critically ill patients regarding mortality or length of stay, likely due to comorbidities
- Treatment decisions in immunocompetent patients require risk-benefit discussion as there are no specific guidelines

### CMV in Immunocompetent Patients
- Prevalence is approximately 3 per 1,000 endoscopies in patients without IBD or overt immunosuppression
- Affects 3-4% of critically ill immunocompetent patients
- Most patients are elderly, critically ill, or have comorbidities like diabetes
- Compared to immunocompromised patients, immunocompetent patients present with:
  - More GI bleeding
  - Shorter symptom onset duration
  - Less extraintestinal involvement
  - More small intestinal disease
- Immunocompromised patients typically have more diffuse GI involvement and higher likelihood of esophageal disease

### Risk Factors for Tissue-Invasive CMV
- Older age
- Renal insufficiency
- Diabetes
- Critical illness
- IBD
- Malignancy
- Blood transfusion
- Steroid use

### Clinical Significance in Immunocompetent Patients
- CMV diagnosis can indicate undiagnosed immunodeficiency syndrome or occult malignancy
- Severe ulcers can form fistulas or abscesses
- IBD diagnosis can be concurrent with or follow CMV diagnosis
- Mortality rates vary widely, primarily due to patient comorbidities

### Patient Case Next Steps
- Monitor patient response to treatment
- Ensure patient is up to date on all malignancy screenings
- Review patient history for evidence of recurrent infections that might suggest undiagnosed immunodeficiency syndrome

### Discussion
- Colon remains most common site in both populations, but immunocompromised patients are more likely to have esophageal disease compared to immunocompetent patients
