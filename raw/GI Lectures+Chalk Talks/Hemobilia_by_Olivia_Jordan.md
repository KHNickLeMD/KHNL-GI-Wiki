## Summary

### Epidemiology and Etiology Trends
- Hemobilia has become a rare but important cause of upper GI bleeding
- Etiology has shifted significantly over time, with iatrogenic causes now representing 65% of cases as of 2001
- Vascular etiologies account for approximately 9% of cases, with neoplastic, trauma, and other causes making up the remainder
- The increase in iatrogenic causes is likely due to more frequent use of interventional procedures

### Iatrogenic Causes
- **Percutaneous procedures** include transhepatic cholangiography (PTC), biliary drainage (PTCD), and liver biopsies, with risk ranging from less than 0.05% to 3%
- **Endoscopic procedures**, particularly ERCP with sphincterotomy, carry a 0.3% to 2% risk of hemobilia
- Risk from ERCP depends on invasiveness of maneuvers including large stones, balloon dilation, or stricture plasticity
- Post-sphincterotomy bleeding involves the papillary sphincter site where the cut is made
- **Surgical causes** include liver transplant, cholecystectomy, and pancreaticoduodenectomy
- Bile can damage blood vessels, leading to delayed healing and pseudoaneurysm formation, which causes intermittent arterial bleeding into bile ducts

### Non-Iatrogenic Causes
- **Hepatobiliary malignancies** account for 7-10% of non-iatrogenic cases, including HCC and cholangiocarcinoma
- Hemobilia from malignancy can be subtle and difficult to diagnose, sometimes requiring repeat ERCP
- **Cancer treatments** including radiofrequency ablation for HCC, TACE, and sorafenib can cause hemobilia
- **Portal biliopathy** results from biliary obstruction associated with cavernous transformation of the portal vein due to portal vein thrombosis
- **Gallstones** can cause hemobilia through chronic inflammation leading to pressure necrosis and erosion into adjacent structures or duodenum (Bouveret's syndrome)
- **Infections** including intraductal parasitic infections (echinococcus, Chinese liver fluke)
- **Trauma** (blunt or penetrating) and rarely acalculous cholecystitis

### Clinical Presentation
- Presentation varies widely and can include hematemesis, melena, or hematochezia
- **Quincke's triad** consists of right upper quadrant pain, jaundice, and GI bleeding, occurring in only about 30% of cases
- Timing of symptoms can help identify underlying cause - ERCP-related hemobilia typically presents immediately or within a few days of biliary injury
- Patients often have abnormal liver enzymes and bilirubin
- Cholangitis is a common comorbid presentation

### Diagnostic Approach
- Diagnostic strategy varies based on clinical stability and severity of bleeding
- **CTA** is used for significant bleeding when patient is too unstable for endoscopy
- **Upper endoscopy** is diagnostic when blood is seen coming from the ampulla or biliary tract, and helps exclude other upper GI bleeding sources
- **ERCP** can be both diagnostic and therapeutic
- **EUS** can evaluate vascular abnormalities and blood clots in the biliary tree when findings are equivocal
- **Conventional angiography** is not first-line due to invasiveness and time requirements
- **MRCP** is less commonly used as it takes too long in actively bleeding patients

### Management Strategies
- Treatment mirrors standard GI bleed management with focus on achieving hemostasis and maintaining bile flow
- **Resuscitation** includes large-bore IVs and transfusion to maintain hemoglobin above 7-8 g/dL as appropriate
- **Conservative management** for minor bleeding may include repositioning catheters to tamponade different areas
- **Endoscopic interventions** for hemostasis include:
  - For distal biliary tract bleeding: epinephrine injection, bipolar cautery, hemoclips, balloon tamponade, and stent placement
  - For proximal bleeding: extraction balloon catheters, retrieval baskets, and stent placement
- **Biliary stenting** maintains biliary flow and provides tamponade effect on bile wall
- Fully covered self-expanding metal stents have better tamponade effect and patency compared to plastic stents
- **Embolization** is generally very successful and should be used as definitive management for:
  - Patients too unstable for endoscopy
  - Hemobilia originating above the hilum
  - Distal bleeding refractory to metal stent placement
- Should avoid embolization in patients with cirrhosis, portal hypertension, portal vein thrombosis as it may compromise collateral flow
- **Vascular stenting** is possible but less commonly used than embolization
- **Surgical management** is rarely used and reserved for refractory cases

### Prognosis and Follow-Up
- Prognosis is generally favorable with early diagnosis, worse with delayed recognition
- Outcomes depend on underlying cause and severity of bleeding
- Recurrence after successful embolization is generally low
- Higher recurrence risk with malignancy etiology or ongoing injury
- Patients require follow-up imaging to monitor for recurrence
